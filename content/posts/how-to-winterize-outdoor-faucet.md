To winterize an outdoor faucet, disconnect every garden hose, shut off the interior valve feeding the faucet, open the outdoor spigot to drain the line, drain the bleeder cap on the interior valve, and leave the outdoor handle open for winter — then slip on a foam faucet cover. The whole job takes about 10 minutes per faucet and prevents the most common freeze-burst flooding homeowners face: a split pipe inside the wall that you don't discover until spring.

## Why it bursts: ice expands inside your wall

Here's the physics that destroys plumbing every winter. Water expands by about 9% when it freezes, and it does so with enormous force — enough to split copper, crack brass, and rupture PEX fittings. An outdoor faucet (hose bib or sillcock) is a pipe that runs from inside your heated house, through the exterior wall, to the outside. The section inside the wall is the danger zone.

When water sits trapped in that pipe and temperatures drop, ice forms and expands. Crucially, the burst rarely happens where the ice is — it happens in the pressurized liquid water trapped between the ice blockage and the closed valve. That pressure spike splits the pipe, often inside the wall cavity where you can't see it. Then spring comes, the ice thaws, and water pours into your wall and flooring for hours before anyone notices. The average insurance claim for burst-pipe water damage runs $15,000 — all from skipping a 10-minute chore.

## Step zero: disconnect every hose (the critical step)

Before anything else, walk around the house and disconnect every garden hose, splitter, nozzle, and timer from every outdoor spigot. This is the single most important step, and it's the one people skip.

A hose left attached traps water in the faucet and the short pipe behind it. That trapped water can't drain, so it freezes — defeating even a frost-free sillcock's design. Detach them all, drain the hoses by stretching them downhill, and store them in the garage or shed. While you're at it, put away spray nozzles so they don't crack.

If you do nothing else on this list, do this. Most burst hose bibs each winter trace back to a forgotten hose.

## The interior shutoff and drain procedure

Most homes have a dedicated shutoff valve inside — in the basement, crawlspace, or utility room — on the water line feeding each outdoor faucet. Here's the full sequence for every exterior spigot:

1. **Find the interior shutoff.** Follow the pipe back from where the outdoor faucet penetrates the wall. You're looking for a small valve on that line — usually a round wheel handle (gate valve) or a lever (ball valve). If you have several outdoor faucets, each should have its own shutoff.
2. **Close the interior valve.** Turn a wheel handle clockwise until snug, or rotate a lever perpendicular to the pipe. Don't over-torque an old valve — work it gently back and forth if it's stiff.
3. **Go outside and open the faucet fully.** Turn the outdoor handle all the way on. You'll see a small amount of residual water drain out. Leave the handle open for the entire winter — if any moisture remains in the pipe and freezes, the open spigot gives expanding ice room to push outward instead of bursting the pipe.
4. **Drain the bleeder cap.** Go back inside to the shutoff valve and look for a small brass cap on the side of the valve body — that's the bleeder (drain) cap. Put a bucket or towel underneath, unscrew it, and let the short section of pipe between the valve and the outside wall drain completely. When it stops dripping, screw the cap back on snugly.
5. **Check for drips over the next day.** The outdoor spigot should stop dripping within a few hours once the line drains. If it keeps dripping steadily for a day, the interior valve isn't sealing fully and needs replacement — call a plumber before the freeze.

No interior shutoff? Some older homes don't have them. In that case, disconnecting hoses, draining what you can, and insulating well with a foam cover is your defense — and it's worth having a plumber add shutoff valves, which is a modest job that pays for itself in peace of mind.

## Frost-free vs. standard sillcocks: know which you have

Not all outdoor faucets are built the same, and the difference matters for how you winterize.

**Standard sillcocks** have the shutoff washer right at the outdoor handle — meaning water sits in the pipe all the way out to the exterior wall, fully exposed to freezing. These absolutely depend on the interior-shutoff-and-drain procedure above. If you have standard sillcocks in a cold climate, they're the highest-risk fixtures on your plumbing system.

**Frost-free (freeze-proof) sillcocks** are longer — the valve seat sits 8 to 14 inches inside the heated wall, so water drains out of the exterior section when you close the handle. They're much more forgiving, but not magic: they only work if the pipe slopes slightly downward toward the outside, and they fail if a hose is left connected, because trapped water can't drain out of the long stem.

How to tell which you have: frost-free sillcocks have a noticeably long barrel — the handle sits several inches out from the wall on a long tube. If you're replacing a standard sillcock, upgrading to frost-free is one of the highest-value plumbing upgrades you can make — the part costs $25–$50 and a plumber can usually swap one in under an hour.

## Foam covers: cheap insurance

After draining, slip an insulated foam faucet cover over each outdoor spigot. They cost $3–$8 each, install in seconds, and add a real layer of freeze protection by trapping ambient heat from the house wall. They're especially important for faucets without interior shutoffs and north-facing spigots. They are not a substitute for draining — a foam cover over a water-filled pipe just delays the freeze by a few hours.

## What to do if it already froze

If you wake up to a hard freeze and realize you forgot — don't panic, but act methodically:

1. **Check for a burst before thawing anything.** Look inside the house along the wall behind each outdoor faucet for damp drywall, bubbling paint, or water stains. Check the basement or crawlspace below for drips. If you see active leaking, shut off the main water valve to the house immediately and call a plumber.
2. **Thaw gently if there's no leak.** Apply gentle heat to the outdoor faucet and the accessible pipe — a hair dryer on low, or warm (not hot) towels wrapped around the pipe. Never use a torch or heat gun; concentrated heat can burst the pipe or start a fire inside the wall.
3. **Open the faucet while thawing.** As ice melts, water needs somewhere to go. An open spigot relieves pressure and lets you see the moment flow returns.
4. **Once thawed, winterize properly.** Then follow the full drain procedure above — late is infinitely better than never, because the next freeze is coming.
5. **Watch that spot for a week.** Hairline cracks don't always leak immediately. Check the interior wall and the area below the faucet daily for a week after a freeze event. A slow seep caught early is a $200 plumber visit; one discovered in spring is a $15,000 insurance claim.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Leaving a hose connected.** The number one cause of burst hose bibs. It defeats frost-free designs, traps water, and turns a 10-minute job into a disaster. Disconnect every hose, every fall, no exceptions.
- **Closing the outdoor faucet for winter after draining.** Leave it open. A closed spigot traps any residual moisture with nowhere to expand; an open one gives ice room to push out harmlessly.
- **Forgetting the bleeder cap.** Draining the outdoor spigot but skipping the little brass bleeder on the interior valve leaves a full section of pipe between the valve and the wall charged with water.
- **Relying on a foam cover alone.** Covers insulate; they don't drain. A water-filled pipe under a foam cover still freezes — just a few hours later.
- **Using a torch to thaw a frozen pipe.** Open flame near a wall cavity is how houses catch fire. Hair dryer or warm towels only.
- **Ignoring a slow drip from the outdoor spigot after shutoff.** A drip means the interior valve isn't sealing, which means the line is still pressurized — the exact condition that bursts pipes.

## Frequently asked questions

**When exactly should I winterize — what temperature?**
Don't wait for the forecast to say 32°F. Winterize when overnight lows start dipping into the upper 30s regularly, or when the first frost advisory hits your area. Pipes in exterior walls can freeze after just a few hours below freezing, especially on windy, north-facing walls. In most northern states, that's sometime in October — which is why this is a fall chore, not a winter one.

**Do I really need to do this if I have frost-free faucets?**
You still need to disconnect the hoses — that's non-negotiable even for frost-free. The interior shutoff and drain is less critical with a properly installed, properly sloped frost-free sillcock, but doing it anyway costs you five minutes and removes all doubt.

**Can I just let the faucet drip instead of winterizing?**
The "let it drip" trick works for interior pipes during extreme cold snaps because moving water resists freezing. It's a terrible strategy for outdoor faucets: you're paying for the water all winter, it doesn't reliably prevent freezing in the wall section, and it does nothing about the trapped-water problem behind a connected hose. Drain, don't drip.

**My outdoor faucet has no interior shutoff — what now?**
Disconnect hoses, open the spigot to drain what gravity allows, and use a foam cover. Then put "add interior shutoffs" on your plumber list — it's a straightforward job, usually $150–$250, and it transforms your highest-risk fixtures into non-issues.

**Should I blow out the line with compressed air?**
For a simple hose bib, gravity draining plus the bleeder cap is sufficient — air blowouts are for sprinkler systems with long lateral runs. If you're unsure the line fully drained, a quick blast from a compressor (under 50 PSI) through the open spigot doesn't hurt, but it's rarely necessary.

**What about the sprinkler system?**
That's a separate, bigger job — in-ground lines hold far more water and almost always need a compressed-air blowout, which most homeowners hire out ($75–$150). Don't confuse "I winterized the hose bibs" with "I winterized the irrigation." Do both.

## When to call a pro

- The interior shutoff valve is corroded, won't budge, or drips when you turn it — forcing it can snap the pipe and flood the area instantly.
- The outdoor spigot keeps dripping a day after you closed the interior valve — the shutoff isn't sealing and needs replacement before the freeze.
- You find water stains, soft drywall, or active dripping inside after a freeze — that's a burst or cracked pipe inside the wall.
- You want to upgrade standard sillcocks to frost-free, or add missing interior shutoffs — both are quick plumber jobs with outsized payoff.
- Your sprinkler system needs a blowout and you don't own a compressor — the $100 service call beats replacing split lateral lines in spring.
