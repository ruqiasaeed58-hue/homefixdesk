**Short answer: set it to 55°F — never lower.** That is the widely recommended minimum that keeps pipes from freezing while saving you real money on heating. Going lower risks burst pipes (a $5,000–$15,000 disaster), and turning the heat off entirely can even void parts of your homeowners insurance.

It feels wasteful to heat an empty house, and the instinct to crank it down to 50°F or off is understandable. But the math and the risk both point to 55°F as the floor. Below: why that number, how much you actually save, the exceptions for extreme cold and vulnerable plumbing, and the smart-thermostat tricks that protect your house while you are gone.

## Why 55°F Is the Magic Number

Fifty-five degrees is not arbitrary — it is the consensus minimum from energy agencies, HVAC professionals, and insurance companies, and each has its own reason.

**Pipes freeze well above 32°F outdoors.** The temperature that matters is inside your walls, where pipes run. Wall cavities on exterior walls can be 10–20 degrees colder than the room air, especially on windy nights or in poorly insulated spots. A 55°F room keeps those hidden cavities safely above freezing in most homes. Drop the room to 45°F and the margin vanishes.

**Your insurance may require it.** Many homeowners policies include a "reasonable care" clause, and insurers commonly cite 55°F as the minimum for maintaining coverage against burst-pipe claims. Turn the heat off, pipes burst, and you could be fighting a denied claim on top of the water damage.

**Furnaces need the margin.** Heating systems cycle; the thermostat reading is an average, not a guarantee for every corner. Drafty rooms, additions over crawl spaces, and far-flung bathrooms run colder than the hallway where the thermostat lives. The 55°F setting builds in a buffer for the coldest spots.

**Condensation and mold.** Very cold indoor surfaces in a heated-then-cooled house invite condensation, which feeds mold in wall cavities. Holding 55°F keeps surfaces warm enough to avoid most of it.

## How Much Do You Actually Save?

The savings are real and worth capturing — just not by going below 55°F.

**The DOE rule of thumb:** turning your thermostat back 7–10°F from its normal setting for 8 hours a day can save up to 10% a year on heating. A two-week vacation at 55°F (down from, say, 68°F) is a 13-degree setback running 24 hours a day — the savings over the trip are meaningful, often $30–$80 depending on your fuel, rates, and climate.

**Setback vs. constant:** the "it costs more to reheat" myth is just that — a myth. Heating an empty house to 68°F for two weeks always costs more than holding 55°F and reheating once. Thermal mass means the reheat takes a few hours, not days, and costs a fraction of what you saved.

**The real comparison is 55°F vs. off.** Turning the heat off entirely might save another $20–$40 over two weeks — against a burst-pipe risk that averages thousands. It is the worst trade in homeownership.

## The Exceptions: When 55°F Is Not Enough

Fifty-five is the floor for a typical home — but some houses need more.

**Extreme cold snaps.** If the forecast shows sustained temperatures below 0°F (or well below your area's normal winter lows), bump the setting to 60°F or even 65°F. The colder it is outside, the colder your wall cavities get, and the safety margin shrinks.

**Pipes on exterior walls.** Kitchens and bathrooms with plumbing in exterior walls are the classic freeze points. If you know your house has them — and many do — consider 60°F as your away setting, and open the cabinet doors under those sinks before you leave so warm room air reaches the pipes. Our full guide on [keeping pipes from freezing while you are away](/posts/keep-pipes-from-freezing-while-away/) covers the complete checklist.

**Crawl spaces and unheated basements.** Pipes in vented crawl spaces or uninsulated basements get almost no benefit from the thermostat. If your plumbing runs through these spaces, 55°F at the thermostat may not protect them — pipe insulation, heat tape, or a higher setting is needed.

**Homes with a history of freezing.** If a pipe has frozen before, it will freeze again under the same conditions. Previous freeze-ups mark your home's vulnerable spots — treat them as requiring extra margin.

**Heat pumps in very cold climates.** Heat pumps lose efficiency as temperatures drop and may run auxiliary heat to hold temperature. A smart thermostat's "away" handling matters here — see the smart thermostat section below.

## What About Turning the Heat Off Entirely?

Do not do it in winter. Ever. Here is the full case:

- **Burst pipes** are the headline risk — water damage from a single burst pipe routinely costs $5,000–$15,000, and a pipe that bursts on day two of a three-week trip runs water the whole time you are gone.
- **Insurance complications**, as noted above.
- **Appliances and systems** suffer: water heaters, dishwashers, washing machines, and even the furnace itself are not designed to sit in a freezing house.
- **Structural issues**: prolonged deep cold can crack plaster, damage wood floors, and kill houseplants — minor compared to flooding, but real.

The only scenario where "off" is acceptable is if you have fully winterized the plumbing — main water shut off, pipes drained, water heater drained or set to vacation mode, toilets and traps treated with antifreeze. That is the [vacation-home winterization](/posts/how-to-winterize-vacation-home/) playbook, a bigger project than a thermostat setting.

## Smart Thermostats: The Away-Game Advantage

If you travel in winter, a smart thermostat pays for itself in peace of mind alone.

**Geofencing and away modes.** Most smart thermostats detect when nobody is home and drop to your away setpoint automatically — set it to 55°F once and forget it.

**Remote monitoring.** The killer feature: check the indoor temperature from your phone anywhere. If the furnace fails while you are gone, you will see the temperature falling instead of discovering it when the pipes burst. Some models alert you automatically.

**Freeze alerts.** Many smart thermostats (and cheap standalone sensors) can notify you if indoor temperature drops below a threshold you set — 50°F is a good alert point, giving you time to act before pipes are at risk.

**Have a human backup.** Technology fails too. Ask a neighbor or friend to check the house every few days during a long trip — a quick walk-through catches furnace failures, leaks, and everything else a thermostat cannot see. Give them your HVAC company's number.

**Do not rely on "learning" alone.** If your thermostat learns your schedule, make sure it knows you are actually gone — manually engage vacation/away mode rather than trusting it to figure out an irregular trip.

## What If the Power Goes Out While You Are Away?

A thermostat setting cannot help if the furnace has no electricity. Winter power outages are the scenario that defeats every setpoint.

**Know your outage risk.** If your area loses power in winter storms, a 55°F setting buys you time — a well-insulated house takes many hours to fall from 55°F to freezing — but it is not a solution for a multi-day outage.

**A standby generator** ($8,000–$15,000 installed) is the full answer for outage-prone areas, automatically powering the furnace. A **portable generator** ($500–$1,500) can run a gas furnace's blower and controls through a transfer switch — but never backfeed through an outlet, and never run it indoors or in a garage.

**Remote temperature alerts are your early warning.** If the power fails, a smart thermostat with battery backup or a standalone cellular/Wi-Fi temperature sensor tells you the house is cooling — and your house-checker can confirm and act. Without monitoring, you find out when you get home.

**For long trips in storm season**, the belt-and-suspenders approach is: 55°F+ setting, water main shut off (so even a freeze cannot flood the house), and a human checking in. Shutting the main is the single highest-leverage move — a frozen pipe with no water pressure behind it cannot burst-flood your home.

## The Pre-Trip Checklist

Run through this before you lock the door:

1. **Set the thermostat to 55°F minimum** (60–65°F if extreme cold is forecast or you have vulnerable plumbing).
2. **Engage vacation/away mode** on a smart thermostat; confirm it on the app.
3. **Open cabinet doors** under sinks on exterior walls.
4. **Set the water heater to vacation mode** (or 120°F) — no point keeping 50 gallons at full temperature for nobody.
5. **Shut off water to the washing machine** and consider the main shutoff if you will be gone more than a week (see the [pipes-away guide](/posts/keep-pipes-from-freezing-while-away/)).
6. **Replace the furnace filter** if it is dirty — a struggling furnace on a long trip is how failures happen ([why your furnace keeps turning on and off](/posts/why-furnace-keeps-turning-on-and-off/) covers the warning signs).
7. **Arrange a house-checker** and leave emergency numbers.
8. **Do not close all interior doors** — let warm air circulate to every room with plumbing.

## What Does It Cost?

**The setting itself is free.** Holding 55°F instead of 68°F for two weeks typically saves $30–$80 on heating, depending on fuel type and climate — so the safe choice is also the cheaper one.

**A smart thermostat** runs $130–$250 (plus installation if you do not DIY), and the remote monitoring alone justifies it for winter travelers. **A burst pipe** costs $5,000–$15,000 in water damage — roughly a hundred times the "savings" of turning the heat off.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is 50°F OK for a winter vacation?**
No — 55°F is the recommended minimum. The extra 5 degrees is your safety margin for cold spots in walls, and many insurers use 55°F as the threshold for burst-pipe coverage. The few dollars saved are not worth the risk.

**Should I turn my heat off if I drain the pipes?**
If you fully winterize — main water off, pipes drained, water heater drained or on vacation mode, traps treated — then yes, the heat can go off safely. That is a full winterization project, not just a thermostat change. For a normal trip, keep the heat at 55°F.

**How long does it take to reheat the house when I return?**
Usually 2–4 hours for the air to reach comfort, longer for walls and floors to feel fully warm. Many smart thermostats let you start reheating remotely before you land — set it to begin a few hours before arrival.

**Will 55°F damage my furniture, piano, or electronics?**
No. 55°F is well within safe range for household contents. Problems start below freezing or with rapid freeze-thaw cycling, neither of which happens at 55°F.

**My thermostat is in a warm hallway — is 55°F enough for cold rooms?**
This is exactly why 55°F includes a margin — but if you have rooms that run much colder (over a garage, far additions), consider 60°F or address the cold spots with the cabinet-door trick and, for chronic cases, added insulation.

**Does a setback hurt my furnace?**
No. Furnaces are designed to cycle, and holding a lower temperature means fewer, steadier cycles. What hurts furnaces is a clogged filter or neglected maintenance — change the filter before a long trip.

## When to Call a Pro

Most of this is DIY, but call an HVAC technician if your **furnace is unreliable** (strange noises, frequent cycling, or a history of winter failures — get a pre-trip tune-up, typically $80–$150), you want a **smart thermostat installed** and are not comfortable with wiring ($150–$300 installed), your home has **chronically freezing pipes** despite reasonable settings (a plumber or insulator can reroute or insulate the vulnerable runs), or the **thermostat itself misbehaves** (wild temperature swings or rooms that never match the setting can mean a bad location or failing unit).

Set it to 55°F, open the cabinet doors, hand a neighbor a key, and enjoy the trip. The house will sip energy while you are gone and be ready to warm up the moment you head home — and your pipes will be exactly where you left them, intact and unfrozen.
