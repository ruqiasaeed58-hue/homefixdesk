A thin white frost on your heat pump's outdoor coil is completely normal — the unit melts it off automatically in a **defrost cycle**. But a thick ice shell that covers the fins, blocks the fan, or keeps building for days is not: it means a defrost, airflow, or refrigerant problem. Start with the 10-minute homeowner checks below (clear snow and debris, change the filter, check the drain path), and if ice keeps returning, call an HVAC technician — don't chip it or pour hot water on it.

## Normal frost vs. abnormal ice: what you're looking at

Knowing which one you have tells you whether to relax or act.

**Normal frost:** a thin, even layer of white crystals on the outdoor coil fins — like frost on a windshield. Powdery, with the coil's metal texture still visible underneath. The unit melts it off on its own, usually every 30–90 minutes of heating, with a brief puff of steam. Expected whenever it's cold and humid outside.

**Abnormal ice:** a solid, glassy shell filling the gaps between coil fins, thick icicles hanging from the fan cage or top, or a lump of ice at the base encasing the bottom of the unit. The coil texture is hidden, the fan looks caged in, and the ice doesn't leave after a defrost cycle — it grows day after day. That's a malfunction, and running the unit that way can burn out the compressor.

## How the defrost cycle works (and how long it should take)

A heat pump heats your house by pulling heat from outdoor air — even cold air. The outdoor coil runs colder than the outside temperature while it absorbs that heat, and airborne moisture freezes onto the cold fins. That's the frost. Then the **defrost cycle** kicks in: a timer or temperature sensor briefly reverses the refrigerant flow (like switching to air-conditioning mode for a few minutes). The coil heats up, the frost melts and drips away, and normal heating resumes.

**What a healthy defrost cycle looks like:**

- Triggers every **30 to 90 minutes** of heating, depending on conditions.
- Lasts **5 to 10 minutes**.
- You may see **steam rising** from the unit — just meltwater evaporating off the warm coil.
- The fan may stop while the coil heats, then restart; afterward the coil looks wet and dark, frost gone.

If you've never noticed this happening, go look — the cycle is easy to miss on milder days when it runs less often. Frost that appears and disappears on its own over a couple of hours is a system working correctly.

## Tools & materials

- Work gloves and a soft brush or broom
- A clean furnace filter (match the size on the old one)
- A flashlight
- A level (only if the unit looks tilted)
- The unit's model number, written down before you call a tech

## Step-by-step: homeowner checks before calling a tech

Work through these in order — many iced-up heat pumps are fixed by step 2 or 3.

### 1. Shut down and clear snow and debris

Switch the thermostat to **emergency heat** or just turn the system off. Clear all snow, leaves, and debris: **at least 18 inches of clearance on all sides**, **5 feet of open air above**. Snowbanks drifted against the coil are the most common cause of icing after a storm. Use a broom or gloved hand — never a shovel — and clear the top fan grille and the drain space under the unit.

### 2. Replace the indoor air filter

A clogged filter chokes airflow across the indoor coil, and the refrigerant cycle runs the outdoor coil colder than it should — more frost, faster. If the filter looks gray and dusty, replace it: a $3–$10 fix that takes two minutes.

### 3. Check the drain path at the base

Meltwater from defrost cycles has to go somewhere. If the unit sits in a low spot, on bare soil, or on a flat pad with nowhere to drain, water pools at the base, refreezes, and builds an ice block that climbs the coil. The unit should be **raised at least 4 inches off the ground** on a stand or risers, sitting level, with meltwater able to run away. Thick ice at the bottom while the upper coil is mostly clear = drainage is your prime suspect.

### 4. Keep gutters dripping away from the unit

If a downspout or roof edge drips directly onto the unit, extend it away or add a diverter — a $15 fix for many "mystery icing" cases.

### 5. Give it one defrost cycle — then watch

After clearing everything, switch back to normal heat and watch for one full cycle. Frost forms, then within about 90 minutes the defrost should kick in (steam, fan pause) and the coil come out clean. If ice keeps accumulating past a couple of cycles, the problem is internal — stop DIY and call a pro (see below).

## The "never" list: things that will damage your unit

- **Never chip ice off the coil with a screwdriver, hammer, or any tool.** The refrigerant tubing in the fins is thin aluminum — one puncture and you've turned a $200 service call into a $1,000+ refrigerant repair.
- **Never pour hot water on it.** Thermal shock can crack components, and the runoff just refreezes at the base, making the problem worse.
- **Never run the unit with the fan caged in ice.** A blocked fan strains the motor and can overheat the compressor — the most expensive part in the system.
- **Never open the electrical panel or touch the refrigerant lines.** Capacitor shocks and refrigerant exposure are technician territory.

## Emergency heat: when to switch over

Your thermostat has an **emergency heat (EM HEAT)** setting. This bypasses the heat pump and runs the backup heat source — usually electric resistance strips or a gas furnace — so you stay warm while the heat pump is iced over or waiting for a technician.

**Switch to emergency heat when:** the outdoor unit is encased in ice and the fan can't turn, the house temperature keeps dropping despite the heat pump running, or you've tried the homeowner checks and ice keeps coming back. Note that electric backup heat costs roughly **2 to 3 times more per hour** than the heat pump — a bridge, not a season-long solution. Run it until the technician arrives or the ice clears, then switch back.

## Causes that need a technician

If the basics check out and ice returns, one of these is likely:

- **Failed defrost control board or sensor.** The most common mechanical cause: the sensor or timer that triggers the defrost cycle fails, so the cycle never runs and frost builds into ice. A tech tests the sensor and control board; replacing the board or sensor typically runs $200–$450.
- **Low refrigerant.** A slow leak makes the coil run too cold, frosting aggressively and overwhelming the defrost cycle. Low refrigerant is always a symptom of a leak — the tech finds and fixes the leak, then recharges. Expect $300–$900+ depending on the leak's location and refrigerant type.
- **Bad reversing valve.** The valve that flips refrigerant flow for the defrost cycle can stick, so the unit can't defrost at all. Replacement runs $400–$900.
- **Failing outdoor fan motor.** If the fan doesn't run during heating, airflow stalls and the coil ices over. $250–$600 for motor replacement.
- **Oversized or misplaced unit.** A unit installed too close to a wall or in a corner that never sees sun can ice chronically — an HVAC assessment can confirm whether placement is the root cause.

## Frequently asked questions

**My heat pump has ice on it but the house is still warm. Is that okay?**
Thin frost with the house staying warm is normal — watch one full cycle and confirm it defrosts itself. Thick ice that keeps growing even though you're comfortable is not fine; the unit is working harder than it should and the defrost control, airflow, or drainage needs attention before the compressor suffers.

**Why is steam coming off my heat pump in winter?**
That's the defrost cycle: the coil briefly heats to melt frost, and the meltwater evaporates as steam. Dramatic-looking but normal — as long as it lasts only a few minutes and the ice disappears.

**Should I cover my heat pump in winter?**
No — a cover blocks airflow and traps moisture, causing the icing you're trying to prevent. Only exception: a top-only panel over the fan grille in heavy snow regions, removed the rest of the time.

**Can I use a space heater to melt the ice off?**
No — it's a fire risk next to electrical components, and the melted water refreezes. Fix the cause, don't attack the symptom.

**Does ice on a heat pump raise my energy bill?**
Yes. An iced coil transfers heat poorly, so the unit runs longer cycles, defrosts more often, or falls back on expensive backup heat — a noticeable bump to winter heating costs.

**My unit is iced up during a big snowstorm — is it broken?**
Not necessarily. Heavy wet snow can overwhelm even a healthy system. Clear the snow, open the drain path, and give it a few cycles. If it recovers and stays clear, it was the storm.

## Prevention tips

- Keep the 18-inch clearance zone around the unit free of plants, leaves, and stored items year-round.
- Change the indoor filter every 1–3 months during heating season.
- Before the first freeze, verify the unit is level and raised off the ground with meltwater able to drain away.
- Redirect any gutter drips or roof runoff that fall on the unit.
- Learn what a healthy defrost cycle looks like early in the season — a failing one is obvious later.

## When to call a pro

Call an HVAC technician if: ice returns after the homeowner checks, the coil is encased in a solid shell or the fan is blocked, the unit never enters a defrost cycle, you hear grinding or the fan won't start, or the system keeps switching to backup heat on its own. Any time you see **sparking, smell burning electrical odor, or find a tripped breaker you keep having to reset** — shut the system off at the breaker and call. Electrical faults are not DIY territory.
