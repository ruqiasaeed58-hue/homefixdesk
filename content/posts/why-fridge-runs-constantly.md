A refrigerator should cycle on and off throughout the day. If yours runs constantly — humming away 24/7 — it is struggling to maintain temperature. That means higher electricity bills, faster wear on the compressor, and food that may not be as cold as it should be. The causes are usually simple and fixable without a technician.

## How a refrigerator cools (and why it cycles)

A fridge does not make cold — it moves heat. The cycle:

1. The **compressor** pressurizes refrigerant gas, making it hot.
2. Hot refrigerant flows through the **condenser coils**, releasing heat into your kitchen (that is why the back/sides feel warm).
3. The cooled refrigerant expands through a valve into the **evaporator coils** inside, getting very cold.
4. The cold evaporator absorbs heat from inside the fridge; a fan circulates the cold air.
5. When the thermostat reads the target temperature, it shuts off the compressor. The fridge coasts until the temperature drifts up, then cycles on again.

Constant running means step 2 or 5 is failing: either heat is not being released efficiently (dirty coils), heat keeps leaking in (bad seals), or the thermostat never gets satisfied (wrong setting, failing sensor, low refrigerant). The steps below follow this logic.

## Quick diagnosis: is it actually a problem?

- **Runs constantly but food is cold** → inefficient, but not urgent. Dirty coils or bad seals are making it work overtime. See Steps 1–2.
- **Runs constantly and food is NOT cold enough** → more serious: failing thermostat, low refrigerant, or dying compressor. See Steps 3–4 and "when to call a pro."
- **Runs constantly in a heat wave or after a big grocery trip** → normal. A fridge works harder in hot kitchens and after loading warm food. Give it 24 hours.
- **Runs constantly plus frost buildup in the freezer** → defrost system failure. See Step 4.

## What you will need

- Vacuum with brush attachment (or a coil cleaning brush, $10)
- Warm soapy water and cloths
- Dollar bill (for the seal test)
- Refrigerator thermometer ($5–$10 — worth owning)

## Step 1: Clean the condenser coils

Dirty coils are the #1 cause of a constantly-running fridge. The coils release heat; when they are caked in dust and pet hair, the fridge cannot shed heat efficiently and the compressor runs nonstop to compensate.

1. **Unplug the fridge** (or turn off the breaker).
2. **Find the coils:** on older fridges they are the black grid on the back; on newer ones they are underneath, behind a front kick-plate grille.
3. **Vacuum thoroughly** with the brush attachment. For stubborn buildup, use a coil brush to loosen dust, then vacuum again.
4. **Plug back in** and listen over the next few hours — many fridges start cycling normally again within a day.

Do this every 6–12 months. Homes with pets need it more often.

## Step 2: Test and fix the door seals

Warm air leaking in through bad gaskets forces the compressor to run constantly.

1. **The dollar-bill test:** close a dollar bill in the door and try to pull it out. It should resist firmly all the way around. If it slides out easily anywhere, the seal is weak there.
2. **Inspect the gaskets** for cracks, tears, warping, or mold. Clean them with warm soapy water — sticky residue prevents a full seal.
3. **Check door alignment.** If the door sags or does not close squarely, adjust the leveling feet (front feet usually adjust with a wrench) until the door seals evenly.
4. **Replace damaged gaskets.** Most press or screw into a channel — a $50–$100 part and a very doable DIY job. Match the exact model number.

Also check: is anything inside blocking the door from closing fully? An overstuffed freezer or a misaligned shelf is a surprisingly common cause.

## Step 3: Check the temperature settings

1. **Verify actual temperatures** with a fridge thermometer: the fridge compartment should be 37–40°F (3–4°C), the freezer 0°F (-18°C).
2. If the settings are much colder than needed (some people crank it to max), the fridge runs constantly trying to reach an unreachable target. Set it correctly and wait 24 hours.
3. **Check the thermostat sensor.** If the actual temperature is fine but the fridge still runs nonstop, the thermostat may be misreading — a $20–$40 part, moderately easy to replace.

## Step 4: Check for frost and airflow problems

1. **Look for heavy frost** in the freezer. A little frost is normal; thick ice on the back wall means the auto-defrost system failed (defrost heater, timer, or thermostat). The ice blocks airflow and the fridge runs constantly trying to cool.
2. **Check the vents** between freezer and fridge — bags of frozen food often block them. Cold air must circulate freely.
3. **Listen to the evaporator fan.** Open the freezer — you should hear the fan running. No fan sound plus warm fridge = failed evaporator fan motor ($30–$60 part).

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Never cleaning the coils.** It is the single most neglected fridge maintenance task and the most common cause of constant running.
- **Setting the temperature to max "to make it colder faster."** Fridges do not work that way — it just makes the compressor run nonstop.
- **Blocking the vents.** Overpacking the freezer or fridge blocks the airflow the whole system depends on.
- **Ignoring the door seal.** A $70 gasket can save hundreds in electricity and compressor wear.
- **Putting hot food directly in the fridge.** Let it cool to room temperature first (within 2 hours for food safety) to avoid marathon compressor runs.

## Frequently asked questions

**How often should a refrigerator cycle on and off?**
Roughly 30–50% duty cycle is normal — running about half the time, varying with ambient temperature and usage. Running 90–100% of the time is not normal.

**Will a constantly running fridge break sooner?**
Yes. Compressor wear is roughly proportional to run time, and the extra electricity adds up — a struggling fridge can use 2–3x its normal power.

**My fridge is new and runs a lot — is that normal?**
New fridges run more during the first 24 hours (reaching temperature) and in hot weather. If it never cycles off after 48 hours in a normal kitchen, check the seals and settings.

**Does the ice maker make it run more?**
Slightly — ice production adds load. But it should not cause constant running on its own.

**How much extra electricity does a struggling fridge use?**
A healthy modern fridge uses 100–200 kWh per month. One with dirty coils and bad seals can use 300–500 kWh — at average US rates, that is an extra $20–$40 per month, or $240–$480 per year. Coil cleaning pays for itself in weeks.

**Should I pull the fridge away from the wall?**
Yes — leave 1–2 inches of clearance behind and on the sides for airflow over the condenser coils. Fridges pushed tight into a cabinet alcove trap their own waste heat and run longer. Check your manual for the exact clearance spec.

## Energy-saving habits

- **Keep it reasonably full.** A full fridge holds temperature better than an empty one (thermal mass). But do not pack it so tight that air cannot circulate.
- **Cool hot food first.** Let cooked food reach room temperature (within 2 hours for safety) before refrigerating.
- **Minimize door-open time.** Know what you want before you open the door — every long open-door session is a compressor marathon.
- **Check the kitchen temperature.** A fridge in a 90°F garage or next to an oven works dramatically harder than one in a 70°F kitchen.

## When to call a pro

- Food is not staying cold despite clean coils and good seals — possible refrigerant leak or failing compressor. Refrigerant work requires EPA certification; this is not DIY.
- The compressor is extremely hot, clicking, or tripping the breaker — electrical/compressor failure.
- Heavy frost keeps returning after manual defrosting — the defrost system needs professional diagnosis.
- The fridge is over 12–15 years old and needs a major repair — replacement ($800–$2,000) usually beats a $400+ repair on an inefficient old unit.
