To winterize a sprinkler system, shut off the water supply to the irrigation line, turn the controller to **off or rain mode**, drain every zone using one of three methods (manual drain, automatic drain, or a compressor blowout), and protect the backflow preventer. Do it **before the first hard freeze** — once water trapped in those shallow lines freezes, it expands about 9% and cracks pipes, fittings, and sprinkler heads.

## Why sprinklers burst: the shallow-line problem

Sprinkler lines sit only **6–12 inches below the surface** — well above the frost line in most northern states. Any water left in them freezes, expands, and finds the weakest point: a PVC fitting, a thin poly line, a sprinkler head, or the backflow preventer. Spring repairs from freeze damage typically run **$200–$500+** — heads at $10–$30 each, a cracked backflow preventer at $150–$350 installed, plus line repairs if the damage is underground. A half-hour of fall maintenance avoids all of it.

## Tools & materials you'll need

- Your system's manual (to find the shutoff valve, drain valves, and zone layout)
- Safety glasses (non-negotiable for blowouts)
- For the **manual drain method:** just the valve key or handle — nothing else
- For the **compressor blowout:** an air compressor (minimum ~20+ gallons tank, or a smaller one cycled carefully), an adapter fitting to connect to the blowout port or backflow preventer, and a pressure regulator — **never exceed 50 PSI for poly pipe or 80 PSI for PVC**
- An insulated cover or pouch for the backflow preventer ($15–$30)
- A bucket or towel for the drained water

Total DIY cost: $0–$40 if you already own a compressor; pro blowout service: $60–$120.

## Step 1: Shut off the irrigation water supply

1. Find the **main irrigation shutoff valve** — usually in a valve box near the house, in the basement, or near the water meter. It's separate from your home's main shutoff.
2. Turn it **fully off**. Ball valves: handle perpendicular to the pipe. Gate valves: clockwise until seated.

## Step 2: Set the controller to off or rain mode

Switch the irrigation controller to **OFF or RAIN mode**. This stops all scheduled watering — the #1 winter sprinkler embarrassment is a system running during a freeze because someone forgot the controller. Leave the controller powered on through winter (OFF mode keeps your programs saved). If you unplug it, photograph the zone settings first.

## Step 3: Drain the system — pick your method

There are **three ways** to get water out. Your system was likely designed for one of them — check the manual or look at what's installed.

### Method A: Manual drain valves

Best for: systems with drain valves at low points.

1. Open each **manual drain valve** fully and let the water flow out.
2. Open a sprinkler head or two to let air in and help it drain completely, then close the valves after draining.

### Method B: Automatic drain valves

Best for: systems with auto-drain valves at low points (common with poly pipe).

1. These valves open on their own when line pressure drops — **shutting off the supply is the whole job**.
2. Run each zone for a minute or two from the controller to drop the pressure, which triggers the auto-drains.
3. Walk the yard: you should see water weeping out at the low-point valve locations. Auto-drains can clog with sediment — a low spot that doesn't drain means a valve to clean or replace ($5–$10).

### Method C: Compressor blowout (the thorough method)

Best for: any system, and the **only reliable method in deep-freeze climates**. This is what most pros do.

1. Put on **safety glasses** — compressed air can blow dirt, insects, and water out of heads at painful velocity.
2. Connect the compressor to the **blowout port** (usually a threaded fitting near the backflow preventer). Tighten it properly — a loose fitting under pressure whips around.
3. Set the regulator: **50 PSI max for poly pipe, 80 PSI max for PVC**. Exceeding it can crack fittings or blow heads off the line.
4. Open **one zone at a time** from the controller. **Never blow air into a closed system** — with no open heads, pressure spikes instantly and something breaks.
5. Let air run until heads spray a **fine mist**, about 2 minutes per zone max. Running a compressor against a dry zone overheats the air and can warp plastic components.
6. Repeat for all zones, then do a **second pass** — water settles back into low spots.
7. Turn off the compressor and release hose pressure before disconnecting.

**Blowout safety — take this seriously:**

- **Eye protection, always.** High-pressure air plus debris is a real injury risk.
- **Never exceed the PSI limits** (50 poly / 80 PVC). A typical shop compressor can push 120+ PSI — the regulator is mandatory, not optional.
- **Never blow into a closed system.** A zone valve must be open before air flows.
- **Stay clear of heads** while they're blowing — the sudden burst of pressure can be violent on the first zone.

## Step 4: Protect the backflow preventer

The **backflow preventer** is the brass assembly where the irrigation line leaves the house — it stops irrigation water from siphoning into your drinking water. It's the most expensive single component to replace, so it gets special treatment:

1. **Drain the test cocks.** The small capped ports hold water — open them, let them drain, then close.
2. **Set the ball valves to 45°.** Half open: fully closed traps water, fully open exposes seals. The 45° position lets remaining water drain and relieves pressure.
3. **Install an insulated cover** ($15–$30) — a purpose-made pouch that stays on all winter. In mild climates this plus draining may be all you need.

## Step 5: Final checks and spring readiness

- **Disconnect and drain any above-ground hoses or quick-connects** tied into the system.
- **Mark any spots** where you noticed weak heads or soggy areas during the blowout — that's your spring repair list.
- **Store the controller manual** with your zone map where you'll find it in April.
- **Don't touch the system again until spring.** No "just a quick test" in January — reintroducing water to a winterized system is how pipes burst in February.

## Safety warnings

- **Eye protection during blowouts.** Compressed air at 50+ PSI turns dirt and debris into projectiles.
- **Respect the PSI limits** — 50 PSI for poly, 80 PSI for PVC. A regulator on the compressor is mandatory.
- **Never pressurize a closed system.** Confirm a zone is open before air flows.
- **Don't use a tiny compressor on a huge system.** Undersized tanks cycle constantly; the air gets hot and can damage plastic. Rent a bigger one ($40–$60/day) if needed.
- **Backflow preventers are code-required** in most areas — don't remove one to "simplify" winterizing.

## Frequently asked questions

**When should I winterize my sprinkler system?**
Before the **first hard freeze** — when overnight lows drop to the mid-20s °F and stay there. In northern states that's typically October; in the South, November or December. Your local extension service or a lawn-care company can confirm the usual date for your area. Too early just means a brown lawn; too late means cracked pipes.

**Can I just skip winterizing in a mild climate?**
If your area **never drops below freezing** (deep South Florida, coastal Southern California), you may not need to — but anywhere that sees even occasional hard freezes, winterize. One surprise freeze is all it takes. The backflow preventer cover alone is cheap insurance in borderline zones.

**Is a compressor blowout really necessary, or is draining enough?**
In **mild-winter areas**, manual or auto-drain valves are usually enough. In areas with **sustained deep freezes**, water in low spots, dips, and heads will freeze — blowouts are the only method that clears those.

**My compressor is small (6-gallon pancake). Will it work?**
It can, with patience: run shorter zones, let the tank recharge between passes, and keep the pressure regulated. For large systems (6+ zones, long runs), rent a 20+ gallon unit for the day ($40–$60) — faster, and the air stays cooler.

**What does "rain mode" do on the controller?**
It suspends all watering schedules while keeping your programs saved in memory. The system won't run, but you won't reprogram everything in spring.

**Do I need to winterize if I have a drip irrigation system?**
Yes — drip lines and emitters hold water too, and the thin tubing freezes fast. Drain or blow out drip zones the same way, at low pressure (drip lines are fragile — stay well under the 50 PSI poly limit), and remove and store above-ground emitters and filters indoors.

## Prevention tips for every fall

- **Schedule it.** Put a recurring October reminder on your calendar — winterizing is one of those jobs everyone remembers the day *after* the first freeze.
- **Fix the small stuff before winterizing.** Replace cracked heads and leaking fittings in fall so spring startup is one step, not a project.
- **Photograph your zone layout** and controller settings before shutting down. Spring-you will be grateful.
- **Inspect the backflow cover yearly.** A torn or missing pouch is a $20 fix that prevents a $250 replacement.

## When to call a pro

Hire a sprinkler pro ($60–$120 for a blowout) if: you don't own or can't borrow a compressor, your system has **no drain valves and no blowout port** (a pro has the fittings and know-how), you're unsure where the shutoff is, the backflow preventer is leaking or damaged, or the system is large and complex (10+ zones, pumps, or a well supply). Also call one if spring startup reveals cracked heads, a leaking preventer, or a zone that won't pressurize — that's freeze damage, and diagnosing underground breaks is pro territory. A one-time pro blowout is cheap; learning your system's layout by watching them do it is a bonus that pays off next fall.
