The short answer: **shut off the main water supply, drain every pipe and fixture, pour non-toxic antifreeze into drains and toilets, set the thermostat no lower than 55°F, unplug non-essential appliances, and secure the exterior.** Do it as one thorough walkthrough on the day you leave, and you eliminate the two disasters that ruin vacant winter homes — burst pipes and unnoticed heating failures.

A vacation home sitting empty for months is uniquely vulnerable: in your primary home a leaking pipe gets noticed within hours, but in a house nobody visits until spring, that same leak runs unchecked until a neighbor spots water under the garage door. Burst-pipe claims in unoccupied homes routinely reach tens of thousands of dollars, and some insurers reduce coverage when a home sits unheated or unchecked. Nearly all of it is preventable with one deliberate day of preparation.

## Plumbing: Shut It Down and Drain It

Plumbing deserves the biggest slice of your time. Water expands as it freezes, and a frozen pipe can split copper, PEX, or PVC — in an empty house, one split pipe thawing in February can dump hundreds of gallons into walls and floors before anyone notices.

**Shut off the main water supply.** Find the main shutoff — usually where the water line enters the house: basement, crawlspace, utility room, or near the meter — and close it fully. On a well, also flip the pump's breaker. Then open the lowest faucet (often a basement laundry tub) and the highest one to let air in, and let gravity drain the lines. Work through every fixture, flushing toilets and holding handles open so tanks and bowls empty.

**Drain the water heater — or set it to vacation mode.** For a months-long vacancy, draining the tank is safest. Turn the heater off first (breaker for electric, gas valve to pilot/off for gas), let the water cool, then hook a garden hose to the drain valve, run it to a floor drain or outside, and open a hot-water faucet upstairs so air enters and the tank drains. For a few weeks away, the lowest setting or vacation mode is fine. The full drain procedure is covered in our guide on how to drain a water heater.

**Blow out the lines in deep-freeze climates.** Gravity draining leaves water in low spots and elbows. The fix is blowing lines out with compressed air through a hose bib or washing-machine connection — short bursts at 30–40 psi until only mist exits each open faucet. A half-day compressor rental runs about $40–75, or hand this to a plumber — it typically takes under an hour.

**Add non-toxic antifreeze to every trap and toilet.** Traps hold standing water to block sewer gases, and toilet bowls hold water too — neither empties by gravity. Pour a cup or two of RV antifreeze (propylene glycol, the pink non-toxic kind safe for plumbing) into each drain, and bail or sponge toilet bowls before adding antifreeze to bowl and tank. Never use automotive antifreeze — ethylene glycol is highly toxic.

**Shut off and drain outdoor water.** Close the interior shutoff valves for every hose bib, disconnect all hoses, then open the outside faucets to drain the line between valve and exterior. Leave the outdoor faucets open so residual water can expand. If interior valves have small drain caps, open them, let them drip out, and close them again.

## Heating: Keep It Above 55°F

Even with water off and pipes drained, keep the heat running low all winter. Some pipes never drain fully, and sustained cold damages the house itself — drywall tape cracks, wood floors contract, humidity swings invite mold.

Set the thermostat to **at least 55°F** and never lower — that keeps interior wall cavities above freezing through cold snaps. A smart thermostat on Wi-Fi with low-temperature alerts is worth more than any other single step: your phone tells you the moment the house drops below your setpoint.

Have the furnace or boiler serviced before you leave — a heating failure in a vacant home is a catastrophe unfolding over weeks. Get the fall tune-up, replace the filter, and confirm the system cycles properly before you lock up.

## Electrical and Appliances: Unplug the Non-Essentials

Unplug everything non-essential: TVs, coffee makers, chargers, lamps, small appliances. This guards against surge damage and removes fire risk from an unsupervised house. Leave running only the furnace or boiler, the refrigerator if kept on, the water heater if in vacation mode, and the Wi-Fi gear for your thermostat and leak sensors.

**The refrigerator decision depends on your timeline.** Gone for months: empty it completely, unplug, clean, and prop the doors open so air circulates and mold can't grow. Gone for weeks: leaving it running with perishables removed is fine — just switch the icemaker off and clear all perishables.

Shut off the water valves to the icemaker, dishwasher, and washer regardless — those thin supply hoses are common leak sources, and inlet valves can weep even when the appliance is off.

## Exterior: Button Up the Shell

Clean the gutters thoroughly — clogged gutters in winter mean ice dams and water backing under shingles. Confirm downspouts carry water several feet from the house.

Disconnect every garden hose, drain it, and store it inside; a hose left attached traps water against the valve and bursts exterior faucets. Trim branches over the roof, power lines, or driveway — ice and snow snap weakened limbs. Lock every door and window, including garage, shed, and basement bulkhead doors. Close the fireplace flue damper, and secure or store patio furniture and anything else wind can move.

## Safety: Detectors, Gas, and Eyes on the House

Replace the batteries in every smoke and carbon monoxide detector, then test each one. A vacant house with a furnace running all winter needs working CO detection as much as an occupied one — early detection brings the fire department before the house is gone.

If the furnace burns gas, it stays on — but shut off gas to the fireplace, grill, and other unused appliances. In an all-electric home with everything drained, shutting the main gas valve entirely is safest; a tech may need to relight pilots in spring.

Arrange human eyes on the property. Ask a trusted neighbor to walk through weekly — checking heat, floors, and obvious damage — or hire a house-checking service ($25–75 per visit). The single most valuable visit lands in January or February, the coldest stretch. At minimum, leave a spare key with someone local. Wi-Fi water-leak sensors near the water heater, under sinks, and by the washer are a cheap second layer that pings your phone at the first sign of water.

## Check Your Insurance Policy Before You Leave

Many policies have vacancy clauses: after the home sits empty 30–60 days, coverage for some perils — especially water damage — can shrink or vanish unless you meet conditions like maintaining minimum heat or arranging regular check-ins. Call your agent before shutdown day and ask what your policy requires and whether coverage stays intact. You may need a vacant-home endorsement, usually a modest premium increase. Get the requirements in writing and keep a dated email confirming compliance — "the agent said it was fine" is not documentation if you ever file a claim.

## What It Costs: DIY vs. Hiring a Pro

DIY winterization runs about **$50–150** in supplies: a few gallons of non-toxic RV antifreeze, detector batteries, and a fresh furnace filter. Add roughly $40–75 for a half-day compressor rental if you blow out the lines yourself. Plan on a full day the first time, half a day once you've done it before.

A plumber winterizing fixtures and blowing out lines typically charges **$150–300** as a standalone visit. A full-service winterization company — water, heat check, exterior, detectors — generally runs **$200–500+** depending on home size and region, with a documented checklist some insurers like to see. An HVAC fall tune-up is usually $100–200 on its own. For a large home, a boiler system, or a well, the pro route is worth it at least the first year — watch, take notes, and DIY next winter.

## Keep It Safe All Winter

Schedule at least one mid-winter check in January or February — go yourself or send your house-checker — to confirm the heat is running, the thermostat reads above 55°F, no water is where it shouldn't be, and detectors still have power.

Between visits, let technology watch: a smart thermostat with low-temperature alerts plus two or three Wi-Fi leak sensors costs under $150 total. Keep a shutdown checklist on your phone or taped in a utility closet — next fall you'll repeat 90% of these steps, and a list capturing your home's quirks beats a generic one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What temperature should I leave the thermostat at in an empty vacation home?**
Keep it at 55°F or higher all winter. That keeps wall cavities above freezing through cold snaps and protects water left in traps and appliance lines. Never shut the heat off entirely — the savings are trivial next to one freeze claim.

**Can I just turn off the water and skip the antifreeze?**
Shutting the main and draining handles supply lines, but traps and toilet bowls hold standing water by design. Untreated, it can freeze and crack the fixture — or evaporate over months, letting sewer gases in. A few dollars of non-toxic RV antifreeze in each fixture closes both gaps.

**Should I drain the water heater or use vacation mode?**
For a full winter, drain it — no pressurized tank sitting for months, no wasted energy. For a few weeks, vacation mode is fine and far less work. Either way, cut power or gas before draining and let the water cool first.

**Do I really need to blow out the water lines with compressed air?**
In mild-winter regions, gravity draining usually suffices. Where freezes run deep, yes — low spots and elbows hold water gravity can't clear, and that's where splits happen. Use short bursts around 30–40 psi, or have a plumber do it the first year.

**Should I unplug the refrigerator for the whole winter?**
For months away: empty fully, unplug, clean, and prop the doors open so air circulates and mold can't grow. For a few weeks, leaving it running with perishables removed is fine — just shut off the icemaker's water supply.

**Will my insurance cover damage if the house sits empty all winter?**
Maybe — call your agent first. Many policies limit water-damage coverage after 30–60 vacant days unless you meet conditions like maintaining heat or arranging check-ins. A vacant-home endorsement usually restores full coverage for a modest premium. Get the requirements in writing.

**What if the power goes out while I'm away?**
An outage kills the furnace and the freeze clock starts — which is why low-temperature alerts and a key-holding checker matter. In extreme cold, your checker may need to get inside, confirm the furnace restarted, and verify the temperature is recovering. Plan that before winter, not during the storm.

## When to Call a Pro

Most of winterization is DIY-friendly, but some jobs deserve a professional. Call a plumber for the compressed-air blowout if you've never done one, for boiler or radiant-heat systems, and for well systems where pump and pressure tank need proper shutdown. Call an HVAC technician for the fall furnace or boiler service — combustion systems need trained eyes on burners, heat exchangers, and venting. Full-service winterization companies do it all for roughly $200–500+, with a documented checklist your insurer will appreciate. And if you arrive in spring to frozen or burst pipes, call a plumber before turning the water back on — pressurizing a damaged system turns a repair into a flood.
