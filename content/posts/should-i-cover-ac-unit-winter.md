The short answer: **no, don't fully cover your air conditioner for winter.** It feels like the protective thing to do, but a full cover traps moisture against the metal, accelerates rust and corrosion, and creates a cozy shelter for mice that love chewing wires. Most HVAC manufacturers don't require covers, and some will even void the warranty if a cover caused the damage. What actually protects your condenser is much simpler — and mostly free.

This guide covers what goes wrong under full covers, the top-only compromise most pros recommend, the heat pump exception, and what to do instead to keep your unit healthy through winter.

## What Your Outdoor Unit Is Built to Handle

Your condenser — the big metal box with the fan on top — was engineered to live outside year-round. The cabinet is galvanized steel with a baked-on finish, the coils are aluminum or copper, and the electrical components sit in a sealed compartment. Rain, snow, and ice are part of its normal operating environment. Manufacturers test these units against weather far worse than a typical winter.

That doesn't mean winter is harmless. The real enemies are **standing moisture** (which corrodes from the inside out), **debris** packed against the coils, and **animals** nesting inside. A full cover doesn't defeat any of these — in most cases it makes the first and third worse.

## What Goes Wrong Under a Full Cover

### Trapped moisture and rust

A full cover — especially cheap vinyl — turns your condenser into a terrarium. Snow melts, moisture evaporates, hits the cover, condenses, and drips back onto the metal. With no airflow to dry it, the cycle repeats all winter: rust on the cabinet, corrosion on the coil fins, and moisture wicking into electrical connections.

### Mice and other tenants

A covered condenser is dark, wind-sheltered, and warm in the sun — prime winter real estate for mice. They gnaw thermostat wires, refrigerant line insulation, and wiring inside the electrical compartment. Come spring, the AC won't start because a mouse spent January remodeling. The same [exclusion mindset from keeping mice out in fall](/posts/how-to-keep-mice-out-fall/) applies: don't build them a shelter.

### Voided warranties

Check your owner's manual before covering. Several major manufacturers exclude damage caused by aftermarket covers — the unit was designed to breathe, and trapping moisture against it counts as owner-inflicted damage. A denied $400 coil warranty claim stings far more than the $30 cover cost.

### The "it looks protected" trap

Full covers also hide problems — a sinking pad, ice dams at the base, branches pressing the coils. Out of sight, out of mind, until the spring startup reveals the damage.

## The Compromise Pros Actually Recommend: Top-Only Cover

If you want to do *something*, do what most HVAC techs do on their own houses: cover **only the top** of the unit. A top cover — a piece of plywood, a purpose-made mesh top, or a fitted top cap — keeps falling leaves, twigs, ice chunks, and snowmelt out of the fan compartment while leaving the sides fully open for airflow.

The rules for a top-only cover:

- **Cover just the top**, extending no more than about **6 inches down the sides**. Air must circulate freely around the coils.
- **Secure it** with a bungee cord or a couple of bricks so winter wind doesn't turn it into a projectile.
- **Use breathable material.** Purpose-made mesh condenser tops ($20–$40) are ideal — they shed debris while breathing. A plywood square works fine too.
- **Remove it before the first spring startup.** Running the AC with any cover on can overheat the compressor in minutes.

This gives you 90% of the benefit (no debris packing into the fan, no ice falling directly onto the blades) with none of the moisture-trap downside.

## Heat Pumps: Never Cover

This is the hard rule. If your outdoor unit is a **heat pump** (it heats your house in winter), it runs all winter long — covering it blocks airflow the system needs to operate and can destroy the compressor. Heat pumps also go through defrost cycles that melt ice off the coils; a cover interferes with drainage and refreezing. No cover of any kind on a heat pump, ever. If you're unsure whether you have a straight-cool AC or a heat pump, check the thermostat: an "emergency heat" or "aux heat" setting means heat pump.

## What Actually Protects Your AC in Winter

Do these instead of wrapping the unit — they're what techs recommend and they cost little or nothing:

**1. Clear debris from around the unit.** Rake leaves, trim back bushes to at least 2 feet of clearance on all sides, and pick up fallen branches. Packed wet leaves against the coils hold moisture all winter. Fold this into your [fall gutter and yard cleanup](/posts/when-to-clean-gutters-fall/) — same trip around the house.

**2. Check that the pad is level.** Condensers sit on a concrete or composite pad. If frost heave or settling has tilted it, water pools on one side and strains refrigerant lines. A slightly tilted pad can be shimmed in spring; note it now.

**3. Hose off the coils before winter.** With the power off at the disconnect, gently rinse the coil fins from the inside out with a garden hose (no pressure washer — it folds the fins). Clean coils resist corrosion better and run more efficiently next summer.

**4. Turn off the power at the disconnect.** This prevents accidental running during a warm snap and stops the crankcase heater from drawing power all winter — a small but real saving.

**5. Keep snow from burying it.** After heavy snowfalls, brush snow away from the unit so meltwater doesn't pool and refreeze around the base. Don't pile shoveled snow against it.

**6. Protect the refrigerant lines.** Make sure the foam insulation on the larger copper line is intact from house to unit. Replace cracked sections — $5–$10 for a six-foot sleeve.

## The Spring Startup Checklist

When warm weather returns, run through this before the first hot day:

1. **Remove any cover completely** — top covers included. Check inside for nests or debris first.
2. **Restore power** at the outdoor disconnect at least 24 hours before you need cooling (this lets the crankcase heater warm the compressor oil).
3. **Clear the area** — 2 feet of clearance all around, no leaves packed against the coils.
4. **Check the pad** — still level? Shim if needed.
5. **Inspect wiring** — look for chewed wires or nests in the electrical compartment (power off).
6. **Test run** — set the thermostat to cool, drop it a few degrees below room temp, and confirm cold air within 10–15 minutes. Listen for grinding or rattling.
7. **Change the indoor filter** — a clogged filter is the number-one cause of poor cooling performance and frozen coils.

If the unit doesn't start, trips the breaker, or blows warm air, call an HVAC tech before the first heat wave — spring is their slow season and you'll get faster, cheaper service than in July.

## Safety Warnings

- **Electrical:** always pull the outdoor disconnect before reaching into the unit or hosing the coils. Capacitors inside can hold a charge even with power off — don't poke around the electrical compartment beyond a visual inspection.
- **Refrigerant:** never cut, kink, or disconnect refrigerant lines. Refrigerant is regulated and requires EPA certification to handle; a damaged line means calling a tech.
- **Roof ice:** don't climb on the roof to clear snow above the unit — falling ice and ladders on icy ground are a bad combination. Clear from the ground with a roof rake if needed.
- **Ladder safety:** if you need a ladder to inspect the top of the unit or trim branches, keep it on firm level ground, maintain three points of contact, and never overreach.
- **Running with a cover on:** if anyone in the household might flip the AC on during a warm winter day, tape a bright reminder note on the thermostat until the cover comes off in spring.

## Long-Term Protection (Better Than Any Cover)

**Keep vegetation back year-round.** The 2-foot clearance rule isn't just for winter — bushes crowding the unit restrict summer airflow and trap humidity against the cabinet constantly.

**Elevate if it floods.** If your yard pools water around the unit, have it raised on risers — standing water corrodes the base pan fast. A $200–$400 pro job that pays for itself.

**Get a surge protector.** The control board is vulnerable to lightning and power surges. A dedicated HVAC surge protector ($150–$300 installed) beats a $500+ board replacement.

**Schedule tune-ups every 2–3 years.** A tech checks refrigerant charge, electrical connections, and coil condition — the things that determine lifespan. Maintained units last 15–20 years; neglected ones die at 10–12.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Will snow damage my uncovered AC unit?**
No. Condensers are designed for it — snow on top melts and drains. What damages units is debris packed inside, standing water at the base, and animals nesting in it. Brush off heavy snow so it doesn't pile up, and you're fine.

**My neighbor covers theirs fully every winter and it's fine. Why shouldn't I?**
Survivorship bias. Plenty of units survive full covers, especially in dry climates. But techs consistently find more corrosion and rodent damage under full covers than on uncovered units — the risk is real even when invisible.

**Can I use a tarp instead of buying a cover?**
For a top-only cover, a tarp folded over the top and secured works — as long as the sides stay completely open. Never wrap a tarp fully around the unit; plastic sheeting is the worst moisture trap of all.

**Should I cover the unit if I live somewhere with harsh winters?**
The colder it gets, the more the top-only approach makes sense — and the less sense a full cover makes, since freeze-thaw moisture cycling is worse. Same advice, more snow brushing.

**Does covering save energy?**
No. The outdoor unit uses no energy when the disconnect is off, covered or not. The only winter energy draw is the crankcase heater, which you stop by flipping the disconnect — not by covering.

**What about window AC units — should those be covered?**
Window units are different: remove them for winter if you can (they're a major heat-loss path). If one must stay in, a fitted window-AC cover on the *outside* portion is fine and recommended — it blocks drafts. Just remove it before running the unit.

## When to Call a Pro

Call an HVAC technician if the spring test run fails — no start, tripped breaker, warm air, or loud grinding. Call if you find chewed wiring or a nest inside the electrical compartment, if the pad has sunk significantly, or if refrigerant lines look kinked. If your unit is over 12 years old and struggling, a tech can advise honestly on repair vs. replacement. Everything else — debris, top covers, snow, filters — is homeowner territory.
