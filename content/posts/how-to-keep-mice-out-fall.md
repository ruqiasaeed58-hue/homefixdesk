Every fall, as nights turn cold, mice do exactly what you would do in their position: look for somewhere warm with food. Your house — with its heated walls, pantry, and pet food bowls — is prime real estate. A mouse does not need an open door. It needs a gap the size of a dime, and most houses have dozens. The good news is that keeping mice out is mostly a one-time sealing job plus a few habit changes, and fall is the perfect time to do it — right before the invasion, not after you hear scratching in the walls.

This is a prevention guide first. If you already have mice inside, the trapping section will get you through the current problem, but the sealing work is what stops next year's mice too.

## Why fall is mouse season

Mice live outdoors all summer, nesting in vegetation, woodpiles, and garden beds. As temperatures drop in September and October, their calculus changes: food gets scarce outside, and your heated home offers warmth, shelter, and a stocked pantry. Pest control companies report their busiest rodent season runs from October through February, with the biggest surge in the first cold snaps.

Once inside, mice chew wiring (a genuine fire risk), contaminate food with droppings and urine, and shred insulation for nesting. A single pair can produce dozens of offspring in a season, so every week of delay in fall makes the job harder.

The encouraging part: mice are creatures of habit and opportunity. Remove the opportunity — the entry points and the food — and most will simply move on to an easier house. Your goal is to make your home the difficult option on the block.

## The dime-sized hole rule

Here is the fact that changes how you look at your house: a mouse can squeeze through a hole the size of a dime — roughly a quarter inch. A young mouse can manage even smaller. That means the gap under your garage door, the hole where the cable line enters, the crack where the foundation meets the siding, and the space around a dryer vent are all highways.

Walk the entire perimeter of your house slowly, at ground level, looking specifically for gaps. Bring a flashlight even in daylight — shining it along the foundation-siding joint reveals openings you would otherwise miss. Check:

- Where utilities enter: cable, phone, electrical conduit, gas lines, water spigots, and the air conditioner refrigerant lines. Each penetration is a candidate gap.
- The garage door seal and any gaps at the corners where the door meets the frame.
- Foundation cracks and the joint where the foundation meets the siding or brick.
- Dryer vents, range hood vents, and bathroom exhaust vents — the flaps often do not seal fully.
- Around basement windows and window wells.
- Where the chimney meets the roof, and any gaps in soffits or fascia boards.
- Gaps under doors, especially the door from the garage into the house.

Inside, check under sinks where pipes enter walls, behind the stove and refrigerator, and around the water heater. Mice travel inside walls along pipes and wiring, so an exterior gap plus an interior pipe chase equals mice in the kitchen.

## Sealing materials that actually work

Mice chew through a shocking range of materials. Standard spray foam insulation, plastic, wood, and rubber are all chewable — a mouse will go straight through great stuff foam in a night. Use materials they cannot gnaw:

**Steel wool + caulk (small holes).** Pack coarse steel wool tightly into holes up to about an inch, then seal over it with caulk or silicone. The steel wool's sharp fibers deter chewing, and the caulk holds it in place and weatherproofs it. Use stainless or copper wool rather than plain steel wool in damp areas — plain steel wool rusts and stains. Copper mesh products (like copper "stuff-it" mesh, $10 to $15 a roll) are the premium version: they do not rust and mice hate them.

**Hardware cloth (larger gaps).** For gaps over an inch — around vents, under decks, behind soffits — use 1/4-inch galvanized hardware cloth, cut to size and screwed or stapled in place. Chicken wire is too flimsy and its holes are too big; hardware cloth is the right product. A roll costs $15 to $30.

**Metal flashing and door sweeps.** Gaps under exterior doors need brush or rubber door sweeps ($8 to $15 each) screwed to the door bottom. If you can see daylight under a door, a mouse can walk under it. Garage doors often need new bottom seals or side seals — check the corners especially.

**What not to use alone.** Expanding spray foam is useful as a backer and air sealer, but never as the mouse barrier by itself — mice chew right through it. If you foam a gap, embed steel wool or copper mesh in it, or cover it with hardware cloth.

Work from the outside in, and prioritize the warm sides of the house and anywhere you have seen droppings before. You do not need a perfect seal on day one — every gap you close is one fewer entrance.

## Top entry points, ranked

1. **Garage door seals.** The number one mouse entrance in most homes. Check the bottom rubber seal and the corners where it meets the frame. Replace worn seals.
2. **Utility penetrations.** Cable, phone, and electrical entries are drilled oversized and rarely sealed properly. Steel wool + caulk each one.
3. **Dryer and exhaust vents.** Add a proper vent cover with a damper and rodent guard if yours is just an open flap.
4. **Doors to the outside and garage.** Install or replace door sweeps wherever you see daylight.
5. **Foundation-siding joint.** Walk the perimeter and seal every gap you can fit a pencil into.
## Sanitation: cut off the food

A sealed house with abundant food still attracts persistent mice, and food is what turns a scouting mouse into a resident. Mice need very little — a few crumbs a night sustains them.

**Store food in hard containers.** Cereal, flour, rice, pasta, pet food, and birdseed all belong in glass, metal, or thick hard-plastic containers with tight lids. Cardboard boxes and thin plastic bags are not mouse-proof. This includes pet food — never leave it out overnight, and store the bag itself in a sealed bin.

**Clean like a mouse is watching.** Wipe counters nightly, sweep under the table and stove regularly, and do not let dishes sit. Pay attention to the toaster area, under the refrigerator, and inside the pantry — crumbs accumulate where you do not look.

**Manage the exterior.** Move firewood at least 20 feet from the house and off the ground. Trim shrubs and tree branches back from the walls — vegetation touching the house is a mouse ladder. Remove bird feeders near the house in fall (or accept that they are a mouse buffet), secure trash cans with tight lids, and clean up fallen fruit promptly.

## Trap placement that works

Traps catch the mice already inside; they do not solve an open entry. Set them while you seal, not instead of sealing.

**Use snap traps, not glue boards.** Snap traps kill instantly and are the most humane lethal option. Glue boards cause prolonged suffering and are banned or discouraged in a growing number of places. Live-catch traps work only if you release mice at least a mile away — released nearby, they come straight back.

**Bait matters less than location.** Peanut butter is the classic because it is sticky and aromatic — a pea-sized dab on the trigger. Chocolate, bacon, or dried fruit also work. Cheese, despite the cartoons, is mediocre.

**Place traps where mice travel.** Mice hug walls and rarely cross open rooms. Set traps perpendicular to the wall with the trigger end touching the baseboard, in pairs a few feet apart — mice often jump over a single trap. Prime spots: along kitchen baseboards, behind the stove and refrigerator, in the pantry, along basement walls, and in the garage near the door. Check daily.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Using spray foam alone.** Mice chew through expanding foam overnight. Always pair it with steel wool, copper mesh, or hardware cloth.
- **Trapping without sealing.** You will catch mice forever if the entries stay open. New mice replace trapped ones within days.
- **Sealing mice inside.** If you already hear activity in the walls, trap first for a week or two, then seal. Sealing live mice inside means dead mice inside — and the smell that follows.
- **Ignoring the garage.** The garage-to-house door and garage door seals are the top entry route. A perfect house seal with a gappy garage is no seal at all.
- **Storing food in the original bags.** Cardboard and thin plastic are packaging, not protection. Hard containers with lids or nothing.
- **Buying ultrasonic repellers.** No credible evidence they work. Spend the money on copper mesh instead.
- **Using poison as a first resort.** Rodenticides kill slowly, mice die inside walls (odor for weeks), and poisoned mice poison owls, hawks, cats, and dogs up the food chain. Traps and exclusion are safer and more effective for homeowners.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I know if I have mice or rats?**
Droppings are the giveaway: mouse droppings are the size of rice grains (about 1/4 inch) with pointed ends; rat droppings are larger (1/2 to 3/4 inch) and blunter. Mice also leave tiny gnaw marks, shredded nesting material, and a musky odor in enclosed spaces. Scratching in walls at night is classic mouse behavior.

**Can mice climb walls to get in upstairs?**
Yes. Mice are excellent climbers — they scale brick, siding, downspouts, and tree branches to reach upper gaps, soffits, and attic vents. Do not limit your inspection to ground level; check the whole exterior including where the roof meets the walls.

**How long does it take to get rid of mice?**
With aggressive trapping plus sealing, most small invasions resolve in 2 to 4 weeks — defined as two full weeks with no new catches and no new droppings. If you are still catching mice after a month, you have missed entry points and need another perimeter inspection.

**Is it safe to seal entry points if mice might already be inside?**
Trap first, seal second. Set snap traps aggressively for one to two weeks to clear the interior population, then seal the exterior. Sealing with mice inside traps them in your walls, where they die and decompose — an odor problem that lasts weeks and requires opening walls to fix.

## When to call a pro

Call a pest control professional if you are catching mice for more than a month with no end in sight (you have missed entries a pro will find), if droppings are widespread across multiple rooms (indicating an established population), if mice are in wall voids or attic spaces you cannot access, or if there are signs of rats rather than mice — rats are larger, warier, and harder for homeowners to eliminate. A professional exclusion job (sealing + trapping) typically runs $200 to $600 and usually includes a warranty period. Also call a pro if anyone in the home is immunocompromised or if you find extensive droppings in food storage areas — commercial-grade cleanup with proper respirators and disinfectants is worth it. For the standard fall scenario — a few mice scouting for winter shelter — the steel wool, traps, and sanitation in this guide will handle it.
