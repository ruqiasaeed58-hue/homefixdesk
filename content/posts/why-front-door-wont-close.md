A front door that sticks, drags on the floor, or refuses to latch is more than an annoyance — it is a security problem and an energy leak. The cause is almost always one of three things: loose or worn hinges, seasonal wood swelling, or a shifted frame. Each has a distinct signature and a distinct fix.

## Why doors stop closing (the three root causes)

A door is a heavy slab hanging on two or three small hinges, expected to align with a strike plate to 1/16-inch precision. Three things defeat this:

1. **Gravity + loose hinges:** a 60–100 lb door hanging on screws in soft jamb wood. Over years, the top hinge screws loosen and the door rotates down on the latch side — the classic sag. The top hinge carries almost all the weight; when it fails, the door drops.
2. **Moisture cycling:** wood expands across the grain when it absorbs humidity. A door that fits perfectly in dry winter air can swell 1/8 inch or more in humid summer — enough to bind. Paint slows this but does not stop it, especially on unsealed edges.
3. **House movement:** foundations settle, framing shrinks, and door frames rack out of square over decades. The reveal (gap around the door) tells the story — uneven reveals mean the frame moved, not the door.

Diagnose in order: hinges first (most common, easiest), swelling second (seasonal pattern), frame third (uneven reveal).

## Quick diagnosis: what exactly is wrong?

- **Door sticks or rubs at the top** → the top hinge is loose or the door is sagging. See Steps 1–2.
- **Door sticks on the lock side (latch edge)** → seasonal swelling or paint buildup. See Step 3.
- **Door drags on the floor/threshold** → sagging hinges or a settled frame. See Steps 1–2.
- **Door closes but won't latch** → strike plate misaligned. See Step 4.
- **Problem comes and goes with the seasons** → humidity swelling. See Step 3.

## What you will need

- #2 Phillips screwdriver (or drill/driver)
- 3-inch wood screws (to replace short hinge screws)
- Wood shims or a folded business card
- Hand plane or belt sander (for swelling)
- Utility knife
- Chisel and hammer (for strike plate adjustment)
- Pencil and painter's tape

## Step 1: Check and tighten the hinges

Open the door and lift it by the handle. If you feel it move up and down, the hinges are loose — this is the #1 cause of doors that suddenly stop closing.

1. **Tighten all hinge screws**, starting with the top hinge (it carries most of the weight).
2. If a screw spins without gripping, the hole is stripped. Remove the screw, push in a wooden golf tee or dowel coated with wood glue, let it dry, trim flush, and re-drive the screw.
3. **Replace one short screw per hinge with a 3-inch screw** driven into the framing stud behind the jamb. This single upgrade fixes most sagging doors permanently — the long screw pulls the whole jamb back to the framing.

Test the door. If it closes cleanly now, you are done.

## Step 2: Shim a sagging hinge

If tightening is not enough and the door still sags (gap wider at the top on the latch side):

1. Remove the screws from the **bottom hinge** leaf on the jamb side.
2. Slide a thin cardboard shim (a folded business card works) behind the hinge leaf and re-screw.
3. This tilts the door upward slightly, countering the sag. Test and add a second shim if needed.

Conversely, if the door binds at the top, shim the **top hinge** instead.

## Step 3: Fix swelling and rubbing spots

Wood doors swell in humid weather. If the problem is seasonal and the hinges are tight:

1. **Find the rub.** Close the door slowly and look for where it contacts the frame. For precision, rub pencil or chalk on the frame edge and close the door — the transfer mark shows the exact rub spot.
2. **Mark the area** with painter's tape so you do not over-remove.
3. **Remove the door** (tap the hinge pins up and out with a nail and hammer) and lay it flat.
4. **Plane or sand the marked area** — remove a little at a time. A hand plane gives the most control; a belt sander is faster but aggressive.
5. **Seal the raw wood.** Bare wood absorbs moisture and swells again. Paint or varnish every planed edge — including the top and bottom of the door, which are often left unsealed from the factory and are the main moisture entry points.
6. Rehang and test.

## Step 4: Realign the strike plate

If the door closes but the latch will not catch:

1. Mark where the latch bolt hits the strike plate (lipstick or chalk on the bolt works).
2. If it is off by less than 1/8 inch, **file the strike plate opening** wider in the direction of the miss.
3. If it is off more, **move the strike plate**: unscrew it, chisel the mortise slightly in the needed direction, and reattach. Fill old screw holes with a glued dowel first if the screws would land in them.
4. For a quick fix on a door that almost latches, a slightly larger strike plate opening filed with a metal file is often enough.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Planing before checking hinges.** Most "swollen" doors are actually sagging. Tighten first, plane last.
- **Removing too much wood.** You can always take off more; you cannot put it back. Remove a hair at a time.
- **Leaving planed edges unsealed.** Unsealed wood re-absorbs moisture and the problem returns worse than before.
- **Ignoring a door that won't latch.** A door that does not latch is a door that does not lock. Treat latch problems as urgent.
- **Shimming the wrong hinge.** Shimming the bottom hinge raises the latch side; shimming the top hinge lowers it. Get it backwards and you make the problem worse.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does my door only stick in summer?**
Wood absorbs moisture from humid air and expands. Exterior doors are most affected. Sealing all six edges of the door (including top and bottom) is the long-term fix.

**Can I fix a sagging door without removing it?**
Yes — the 3-inch screw trick (Step 1) and hinge shimming (Step 2) are both done with the door in place.

**My door drags on the new flooring — what now?**
The floor raised the threshold. You may need to trim the bottom of the door — mark it, remove it, and plane or saw 1/4 inch off, then reseal the cut edge.

**How do I know if the frame itself shifted?**
Check the reveal (the gap around the door). It should be roughly even — about 1/8 inch all around. If the gap is wide at the top on one side and tight at the bottom on the other, the frame racked and may need a carpenter to re-square it.

**Why won't my door latch in winter but latches fine in summer?**
The reverse of the usual swelling pattern — in this case the house framing shrinks in dry winter air, shifting the strike plate alignment slightly. A small strike plate adjustment (filing the opening) usually covers both seasons.

**Should I replace the hinges entirely?**
If the hinges are visibly worn (loose pins, bent leaves) or undersized for the door weight, yes — upgrade to ball-bearing hinges rated for exterior doors ($10–$20 each). They last decades longer than cheap builder hinges and support heavy doors better.

## Weatherstripping and energy (while you are at it)

A door that does not close properly leaks air — fixing the alignment is also an energy fix:

1. **Check the weatherstripping** once the door closes properly. Compressed or torn weatherstripping defeats the whole repair.
2. **The dollar-bill test:** close the door on a dollar bill at several points. If it pulls out easily, air leaks there too.
3. **Adjust the threshold.** Most thresholds have adjustable screws — raise it until it just kisses the door bottom sweep.
4. **Replace the door sweep** if it is worn — a $10 part that blocks the biggest air gap.

## When to call a pro

- The frame is visibly racked or the house has settled — re-squaring a door frame is carpenter work ($200–$400).
- The door is steel or fiberglass and warped — these cannot be planed; replacement is the fix.
- The latch will not engage and you need security tonight — a locksmith can adjust the strike on the spot.
- Water is coming under the door — the threshold or weatherstripping has failed and may need replacement.
