A washer that shakes violently during spin is almost always suffering from a short list of fixable problems — most cost nothing. Work the free-fixes ladder first: rebalance the load, level the feet, and on a new front-loader, check for shipping bolts the installer forgot to remove. Only when it shakes with balanced loads on level feet do you move to worn suspension or bearings.

During spin, the drum reaches 800–1,200 RPM, and any off-center weight becomes a powerful centrifugal force. The suspension — springs, rods, or shock absorbers — is designed to absorb normal vibration, not fight a lopsided load or a machine rocking on three feet. A washer that walks across the floor is telling you the vibration has nowhere to go but the whole cabinet.

One distinction: **rhythmic thumping** usually means an unbalanced load; a **constant loud roar or grinding** means bearings. Thumping gets the free-fix ladder below; roaring skips to the bearings section near the end.

## Free fix #1: redistribute the load

This solves the majority of shaking complaints. A single heavy item — a bathrobe, a comforter, soaked jeans — clumps to one side, and at 1,000 RPM that one-sided weight hammers the machine around.

Pause the cycle, open the door, and redistribute items evenly. If it's one huge item, add a few towels. For top-loaders, arrange items in a loose ring around the agitator — never a mound on one side. Restart and watch the spin; if the shaking stops, you've found it.

Wash heavy items with other heavy items. Three bath towels and one king-size sheet is a thump-fest — mix in a couple of medium items to give the drum a fighting chance.

## Free fix #2: level the feet (front-to-back and side-to-side)

A washer rocking on uneven feet amplifies every vibration — this takes five minutes and a $10 level (or a free level app).

1. **Check both directions.** Place the level across the top, front to back and left to right — and on front-loaders, check the front face too, since the cabinet can rack (twist) on an uneven floor.
2. **Adjust the feet.** Washer feet screw in and out — reach under the corners and turn to raise or lower each one. (Some models have self-leveling rear legs that reset when you tilt the machine forward.) Most feet have a locking nut: loosen it first, adjust, then tighten it back so vibration can't spin the foot out of position.
3. **The push-rock test.** With the machine empty, push firmly on each top corner in turn. If any corner rocks or clunks, that foot isn't carrying weight — adjust until all four are planted and nothing rocks.
4. **Recheck with a spin.** Run a drain-and-spin cycle empty. It should hum and vibrate slightly but stay planted. If it walks, a foot is still off or the floor is the problem (more below).

On many machines the front feet adjust by hand or wrench, and the rear feet are self-leveling — tilt the machine forward and drop it to reset them. Check your manual for your model's scheme.

## Free fix #3: check the pedestal and the floor

If your front-loader sits on a storage pedestal, the pedestal is part of the leveling equation — and a common culprit. Pedestals have their own adjustable feet; a level pedestal under an unlevel washer (or vice versa) rocks. Level the pedestal first, then the washer on top, then re-run the push-rock test on the stack. Also verify the washer is bolted to the pedestal — some installs skip the brackets, and an unsecured washer dances no matter how level it is.

The floor matters too. Washers on springy wood floors — especially upper stories — shake more because the floor itself flexes and resonates. If the machine is level and balanced but still vibrates excessively, an anti-vibration pad (~$25–$40) under the feet decouples it from the floor and stops the vibration from transmitting through the house.

## The classic gotcha: shipping bolts on new front-loaders

If the machine has shaken violently from the very first load, **check the shipping bolts.** Front-loaders ship with 3–5 large bolts (or plastic spacers) that lock the drum rigid for transport — they must be removed before first use. With the bolts in, the drum can't move on its suspension, so every vibration goes straight into the cabinet.

The bolts are on the back panel, usually with yellow or orange warning tags. Remove them with a wrench and plug the holes with the plastic caps from the parts bag. Repeated running with the bolts in can crack the drum spider or damage the suspension — so if you find them, run a test spin before assuming all is well. This accounts for a remarkable share of "brand new washer shakes" complaints, and it's a five-minute fix.

## Worn suspension: rods, springs, and shock absorbers

When a machine that used to run smoothly starts shaking with normal loads on level feet, the suspension is wearing out:

- **Top-loaders:** four suspension rods with springs and dampers that hang the tub from the cabinet corners. When the dampers wear, the tub swings too freely. Telltale sign: violent shaking only during spin, and the empty tub keeps oscillating when pushed by hand instead of settling. A rod kit costs **$30–$60** and is a genuine DIY job — tip the machine back, unhook the old rods, drop in the new ones (30–60 minutes).
- **Front-loaders:** two or three shock absorbers at the bottom plus large springs at the top. Worn shocks let the drum slam around. A set runs **$20–$50**, and the job is moderate DIY — remove the front or rear panel, unclip the old dampers, clip in the new ones.

Before ordering parts, do the push test: push the empty tub down or sideways and release. It should bounce once and settle — continued oscillation like a bobblehead means the dampers are shot.

## Bearings: the loud roar

If spin sounds like a jet engine — a deep roar that gets louder as the spin speeds up — that's the drum bearings. They fail from age, chronic overloading, and front-loader seal leaks that let water reach them. Unlike load imbalance, bearing noise is constant through the whole spin, often with rusty residue leaking from under the machine, or slight play when you lift the inner drum.

Bearing replacement is the expensive fix: **$300–$500 professionally**, because the bearing is pressed into the outer tub and the whole machine must come apart. On some models the outer tub is replaced as a unit, pushing the repair toward a new machine's cost. Rule of thumb: if the quote exceeds half the price of a comparable new washer, replace the washer.

## Safety warnings

**Unplug the machine before any repair that involves removing panels.** Washers combine water, electricity, and heavy moving parts — pull the plug, not just "turn it off," first.

**A walking washer is a flooding and fire risk.** A migrating machine can kink or tear supply hoses and yank the drain hose out of the standpipe. Fix it promptly — and check that the supply hoses aren't over five years old and the drain hose is secured.

**Don't overload to "balance" a small load.** Stuffing extra items in to even out one heavy piece just creates a heavier unbalanced load. The fix is fewer items plus towels, not more laundry.

**Front-loader door locks engage for a reason.** Never force the door open mid-cycle — wait for the pause/unlock. Forcing it can break the $50–$100 lock mechanism or open on a drum full of water.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does my washer only shake during the spin cycle?**
Only spin reaches 800–1,200 RPM, where small imbalances become huge centrifugal forces; wash and rinse run at 40–60 RPM. Shaking exclusive to spin points at load balance, leveling, or suspension — not the motor or transmission.

**Can an unbalanced load damage the washer?**
Yes, over time. Occasional thumping is harmless, but chronic violent shaking wears dampers prematurely, can crack the front-loader drum spider, loosens hose clamps, and can tear supply hoses. Treat recurring violent shaking as a repair prompt.

**Should I use a high-spin speed or low-spin speed?**
Higher spin extracts more water but amplifies imbalance. If it shakes at max spin, try one step down — a load that shakes at 1,200 may run smooth at 800. But don't use reduced spin as a permanent bandage for worn suspension.

**Why does my brand-new washer shake?**
Shipping bolts, almost always. New front-loaders ship with the drum bolted rigid — the bolts must come out before first use. If they're out and it still shakes from day one, the feet weren't leveled at installation — run the push-rock test.

**My washer shakes even when empty. What's wrong?**
An empty machine that shakes on spin has a mechanical problem, not a load problem. Check leveling (push-rock test), then suspension (free tub oscillation = worn dampers), then shipping bolts if new, then bearings (roaring). Ruling out the load narrows the diagnosis.

**Do anti-vibration pads really work?**
They help with floor-transmitted vibration — springy wood floors, upper-story laundry rooms — but don't fix unbalanced loads or worn suspension. The last 20% of the solution, not the first.

## Prevention tips

- **Balance loads and don't exceed capacity.** Mix heavy and light items, never wash one heavy thing alone, and fill to about 3/4 full — overloading strains suspension and bearings.
- **Level check annually.** Feet vibrate out of adjustment; the push-rock test takes 30 seconds.
- **Replace rubber supply hoses with braided stainless steel** ($15–$25 a pair) — a walking washer is less likely to burst them.
- **Leave the front-loader door ajar between loads.** This prevents mold on the door gasket — a degraded seal lets water reach the bearings.
- **Run the cleaning cycle monthly.** A hot empty cycle with washer cleaner removes residue buildup and odor.

## When to call a pro

- Constant loud roaring or grinding during spin (bearings) — get a quote and compare against replacement cost
- Brown/rusty water under the machine — likely a seal and bearing failure
- The drum has visible play when you lift it — worn bearings or a broken spider arm
- Visibly broken suspension parts (snapped rod, detached spring) and you're not comfortable tipping the machine
- The machine trips the breaker or smells of burning during spin — electrical fault, stop using it
- Shipping bolts were run in for many cycles and you suspect damage — have a tech inspect the drum spider and suspension
- Any repair that requires splitting the outer tub — that's a full teardown best left to a professional
