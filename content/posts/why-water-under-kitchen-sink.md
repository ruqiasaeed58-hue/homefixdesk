Finding water under the kitchen sink is stressful — but the leak is almost always visible once you know where to look. Water under a sink comes from only three places: the supply lines (pressurized, leaks constantly), the drain assembly (leaks only when water runs down the drain), or the faucet/tap above (drips down through the mounting holes). This guide helps you trace it to the source.

## First: protect the cabinet

Before diagnosing, put down old towels and empty the cabinet. If the cabinet floor is swollen, soft, or moldy, the leak has been going a while — dry everything with a fan and check for mold. A cabinet floor that stays wet rots, so finding the source quickly matters.

## Quick diagnosis: when does it leak?

- **Leaks constantly, even with everything off** → pressurized supply line or shutoff valve. See Step 1.
- **Leaks only when the faucet runs** → faucet base, supply connection at the faucet, or sprayer hose. See Step 2.
- **Leaks only when the drain is used** → drain assembly: basket strainer, P-trap connections, or disposal. See Step 3.
- **Leaks only when the dishwasher runs** → dishwasher drain hose connection. See Step 3.

## Step 1: Check the supply lines and shutoff valves

These are the pressurized lines — a leak here does not stop on its own.

1. **Dry everything** with a towel so new moisture is obvious.
2. **Run your hand along each supply line** (hot and cold) from the shutoff valve up to the faucet. Feel for moisture at every connection.
3. **Check the shutoff valves themselves.** The packing nut (under the handle) often weeps — tightening it a quarter-turn with a wrench usually stops it. If the valve body is corroded or the leak persists, the valve needs replacing ($10–$20, or a plumber if you are not comfortable).
4. **Look at the line material.** Braided stainless lines last 10–15 years; old plastic or corroded copper lines should be replaced proactively. A burst supply line floods the kitchen — this is the leak to take seriously.

If a supply connection is the source: turn off the valve, disconnect the line, replace the washer or the whole line, reconnect hand-tight plus a quarter-turn with a wrench.

## Step 2: Check the faucet and sprayer from above

Water from the faucet area runs down through the mounting holes and appears "under the sink" even though the source is above.

1. **Run the faucet and watch underneath** with a flashlight. Drips falling from the faucet shanks or mounting nuts point upward.
2. **Check the sprayer hose** — pull it out and run water through it. A cracked sprayer hose leaks only when the sprayer is used, and the water runs back down into the cabinet.
3. **Check the faucet base.** If water pools around the faucet on the countertop and seeps down, the faucet's O-rings or base gasket need replacing.

## Step 3: Check the drain assembly

Drain leaks only appear when water flows down the drain — fill the sink and release it to test.

1. **Basket strainer:** the metal strainer where it meets the sink. If water drips from the locknut underneath, the plumber's putty seal has failed. Fix: remove the strainer, roll a fresh rope of plumber's putty, reset the strainer, and tighten.
2. **P-trap connections:** the curved trap under the drain has slip-joint nuts. Hand-tighten each nut — they loosen over time. If a nut is cracked or the washer inside is flattened, replace it ($3–$5 in parts).
3. **Disposal connections:** if you have a garbage disposal, check where the drain pipe meets it and where the dishwasher hose connects. These clamps loosen.
4. **Dishwasher drain hose:** the hose should loop up high before connecting. If the connection drips only during dishwasher cycles, tighten the clamp.

## The paper towel trick

For elusive leaks: dry everything, then wrap dry paper towels around each connection and joint. Run water (faucet, then drain, then dishwasher). Check the towels — the wet one marks your leak. This turns an invisible seep into an obvious answer.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Ignoring a supply-line leak.** Drain leaks are messy; supply leaks are destructive — they run 24/7 at full pressure. Prioritize accordingly.
- **Overtightening plastic slip nuts.** Hand-tight plus a small nudge is enough. Cranking cracks them.
- **Using the wrong sealant.** Plumber's putty for the basket strainer (not silicone — it makes future removal miserable); Teflon tape for threaded supply connections.
- **Not replacing old supply lines.** If they are 15+ years old, discolored, or stiff, replace them now — on your schedule, not at 2 AM.
- **Forgetting the dishwasher hose.** It is the forgotten connection that only leaks during dishwasher cycles, making it hard to catch.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is water under the sink an emergency?**
A supply-line leak is — turn off the shutoff valve immediately. A drain leak can wait a day or two but should not wait a week; standing water rots the cabinet and grows mold.

**Why does it only leak sometimes?**
Intermittent leaks are almost always drain-side (only when water flows) or temperature-related (a connection that weeps when pipes expand with hot water). The paper towel trick catches these.

**Can I just put a bucket under it for now?**
As a stopgap for a drain drip, yes — but a supply leak needs the valve shut off, not a bucket. And do not let "temporary" become permanent; cabinet damage compounds.

**How do I know if there is mold?**
Musty smell, black or green staining on the cabinet floor or back wall, or wood that stays damp. Small surface mold can be cleaned with a mold-killing cleaner; extensive mold inside the cabinet structure needs professional remediation.

## When to call a pro

- A supply line bursts or a shutoff valve will not close — water is flowing and you cannot stop it. Know where your home's main water shutoff is before this happens.
- The leak is inside the wall behind the cabinet — opening the wall and repairing supply pipe is plumber work.
- The cabinet floor is rotted through — a carpenter or cabinet shop may need to rebuild it.
- You find extensive mold — professional remediation is safer than DIY for large growth.
- The faucet itself needs replacing and the mounting nuts are corroded solid — a plumber has the tools (and patience) for seized fittings.
