A dripping faucet is almost always caused by a worn-out internal seal — a cartridge in single-handle faucets, or a rubber washer in older two-handle faucets. The fix takes about 30 minutes, costs under $25 in parts, and stops the drip that can waste over 3,000 gallons of water per year.

## Why faucets drip (what actually wears out)

Every faucet seals water with the same basic principle: a soft material pressed against a hard seat. The drip starts when that seal degrades:

- **Rubber washers harden** — years of hot water and chlorine make rubber brittle. A hardened washer no longer compresses to seal, so water seeps past.
- **Cartridges wear** — the ceramic discs or rubber seals inside a cartridge develop micro-scratches from mineral grit in the water. Each scratch is a tiny channel for water.
- **O-rings flatten** — the round profile compresses permanently over time (called "compression set"), leaving gaps.
- **Valve seats corrode** — the brass seat the washer presses against pits from mineral-laden water. A pitted seat chews up new washers fast.

This is why "just tighten it harder" fails: you are crushing an already-dead seal against a possibly damaged seat. Replacement is the only real fix.

## Quick diagnosis: what kind of faucet do you have?

**One handle** (lever or knob controlling both hot and cold)? You have a **cartridge faucet**. The drip is coming from a worn cartridge — a single replaceable unit.

**Two separate handles** (one hot, one cold)? You have a **compression faucet**. The drip is coming from a worn rubber washer or O-ring on the valve stem.

Not sure? Look under the sink at the supply lines, or check the brand name printed on the faucet — you will need it when buying the replacement part.

## Tools and parts you will need

- Adjustable wrench and/or basin wrench
- Phillips and flat-head screwdrivers
- Replacement cartridge (single-handle) or washer/O-ring kit (two-handle) — **take the old part to the store to match it**
- Plumber's grease (small tube, a few dollars)
- Towel and a small bucket
- Phone camera — take a photo at each disassembly step so reassembly is easy

## Fix 1: Single-handle faucet (cartridge replacement)

1. **Turn off the water.** Close both shut-off valves under the sink (turn clockwise). Open the faucet to release pressure and confirm the water is off.
2. **Plug the drain.** Drop the stopper in or lay a towel over it — small screws love to disappear down drains.
3. **Remove the handle.** Look for a small set screw on the side or back of the handle (often hidden under a decorative cap — pry it off gently with a flat screwdriver). Loosen the screw and lift the handle off.
4. **Remove the retaining nut and cartridge.** Unscrew the dome-shaped retaining nut with your adjustable wrench. Pull the old cartridge straight out. Note its orientation.
5. **Install the new cartridge.** Apply a thin film of plumber's grease to the O-rings, slide the new cartridge in exactly the same way the old one sat, and re-tighten the retaining nut — snug, not gorilla-tight.
6. **Reassemble and test.** Put the handle back on, turn the water supply back on slowly, and check for drips. Run both hot and cold.

## Fix 2: Two-handle faucet (washer replacement)

1. **Turn off the water** at the under-sink shut-off valves and plug the drain.
2. **Remove the handle.** Pop off the decorative cap (often marked H or C), unscrew the handle screw, and lift the handle off.
3. **Unscrew the valve stem.** Use your wrench on the packing nut to unscrew the whole stem assembly counter-clockwise.
4. **Replace the washer.** At the bottom of the stem you will see a small rubber washer held by a brass screw. Remove the screw, swap in the new washer (same size — this is why you brought the old one to the store), and re-tighten the screw.
5. **Check the O-ring.** While the stem is out, inspect the O-ring around its body. If it is cracked or flattened, replace it too — a five-minute job that prevents future leaks around the handle base.
6. **Reassemble and test.** Screw the stem back in, reattach the handle, turn the water on, and check for drips.

## What the fix costs

| Option | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Washer / O-ring kit | $3–$8 |
| Replacement cartridge | $10–$25 |
| Plumber's grease | $3–$5 |
| Plumber visit (if you hire it out) | $150–$300 |

A single faucet dripping once per second wastes roughly 3,000 gallons a year — at average US water rates, that is $20–$60 literally down the drain. The $15 part pays for itself fast.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Overtightening the retaining nut or stem.** This cracks cartridges and deforms new washers. Snug is enough.
- **Buying the part before removing the old one.** Cartridges are brand- and model-specific. Bring the old cartridge to the store, or order by the exact model number.
- **Forgetting which way the cartridge faced.** Mark the top with a marker before pulling it out.
- **Skipping the plumber's grease.** Dry O-rings tear during installation and leak within weeks.

## Preventing future drips

- Do not crank handles shut with force — gentle closing dramatically extends washer life.
- If your water is hard, mineral buildup wears seals faster. Cleaning aerators every few months helps the whole faucet last longer.
- Replace supply-line shut-off valves if they are frozen or corroded while you are under the sink anyway — you will thank yourself next time.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does my faucet drip only at night?**
Thermal expansion. As your water heater runs overnight, pressure in the lines rises slightly and pushes past a worn seal that holds fine during the day. The seal still needs replacing — the nighttime drip is just the early warning.

**Can I just tighten the handle harder to stop the drip?**
Temporarily, maybe. But it grinds the washer down faster and can score the metal valve seat, turning a $5 washer job into a faucet replacement. Fix the seal instead.

**My faucet drips from the base of the spout, not the tip. Same fix?**
Usually yes — that is a worn O-ring on the spout base (common on single-handle kitchen faucets). The cartridge replacement steps above expose it; swap the O-rings while you are in there.

**How do I know the valve seat is damaged?**
If a brand-new washer still drips, run your fingertip around the metal seat inside the faucet body. A rough or grooved seat needs re-seating with a cheap valve-seat wrench, or the faucet needs replacing.

**Should I repair or replace the whole faucet?**
Repair if: the faucet is under 10 years old, the finish is good, and it is a single failed seal. Replace if: the faucet is 15+ years old, the finish is corroded, you have repaired it before and it drips again, or parts are discontinued. A decent new faucet is $50–$150 — sometimes cheaper than repeated part hunts.

**Why does my new cartridge still drip?**
Three possibilities: the cartridge is not seated fully (remove and reinstall, checking orientation), debris on the valve seat (flush the line before installing), or the wrong cartridge model (even close-looking cartridges differ by millimeters — match the exact part number).

## Extending faucet life

- **Clean aerators quarterly.** Unscrew the aerator, soak it in vinegar for an hour, scrub, and reinstall. Mineral-clogged aerators increase back-pressure that stresses internal seals.
- **Exercise the shut-off valves.** Turn under-sink valves off and on once a year so they do not freeze in place — you need them working for the next repair.
- **Soften hard water.** If your area has very hard water, a softener dramatically extends the life of every rubber seal and cartridge in the house.
- **Fix drips promptly.** A small drip becomes a scored valve seat over months — the $5 fix becomes a $150 faucet replacement if you wait.

## When to call a plumber

- The shut-off valves under the sink are frozen, corroded, or missing — you need a working shut-off before any faucet work.
- Water is leaking inside the wall or under the cabinet floor, not just from the faucet.
- The valve seat is deeply pitted or cracked — re-seating will not save it.
- You have replaced the cartridge or washer correctly and it still drips — the faucet body itself may be worn out.
