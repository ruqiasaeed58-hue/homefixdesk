**Short answer: keep the heat at 55°F or higher, shut off the main water valve, and drain the pipes if you will be gone more than a few days.** A burst pipe is the most expensive thing that can happen to an empty house in winter — water damage averages $5,000–$15,000 per incident, and a pipe that bursts on day two runs water until you return.

Pipes do not freeze because it is cold outside. They freeze because the water inside them gets cold enough, long enough — and that happens in wall cavities, crawl spaces, and unheated corners long before the living room feels chilly. When you are home, daily water use and normal heating protect the system. When you leave, you have to replace that protection deliberately. Here is the full playbook.

## Why Empty Houses Freeze Pipes Faster

Three things change when you leave:

**No water movement.** Running water resists freezing far better than standing water. A house where nobody flushes, showers, or runs the tap for a week gives every pipe the still, cold conditions freezing needs.

**Lower thermostat.** Most people set the heat back when they travel — sensible for the bill, but it shrinks the safety margin in every wall cavity. Rooms you never think about (the guest bath, the laundry corner) run colder than the thermostat hallway.

**No one to notice.** A pipe does not burst the instant it freezes — it bursts when the ice blockage builds pressure, often when things start thawing. At home, you hear the hammering, see the bulge, catch the first drip. Away, a pinhole leak becomes a flooded first floor over days.

The combination is why insurance claims for burst pipes spike after holiday travel periods. The protection strategy has three layers: keep heat in, take water out, and watch remotely.

## Layer 1: Keep the Heat On (the Right Way)

**Set the thermostat to 55°F minimum — 60°F if extreme cold is forecast.** This is the widely recommended floor (see our full breakdown of [thermostat settings for winter vacation](/posts/thermostat-setting-winter-vacation/)): wall cavities run 10–20 degrees colder than room air, so 55°F in the room keeps pipes in walls safely above freezing.

**Open cabinet doors** under sinks on exterior walls — kitchen, bathrooms, laundry. This lets warm room air circulate around the pipes instead of trapping them in a cold box. It looks odd; do it anyway.

**Keep interior doors open** so warm air reaches every room with plumbing. Close off only genuinely unplumbed spaces.

**Do not close heating vents in unused rooms.** Closing vents does not save meaningful energy with a modern system, and it creates the cold pockets where pipes freeze. Every room with a pipe needs its share of heat.

**Replace a dirty furnace filter before you leave.** A clogged filter strains the furnace, and a furnace that fails on day three of a two-week trip is how disasters start. If the furnace has been acting up — short cycling, strange noises — get a tune-up first ([warning signs here](/posts/why-furnace-keeps-turning-on-and-off/)).

## Layer 2: Shut Off the Water (the Highest-Leverage Move)

Here is the key insight most travelers miss: **a frozen pipe only floods your house if water pressure is behind it.** Shut off the main, and even a freeze becomes a minor repair instead of a catastrophe.

**Find your main shutoff now, not the night before.** It is usually where the water line enters the house — basement wall, crawl space, utility closet, or near the water meter. The valve is either a wheel (gate valve, turn clockwise) or a lever (ball valve, quarter-turn perpendicular to the pipe). If you have never touched it in years, test it gently — old gate valves can seize or break. If it will not move, call a plumber before your trip, not after.

**For trips over a week, shut the main and drain the system.** After closing the main, open the lowest faucet in the house (often a basement laundry tap or outdoor hose bib) and the highest faucet to let air in, draining standing water from the lines. Flush toilets to empty tanks (not bowls — the bowl water keeps sewer gas out).

**Consider individual shutoffs for shorter trips.** Washing machine valves, in particular, should be closed for any trip — burst washer hoses are a top-five home flooding cause, and rubber hoses age badly. If your washer hoses are plain rubber and over 5 years old, upgrade to braided stainless steel ($15–$25) regardless of travel plans.

**What about the water heater?** For trips of a week or more, set it to "vacation" mode (or pilot/low) rather than shutting it off entirely — a drained-and-off heater in a cold house risks its own freeze damage, and relighting has its own pitfalls. For trips over a month, the [vacation-home winterization](/posts/how-to-winterize-vacation-home/) playbook covers full drain-down.

**Well water systems:** shut off the well pump breaker too, and drain the pressure tank. A pump that runs dry against a frozen line can burn out — a $500–$1,500 replacement on top of everything else.

**Sprinkler systems:** if not already winterized, shut off the sprinkler main and drain the lines. Frozen backflow preventers crack expensively.

## Layer 3: Protect the Vulnerable Spots

Every house has freeze-prone plumbing. Walk yours before you leave:

**Exterior-wall plumbing.** Kitchen sinks on outside walls are the classic victim. Beyond opening cabinets, consider foam pipe insulation on exposed runs ($3–$8 per 6-foot tube at any hardware store) — it is cheap, tool-free, and effective for the marginal cases.

**Crawl spaces and unheated basements.** Pipes here get almost no benefit from your thermostat. Insulate exposed pipes, seal crawl-space vents for winter (if your climate and code allow), and consider heat tape (self-regulating electric cable, $25–$60 per run) on the most exposed sections — plugged into a GFCI outlet.

**Outdoor hose bibs.** Disconnect all hoses — a connected hose traps water in the bib and the wall pipe behind it, the single most common freeze point in many homes. Shut off the interior valve to each bib if you have one, then open the outside bib to drain.

**Garage plumbing.** Laundry or utility sinks in unheated garages are vulnerable. Insulate, and if the garage has its own shutoff, consider closing it and draining that branch.

**Seal the drafts.** Cold air pouring through gaps around pipe penetrations (where pipes pass through exterior walls or the sill plate) super-chills the pipe. A $6 can of expanding foam or a tube of caulk on those gaps is one of the highest-ROI winter tasks there is.

## Layer 4: Watch the House While You Are Gone

**A human checker is the gold standard.** Ask a trusted neighbor or friend to walk through every few days — run each tap briefly, check under sinks, glance at the water heater and basement. Leave them the plumber's number, the main shutoff location (show them, do not just describe it), and your itinerary.

**Smart water sensors are cheap insurance.** Wi-Fi leak detectors ($25–$50 each) placed under sinks, by the water heater, behind the washing machine, and in the basement alert your phone the moment they touch water. For the price of a dinner out, you buy the early warning that turns a flood into a mop-up.

**Automatic shutoff valves** ($400–$800 plus installation) take it further: when a sensor detects water, the valve closes the main by itself. For frequent travelers or second homes, this is the set-and-forget answer.

**Temperature sensors** in the coldest spots (basement, crawl space, far bathroom) alert you if the house drops toward freezing — catching furnace failures before pipes do. Many smart thermostats include this; standalone sensors are $20–$40.

## If You Come Home to Frozen Pipes

First, the most important rule: **a frozen pipe has not necessarily burst — yet.** Most bursts happen during thawing, when pressure spikes. What you do in the next hour matters enormously.

1. **Keep the faucet open.** Open the tap the frozen pipe feeds. As ice melts, water and steam need somewhere to go — an open faucet relieves the pressure that causes bursts.
2. **Warm the pipe gently.** A hair dryer on low, heating pad, or towels soaked in hot water, working from the faucet end back toward the blockage. Never an open flame, heat gun, or torch — fire risk and cracked pipes.
3. **Check for leaks as it thaws.** Watch every joint and the full run. If water appears where it should not, shut the main immediately — the pipe has burst.
4. **Do not know where the freeze is?** Feel along exposed pipes for the intensely cold section, and check the usual suspects: exterior walls, crawl spaces, near the main entry point.
5. **When in doubt, call a plumber.** Thawing the wrong way bursts pipes; a pro with a pipe-thawing machine does it safely ($150–$400 for a service call — cheap against a flood).

## The Night-Before Checklist

- [ ] Thermostat set to 55°F+ (60°F+ if extreme cold forecast), vacation mode engaged
- [ ] Cabinet doors open under exterior-wall sinks
- [ ] Main water shutoff closed (for trips over a week); system drained via lowest faucet
- [ ] Washing machine valves closed
- [ ] Water heater on vacation mode
- [ ] Outdoor hoses disconnected; hose bibs drained
- [ ] Furnace filter checked; vents open in all plumbed rooms
- [ ] Leak sensors active and tested; temperature alerts set
- [ ] House-checker briefed: shutoff location, plumber number, itinerary
- [ ] Someone knows you are gone and when you return

## What Does It Cost?

**The basics are nearly free:** adjusting the thermostat, opening cabinets, shutting the main valve, and disconnecting hoses cost $0. Foam pipe insulation for the vulnerable runs is $15–$40 in materials.

**Smart protection is cheap:** Wi-Fi leak sensors $25–$50 each (3–4 covers a house), braided washer hoses $15–$25, heat tape $25–$60 per run.

**The disaster is not:** burst-pipe water damage averages $5,000–$15,000, and that is for a discovered-quickly incident. A pipe that runs for a week while you are away can total tens of thousands — floors, drywall, mold remediation, the works.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should I leave a faucet dripping while I am away?**
A slight drip (pencil-lead stream) on the faucet farthest from the main does help — moving water resists freezing. But it is a backup, not a plan: it wastes water, can freeze the drain in extreme cold, and does nothing if the furnace fails. Use it alongside the thermostat and shutoff steps, not instead of them.

**Is shutting off the main water really necessary for a long weekend?**
For 2–3 days with the heat at 60°F and no extreme cold forecast, the risk is low and many people skip it. For a week or more, shutting the main is the single best move you can make — it converts any freeze from a flood into a repair.

**Will my pipes freeze at 55°F?**
In a reasonably insulated home, no — 55°F at the thermostat keeps wall cavities above freezing in normal winter weather. The exceptions are extreme cold snaps (below ~0°F), uninsulated crawl spaces, and pipes on exterior walls with no insulation — those need the extra measures above.

**Do I need to drain the water heater?**
For typical 1–3 week trips, no — vacation mode is enough. Draining is for seasonal shutdowns (a month or more), and an improperly drained heater causes its own problems. When in doubt, vacation mode plus the main shutoff covers you.

**What temperature should the heat be if I have a well?**
Same 55°F minimum, plus shut off the well pump breaker and drain the pressure tank. A pump running against a frozen line burns out fast, adding a $500–$1,500 pump replacement to your problems.

**Can pipes freeze in the summer while I am away?**
Essentially never in a lived-in home — but air conditioning set too low in a humid climate can cause condensation issues on cold pipes. The winter playbook above is the one that matters.

## When to Call a Pro

Call a plumber **before** your trip if the **main shutoff valve is seized or leaks** when tested (a valve that will not close is an emergency waiting to happen), you have **a history of frozen pipes** (a pro can identify and fix the vulnerable runs with insulation or rerouting), or you want an **automatic shutoff valve installed** ($400–$800 installed — worth it for frequent travelers). Call **during** thawing if you **find a burst pipe** (shut the main, then call — do not try to solder a repair yourself on a pressurized line) or **cannot locate the freeze** (pro thawing equipment is faster and safer than guesswork).

Heat on, water off, sensors watching, human checking — that is the complete system. It takes an hour to set up and it protects against the single most expensive thing that can happen to an empty house in winter. Travel with the confidence that the only surprise waiting at home is how much you missed it.
