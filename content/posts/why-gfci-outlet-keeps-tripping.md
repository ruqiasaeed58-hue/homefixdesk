A GFCI that keeps tripping is doing its job — detecting a ground fault, a leak of current that could shock you. Unplug everything on that circuit, let any moisture dry out, press RESET firmly, then plug items back in one at a time until the trip returns. The item that causes the trip is the culprit. If it won't reset with nothing plugged in, the outlet itself has likely worn out.

One clarification: **a GFCI does not protect against overloads** — that's the circuit breaker's job. If a space heater and hair dryer on the same circuit kill the power, that's a breaker trip, not a GFCI trip. A GFCI watches for current leaking out of the normal hot-to-neutral path — as little as 4–6 milliamps — and cuts power in about 1/40th of a second. It protects people, not wiring.

## Why GFCI outlets trip: the usual causes

**Moisture is the #1 cause, especially in fall.** Outdoor outlets, and bathroom or kitchen outlets near sinks, trip after rain, splashing, or condensation — water in the box creates a leakage path to ground, exactly what the GFCI is designed to detect. Outdoor boxes with cracked gaskets or missing in-use "bubble" covers soak up every storm. If your outdoor GFCI started tripping in October, suspect moisture first.

**A failing appliance with a ground fault.** Any corded appliance can develop leakage as it ages — cracked insulation, moisture inside the motor housing, worn heating elements. The classic offenders: old coffee makers, space heaters, dehumidifiers, bathroom heaters, outdoor power tools, and pool pumps. The GFCI trips when you plug in the guilty appliance because current is leaking from its internals to its metal case or the ground wire.

**The outlet itself has worn out.** GFCI internals — sensing electronics and the trip mechanism — wear with age and use; typical useful life is about **10 years**. An older GFCI that trips randomly with nothing plugged in, or won't reset at all, has usually reached end of life. Newer (post-2015) self-testing models permanently disable themselves when their own protection fails, which shows up as "won't reset."

**Downstream wiring problems.** A single GFCI outlet can protect every outlet downstream of it on the same circuit — that's the LOAD side of the device. So a ground fault in an ordinary downstream outlet, a junction box, or a light fixture on that circuit will trip the GFCI even though nothing is plugged into it directly. This is the confusing case: the tripping outlet looks fine, but the fault is somewhere else on the circuit.

**Shared neutral or miswired circuits.** Less common in homes that haven't been modified, but miswiring (especially swapped LINE/LOAD terminals or a shared neutral across circuits) causes chronic nuisance tripping. If a GFCI has tripped since the day it was installed, miswiring is likely.

For a related electrical symptom with different causes — a buzzing or humming switch — see [Why Does My Light Switch Buzz?](/posts/why-light-switch-buzzing/).

## The safe diagnostic ladder (do this in order)

Work top to bottom. Stop and call an electrician if you ever reach the hard-stop conditions below.

1. **Unplug everything.** Remove every plug from the tripping GFCI and from all other outlets on that same circuit (in bathrooms, the other bathroom outlets are usually on the same GFCI; in kitchens, nearby counter outlets; outdoors, every receptacle on that run).
2. **Inspect for moisture.** Look into the outlet box for condensation, water staining, or wet debris. If it's wet, **leave it unplugged and let it dry completely** — 24 hours minimum. Do not use a hair dryer to speed this up; heat can damage the outlet and force moisture deeper into the device.
3. **Press RESET firmly.** With everything unplugged and the box dry, push the RESET button until it clicks and holds. If it won't hold, press TEST first (it should trip with a click), then RESET. An outlet that refuses to reset with zero load has failed internally.
4. **Plug items back one at a time.** Reconnect each appliance individually and give it 30–60 seconds before adding the next. When the GFCI trips, the last item you added has the ground fault — unplug it, reset, and confirm everything else stays on.
5. **Test the suspect appliance elsewhere.** Plug the suspect item into a non-GFCI outlet (or a different GFCI circuit) to confirm it trips that one too. If it does, the appliance is faulty: repair or replace it. If it doesn't trip anywhere else, the original GFCI outlet may be overly sensitive or worn out.
6. **Map the circuit.** If the GFCI trips with nothing plugged in anywhere, check every downstream outlet and fixture for moisture, damage, or new wiring. Most homeowners should hand off here — opening boxes on a live-tested circuit is real electrical work.

## Hard stop: call an electrician immediately

**Stop resetting and call a licensed electrician if any of these are true:**

- **The cover plate is warm to the touch.** Warmth means heat is being generated inside the box — a loose connection or failing device. Do not keep resetting it; turn the circuit off at the breaker panel.
- **You smell burning, hot plastic, or see scorch marks.** This is an active electrical fault. Turn the circuit off at the panel and call now.
- **The outlet crackles, buzzes, or sparks when reset.**
- **Water is actively dripping into the box or the area is flooded.** Turn off the breaker first, then call.
- **You need to open the electrical panel or outlet boxes to continue diagnosis.** If the ladder above didn't find the fault, the problem is in the wiring, and that's electrician territory.

Repeatedly resetting a tripping GFCI without finding the cause doesn't fix anything — it just wears out the device and, in the worst case, trains you to ignore a warning that's keeping you safe.

## Tools and materials (if replacing the outlet yourself)

- **New GFCI receptacle** — 15-amp or 20-amp to match the circuit (about $12–$20; WR = weather-resistant for outdoors)
- **Non-contact voltage tester** — to verify power is off ($15–$25; non-negotiable safety gear)
- **Screwdrivers** (flat and Phillips), **wire strippers**
- **Electrical tape** and wire nuts (usually included with the outlet)
- **Outdoor "in-use" bubble cover** if replacing an exterior outlet (~$10) — this is the single best upgrade against rain-driven tripping

Replacing a GFCI is a moderate DIY job: turn off the breaker, verify dead with the tester, photograph the existing wiring, transfer wires one at a time to matching terminals (LINE from the panel, LOAD to downstream outlets — mixing these up is the #1 DIY mistake), then test with TEST/RESET. If your panel isn't labeled or you're unsure which wire is LINE, that's a $150–$250 electrician visit well spent.

## Safety warnings

**A GFCI protects people, not equipment — and it only works if it's functioning.** Press the TEST button monthly; it should trip with an audible click, and RESET should restore power. A GFCI whose TEST button does nothing is a dead device that looks alive — replace it.

**Never bypass, remove, or wire around a GFCI to stop nuisance tripping.** Downgrading protection to fix an annoyance trades a tripped outlet for a shock hazard. The code requires GFCIs in bathrooms, kitchens, garages, outdoors, and near water for a reason: these are the places people die from ground faults.

**Water and electricity: breakers first.** If an outlet box is wet, turn off the circuit breaker before touching anything, and keep it off until the box is fully dry. Standing water near a live outlet is a lethal combination — do not stand in water while working on electrical anything.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does my GFCI trip when it rains?**
Rainwater enters outdoor boxes through cracked gaskets, missing covers, or damaged conduits, creating a leakage path to ground. Fix: dry the box completely, replace the gasket, and install an in-use "bubble" cover that protects the outlet while cords are plugged in. If tripping continues in dry weather, the internals may be corroded — replace the outlet.

**My GFCI won't reset — is it dead?**
Probably, if nothing is plugged in and it's dry. First confirm the circuit has power — check the breaker, and press TEST before RESET. A GFCI that won't reset with zero load, a good breaker, and a dry box has failed internally (~10-year typical life) — replace it. If the new one won't reset either, the fault is in the downstream wiring: call an electrician.

**Can I replace a GFCI with a regular outlet?**
No — not legally, and not safely. Electrical code requires GFCI protection in bathrooms, kitchens, garages, unfinished basements, and outdoors. Removing it leaves the shock hazard the GFCI was protecting against. If an outlet keeps tripping, fix the cause; don't delete the protection.

**What's the difference between a GFCI trip and a breaker trip?**
A GFCI trip means current leaked to ground — a shock hazard — and the outlet cuts its own power. A breaker trip means too much current flowed (overload) or a dead short, and the whole-circuit breaker opens. Space heater killing the room's power: breaker. Damp appliance killing just that outlet: GFCI.

**Do GFCIs wear out even if they rarely trip?**
Yes. The internal electronics and trip mechanism degrade with age, heat, and moisture — ten years is a reasonable replacement horizon, sooner outdoors or in garages. Newer self-testing models permanently lock out when their protection circuit fails, which is why an old outlet can silently stop protecting while still delivering power.

**Why does my bathroom GFCI trip the outlets in the other bathroom?**
That's by design. One GFCI device protects everything wired to its LOAD terminals — commonly all the bathroom receptacles on the same circuit. It's cheaper and code-compliant. When it trips, check appliances in every bathroom on that circuit, not just the one with the buttons.

## Prevention tips

- **Test monthly.** The TEST button takes five seconds and is the only way to know the device still works.
- **Install in-use bubble covers outdoors.** Rain-driven nuisance tripping drops to near zero with proper covers.
- **Replace aging GFCIs proactively.** If yours are 10+ years old, swap them during the next round of electrical maintenance rather than waiting for failures.
- **Keep outdoor boxes sealed.** Replace cracked gaskets and damaged covers before fall rain season.
- **Retire failing appliances.** An appliance that trips GFCIs has a ground fault — repair it or replace it, don't just move it to a non-GFCI outlet.

## When to call a pro

- Burning smell, scorch marks, or a warm cover plate — turn off the breaker and call immediately
- The GFCI trips with nothing plugged in and the box is dry — likely a downstream wiring fault
- A new GFCI won't reset either — wiring problem, not a device problem
- You find aluminum wiring, knob-and-tube, or ungrounded circuits while investigating
- The circuit's breaker is unlabeled and you can't confidently identify it
- You need new GFCI locations added (garage, outdoor, basement) — new wiring runs belong to a licensed electrician
- Any point where the diagnostic ladder asks you to open boxes and you're not confident — an hour of an electrician's time beats a hospital visit
