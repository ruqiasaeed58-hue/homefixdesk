A loud **bang or boom when your furnace starts up** is most often delayed ignition: gas flows into the combustion chamber but doesn't light right away, so unburned gas builds up and ignites all at once. A sharp bang every cycle needs a service call soon, before the repeated shockwaves crack the heat exchanger — and a cracked one can leak carbon monoxide (CO) into your home.

Not every bang is dangerous, though. Ductwork **popping** as it expands is usually harmless, while rattling is often just a loose access panel. This guide covers the six noises, what each means, the delayed-ignition problem, the CO risk from a cracked heat exchanger, what you can fix yourself, and the fall maintenance that keeps bangs away.

## The 6 furnace noises and what each one means

Note *when* the noise happens — timing tells you which of these six it is.

### 1. Bang or boom on startup = delayed ignition (the scary one)

One loud **bang a few seconds after the burners should have lit** is the hallmark of delayed ignition. Gas enters the combustion chamber but the ignition is late, so a pocket of unburned gas accumulates and ignites all at once. The repeated pressure waves hammer the burners, the ignition assembly, and the heat exchanger. This always deserves a technician's attention.

### 2. Popping or clanking from the ducts = expansion and contraction

If the noise comes from the **ductwork** (a tick-tick-tick or pop-pop-pop starting a minute or two after the burners fire), that's sheet metal expanding with heat and contracting as it cools. Noisy but usually harmless — thin ductwork flexing against its hangers. More common in older homes and in systems where ducts run through cold crawlspaces.

### 3. Squealing or screeching = blower belt or bearings

A high-pitched **squeal** when the blower runs points to the belt on older belt-driven blowers (slipping or frayed) or failing blower motor bearings on direct-drive units. Screeching bearings are on borrowed time — when they seize, the blower stops and the furnace shuts down on its high-limit safety switch. Budget **$150–$400** for a belt or **$400–$800** for a blower motor, parts and labor.

### 4. Rattling = loose panel (usually) vs. something worse

A **rattle** is most often the simplest fix on this list: a loose blower-compartment access panel or a screw that worked free. But if the rattle persists with panels tight, or it's metallic and coming from *inside* the combustion area, it can be a loose burner or a failing motor mount — those need a tech.

### 5. Clicking repeatedly without ignition = ignition failure

A **click-click-click** or a single click followed by nothing means the furnace is trying to light and can't. After a few failed attempts, modern furnaces lock out. This is an ignition-component problem — igniter, flame sensor, or gas valve — and a call-a-pro situation.

### 6. Rumbling or loud booming throughout the cycle = undersized or dirty burners

A low, ongoing **rumble** while the burners fire can come from burners that need cleaning, a sooting combustion chamber, or misadjusted manifold pressure. It wastes fuel and overheats parts of the furnace that shouldn't be hot. Schedule service — it's not urgent tonight, but it shouldn't wait the season.

## Delayed ignition, explained

In a healthy furnace, the sequence is fast: thermostat calls for heat, the **inducer fan** spins up, the gas valve opens, and the **hot-surface igniter** lights the gas within about a second. Delayed ignition happens when any link lags: gas pours in, the flame appears seconds late, and the accumulated fuel-rich pocket burns all at once with a **bang**.

The usual causes, in rough order of likelihood:

- **Dirty or corroded burners.** Gas ports get partially blocked by rust and scale, so the flame can't travel from the ignition point across all the burners promptly. This is the number-one cause in furnaces that have skipped annual service. Noticed a dusty burn-off smell when the heat kicked on? [Why Does My Furnace Smell When I Turn It On for the First Time in Fall?](/posts/why-furnace-smells-first-heat-fall/) explains when that smell is normal — dirty burners and that smell often go together.
- **A failing igniter or weak spark.** The ignition component takes longer to heat up or spark, giving gas a head start.
- **Delayed gas-valve opening or low gas pressure.** Less common, but a sluggish valve or misadjusted manifold pressure creates the same fuel-rich buildup.
- **A failing flame sensor.** A sensor that can't confirm the flame reliably contributes to repeated failed starts, each dumping more gas.

Why it matters beyond the noise: every delayed ignition sends a pressure wave through the combustion chamber. The heat exchanger — the thin steel box carrying exhaust gases — flexes with each shock. Repeated flexing over hundreds of cycles fatigues the metal until it **cracks**.

## Why banging can crack the heat exchanger — and why that means CO

The heat exchanger separates combustion gases (including **carbon monoxide**) from the air blown into your ductwork. Exhaust goes up the flue; house air passes over the outside of the exchanger. A crack lets the blower pull CO-laden combustion gases into the supply air — and because CO is colorless and odorless, you won't know until symptoms appear.

**This is why delayed ignition is a red-flag issue, not a "get to it someday" one.** A furnace that bangs every startup should be serviced within days, not months. These hard red-flag lines mean *tonight*, not next week:

- **You smell gas around the furnace.** Don't touch the furnace or any switches, leave the house, and call your gas company or 911 from outside. Never try to diagnose gas odor yourself.
- **Your CO detector alarms.** Get everyone out, call 911 from outside, and have the furnace inspected before it runs again — even if the alarm stops.
- **The bang is getting louder or more frequent**, or you see soot around the burner compartment or scorch marks on the cabinet. Turn the furnace off at the thermostat and service switch and call an HVAC pro immediately.
- **Anyone in the home has flu-like symptoms** (headache, nausea, dizziness) that improve when leaving the house, especially in heating season. Leave, get checked, and don't run the furnace until it's inspected.

A cracked heat exchanger is effectively a furnace replacement conversation: swapping one on an old unit runs **$1,000–$2,500** in parts and labor, and on furnaces past ~15 years most techs recommend a full replacement (**$3,500–$7,500**) instead. Catching delayed ignition early — burner cleaning and an igniter replacement, typically **$150–$350** — is dramatically cheaper.

## DIY-safe fixes vs. call-a-pro problems

Everything **outside the combustion chamber** is fair game for a careful homeowner. Everything involving **gas, burners, ignition, or the heat exchanger** belongs to a licensed HVAC technician.

**Safe to do yourself:**

- **Tighten access panels and screws.** Kill power at the red service switch first, then snug up every panel, the filter door, and visible screws in the blower compartment. Half of all furnace rattles die right there.
- **[How Do I Replace My Furnace Filter?](/posts/how-to-replace-furnace-filter/)** — a clogged filter is one of the most common causes of overheating-related noises, and it takes five minutes. Replace it before heating season and every 60–90 days.
- **Listen and locate.** Note exactly when the noise happens — startup bang, duct popping a minute later, or constant rattle. That saves the tech time and you money.
- **Install or check CO detectors.** Every home with a gas furnace needs one on each floor and near the furnace room. Replace detectors every 7–10 years.

**Call an HVAC pro:**

- Delayed ignition (startup bang) — burners, igniter, gas valve, flame sensor
- Squealing blower motor bearings
- Ignition lockouts and repeated failed starts
- Any gas odor — skip the furnace tech and call the gas company first


## Fall maintenance that prevents banging

Most startup bangs are a deferred-maintenance bill coming due. Do this every autumn and you'll almost never hear one:

1. **Replace the filter** before the first sustained heating run. The filter that sat through a dusty summer is loaded — restricted airflow overheats the heat exchanger.
2. **Book a professional tune-up.** A tech cleans the burners, checks the igniter and flame sensor, tests the gas valve and manifold pressure, inspects the heat exchanger, and checks flue draft. Typical cost: **$80–$150**, or **$150–$300/year** for a maintenance plan with spring and fall visits — the cheapest insurance against the $1,000+ problems above.
3. **Watch the first cold-weather start.** One soft ignition "whoosh" is normal; a bang is your cue to call before the next cycle.
4. **Check the flue and combustion air.** Make sure the exhaust vent is clear (no nests, no crushed sections) and combustion-air openings aren't blocked — poor draft contributes to ignition problems.

## Safety warnings

**Never ignore a startup bang.** Each event flexes the heat exchanger, and a cracked one can leak carbon monoxide into your home's air. Schedule service within days.

**Smell gas? Don't investigate.** Don't touch switches or use phones inside, get out, and call the gas company or 911 from outside the house.

**CO detectors are mandatory, not optional.** Test them monthly in heating season. If one alarms, treat it as real: leave the house and call for help from outside.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a banging furnace dangerous?**
A startup bang from delayed ignition is a real hazard, not a cosmetic annoyance. Each event flexes the heat exchanger, and a cracked one can leak carbon monoxide into your home's air. Ductwork popping, by contrast, is noisy but harmless. Timing is the key: bang *at ignition* = dangerous; pops *a minute after startup* = expansion noise.

**Can I just live with the banging noise?**
No. Delayed ignition worsens as burners get dirtier and the igniter gets weaker — the bangs get louder and the chance of a cracked heat exchanger grows with every cycle. A $150–$350 burner-and-igniter service now beats a $3,500+ furnace replacement later.

**Why does my furnace only bang on the first start of the day?**
The first start is the hardest: everything is stone cold and the igniter heats from room temperature. Slight ignition delays that are silent on warm starts can bang on cold ones. Cold starts don't excuse it — they reveal it earlier.

**Does duct popping mean my ducts are failing?**
Usually not. Popping is thin sheet metal flexing with temperature changes — annoying but not destructive. Worry only if it's accompanied by airflow loss or visible damage.

**How fast do I need to act on a startup bang?**
Within days. A furnace that bangs every cycle is actively hammering its heat exchanger. Don't let it run a whole season like that. If there's also a gas smell, visible soot, or a CO alarm, act tonight.

**Will a tune-up fix delayed ignition?**
Often, yes. Burner cleaning plus an igniter or flame-sensor replacement resolves most cases — that's the core of a fall tune-up. If the tech finds a cracked heat exchanger or failing gas valve, the repair is bigger, but catching it during maintenance beats a January no-heat emergency.

## Prevention tips

- **Replace the filter every 60–90 days** in heating season — restricted airflow overheats everything
- **Get a fall tune-up every year** — burner cleaning and ignition checks catch delayed ignition before it starts
- **Listen to the first cycle each fall** and call at the first bang, not the fiftieth
- **Test CO detectors monthly** during heating season and replace detectors older than 7–10 years

## When to call a pro

- A loud bang or boom at startup, every time or most times — likely delayed ignition
- Gas odor near the furnace — call the gas company first, then HVAC
- CO detector alarm or flu-like symptoms in the household during heating season
- Visible soot, scorch marks, or flame outside the burner area
- A metallic rattle from the combustion area with panels tight — possible cracked exchanger or loose burner
- Repeated ignition lockouts, or clicking without lighting
- Squealing blower bearings that tightening panels didn't fix
