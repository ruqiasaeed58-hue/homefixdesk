Bathroom exhaust fans get louder with age — but "louder than it used to be" is a symptom, not a life sentence. The noise almost always comes from one of three places: dust caked on the fan wheel throwing it off balance, a worn-out motor bearing, or a housing that has worked loose. Most fixes take under an hour.

## Why fans get louder with age (the three culprits)

Fan noise increases for physical reasons, not mysterious ones:

1. **Unbalanced wheel:** dust accumulates unevenly on the fan wheel. Even a gram of extra weight on one side creates vibration that grows with RPM — the same physics as an unbalanced ceiling fan or car tire.
2. **Worn bearings:** the motor's bearings lose lubrication and develop play over thousands of hours. Play means the shaft wobbles microscopically, producing grinding or whining.
3. **Loosened mounting:** years of vibration back out screws and fatigue spring clips. A housing that was solid now has micro-gaps that rattle at fan frequency.

Cleaning addresses #1, tightening addresses #3, and only motor replacement addresses #2. Diagnose by noise type (below) to avoid replacing a motor that just needs cleaning.

## Quick diagnosis: what kind of noise?

- **Gradually got louder over years** → dust buildup and motor wear. See Steps 1–2.
- **Rattling or vibrating** → loose cover, loose housing screws, or a loose duct connection. See Step 3.
- **Grinding or squealing** → failing motor bearings. The motor needs replacement. See Step 4.
- **Loud from day one** → it is a cheap, high-sone builder fan. Replacement with a quiet model is the real fix. See Step 5.

## Safety first

- **Turn off the breaker** for the bathroom circuit before removing the cover or touching the motor. The wall switch is not enough — someone could flip it while you work.
- Let the fan cool down if it has been running; motors get hot.

## Step 1: Deep-clean the fan

Dust is the #1 noise amplifier. A dust-caked fan wheel is unbalanced (like a wobbling ceiling fan) and has to work harder, so it gets louder.

1. **Remove the cover.** Most covers pull straight down — squeeze the spring clips or slide the mounting wires. Some have a small screw.
2. **Vacuum everything** — the fan wheel, the housing interior, the motor. Use the brush attachment.
3. **Wash the cover** in warm soapy water, dry completely, and set aside.
4. **Wipe the fan blades** with a damp cloth. Get the leading edges where dust cakes thickest.
5. Reattach the cover and test. Cleaning alone fixes a surprising number of "loud fan" complaints.

## Step 2: Lubricate (older motors only)

Some older fan motors have oil ports or accessible bearings.

1. With the cover off and power OFF, spin the fan wheel by hand. It should spin freely and coast a bit. If it stops immediately or feels gritty, the bearings are dry or worn.
2. If the motor has small oil caps or ports, add 2–3 drops of electric motor oil (not WD-40 — it evaporates).
3. Spin by hand to work the oil in.

If there are no oil ports (most modern motors are sealed), skip to Step 4 — sealed bearings cannot be serviced.

## Step 3: Tighten everything

Vibration loosens parts over time, and loose parts rattle.

1. **Housing screws:** tighten the screws holding the metal housing to the ceiling joist.
2. **Motor mounting:** the motor/blower assembly usually attaches with a screw or plug-in bracket — make sure it is seated firmly.
3. **Duct connection:** reach into the housing and check where the duct connects. A loose duct rattles and leaks moist air into the attic. Tighten the clamp or re-tape with foil duct tape (not cloth duct tape, which fails in heat).
4. **Cover fit:** a cover that does not sit flush vibrates. Make sure the spring clips engage properly.

## Step 4: Replace the motor assembly

If the fan still grinds or squeals after cleaning, the bearings are shot. You do not need to replace the whole fan — just the motor/blower unit.

1. **Find the model number** on the housing or the old motor. Universal replacement motors exist, but matching the brand (Broan, Nutone, Panasonic) makes it plug-and-play.
2. With the breaker OFF, unplug the motor (most are plug-connected, no wiring needed) and remove its mounting screw or bracket.
3. Install the new motor assembly ($20–$50), reconnect, and test before replacing the cover.

This is a 20-minute job and dramatically quieter than the worn motor.

## Step 5: Upgrade to a quiet fan

If the fan was always loud, it is probably a cheap builder-grade unit rated at 3–4 sones (a sone is a loudness unit — lower is quieter). Modern fans run at 0.3–1.0 sones — essentially a whisper.

1. **Pick a replacement** sized to your bathroom: you need about 1 CFM per square foot (a 80 sq ft bathroom needs an 80 CFM fan). Look for ≤1.0 sones.
2. Many retrofit fans fit the existing housing — no drywall work needed.
3. Full replacement (new housing) means attic access and some wiring — a handyman or electrician job ($150–$300).

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Working with just the switch off.** Always kill the breaker. Bathroom circuits are often shared and mislabeled.
- **Using WD-40 on motor bearings.** It is a solvent, not a lubricant — it strips existing grease and evaporates. Use electric motor oil.
- **Venting into the attic.** If you find the duct just ends in the attic (common in older homes), that is a mold factory. Route it outside through the roof or soffit.
- **Buying the loudest cheap fan.** Check the sone rating before buying — anything above 1.5 sones will annoy you daily.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should a bathroom exhaust fan last?**
About 10 years for builder-grade fans; 15–20 for quality models. If yours is over a decade old and noisy, replacement usually beats repair.

**Why does my fan get louder in winter?**
Cold, dry air makes bearings noisier and dust drier. Also, the damper flapper in the duct can stick in cold weather and rattle.

**Should the fan run after I shower?**
Yes — run it during the shower and for 20–30 minutes after. Moisture left in the air condenses on walls and feeds mold. A timer switch ($20) automates this.

**Can a loud fan cause damage?**
The noise itself is just annoying, but the underlying causes matter: a dust-choked fan moves less air (mold risk), and a disconnected duct dumps moisture into the attic (rot risk).

**What do sone ratings actually mean?**
The sone scale is linear — 2 sones sounds twice as loud as 1 sone. A 4-sone builder fan sounds roughly 8x louder than a 0.5-sone premium fan. That is why the upgrade feels so dramatic: it is not a small improvement, it is an order-of-magnitude difference.

**Why does my new quiet fan barely seem to move air?**
Check the duct — a crushed, kinked, or overlong duct run chokes even a good fan. Also verify the exterior vent cap opens freely. A premium fan on a bad duct performs like a cheap fan.

## Maintenance schedule (keeps it quiet)

- **Every 6 months:** vacuum the cover and fan wheel. Takes 10 minutes, prevents the dust-unbalance cycle.
- **Annually:** check the duct connection and exterior vent cap. Clear any lint, nests, or stuck dampers.
- **Every 2–3 years:** if the motor has oil ports, add a few drops of electric motor oil.
- **At 10 years:** budget for replacement. Even well-maintained builder fans wear out — plan the upgrade on your terms, not as an emergency.

## When to call a pro

- There is no duct, or the duct vents into the attic — an HVAC contractor or handyman should route it outside properly.
- The wiring looks damaged, scorched, or aluminum — electrician territory.
- You want a full new-housing install and lack attic access or electrical comfort — $150–$300 for a pro install.
- Mold is visible inside the housing or on the surrounding ceiling — address the moisture source and consider mold remediation for large areas.
