The snow is coming down, the driveway is filling up, and your snow blower just cranks — or worse, does nothing at all. Before you call a repair shop, work through this checklist. Most no-start snow blowers are fixed by one of five things: the **fuel valve is off**, the **gasoline is stale**, the **choke is in the wrong position**, the **spark plug is fouled**, or the **primer bulb needs more presses**. All are free or nearly free to check and fix in your own garage.

This guide starts with the 60-second checks, moves into the most common culprit — bad fuel — then covers spark plugs, primer and choke technique, carburetor clogs, electric and battery models, and how to store the machine so this never happens again.

## The 60-Second Checks (Do These First)

Every year, repair shops charge $100 for a "repair" that turns out to be one of these:

1. **Fuel valve:** On the side of the engine, between the tank and carburetor, there's a small lever or petcock. Make sure it's ON. Many owners close it at the end of the season and forget by November. If it's off, nothing else you do matters.
2. **The key:** Most gas blowers have a plastic safety key that must be fully inserted in its slot. Kids, moves, and garage cleanouts relocate these constantly. Check that it's in — fully, not hanging halfway out.
3. **Throttle and kill switch:** Some models have a kill switch that must be in RUN, and the throttle usually needs to be at FAST for starting.
4. **Choke position:** Cold engine = choke FULL/CLOSED. Warm engine = choke open. If you tried starting a cold engine with no choke, that's likely your whole problem.
5. **Electric start (if equipped):** Cord firmly plugged in at both ends, outlet has power. Try a different outlet.

If all five check out and it still won't start, keep going.

## Stale Fuel: The #1 Culprit

If a snow blower sits all summer with gasoline in the tank, that fuel goes stale. Gasoline starts degrading in as little as **30 days** — lighter compounds evaporate and the rest forms varnish and gummy deposits that clog the carburetor's tiny passages. E10 fuel (the 10% ethanol blend at nearly every US pump) goes bad faster because ethanol absorbs moisture from the air.

Symptoms: the engine cranks but won't catch, catches briefly and dies, or runs rough and surges. Smell the gas — fresh gasoline smells sharp; stale fuel smells sour, like varnish. If it's been in there since March, assume it's bad.

**The fix:**

1. Turn the fuel valve OFF.
2. Drain the old fuel into an approved container — via the carburetor bowl drain, by siphoning, or by disconnecting the fuel line at the carb. Don't pour it on the ground; take it to municipal hazardous-waste collection or an auto parts store that accepts it.
3. Fill with fresh gasoline — ethanol-free ("rec fuel") if you can find it, standard E10 otherwise.
4. Add **fuel stabilizer** per the label. Stabilizer keeps fuel fresh for 12–24 months and prevents the varnish that caused this mess.
5. Turn the valve back ON, let fresh fuel flow for a minute, and try starting.

**If fresh fuel doesn't fix it**, varnish has likely gummed the carburetor jets — see the carburetor section below. And skip premium gas: regular 87-octane is what these engines are designed for.

## Spark Plug: The Cheap, Easy Swap

The next most common no-start cause, especially on machines that ran poorly at the end of last season. A fouled plug — carbon-coated, fuel-wet, or with a worn electrode — can't make a strong enough spark.

**Checking it:**

1. Pull the spark plug boot off (grab the rubber boot, not the wire).
2. Unscrew the plug with a spark plug socket.
3. Inspect: it should be tan or light brown. Black and sooty = carbon fouling; wet = flooding; oily = engine wear.
4. Check the gap with a feeler gauge against your manual's spec (usually ~0.030").
5. Test for spark: reconnect the boot, hold the plug's threads against bare engine metal, and pull the starter cord. You want a bright blue spark. No spark — or a weak orange one — means the plug or ignition coil is done.

**The fix:** A new plug costs **$3 to $8** and takes five minutes. If the old one looks anything less than clean and tan, just replace it — it's the cheapest maintenance item on the machine. Keep a spare in the garage.

If a new plug still makes no spark, the ignition coil may have failed — a $20–$40 part on most engines, and a moderate DIY job under the engine cover.

## Primer Bulb and Choke: The Right Technique

Cold-starting a small engine is a ritual, and order matters. Wrong order floods the engine; right order usually means a first-or-second-pull start.

**Cold-start routine:**

1. Throttle to FAST.
2. Choke to FULL/CLOSED.
3. Press the primer bulb **3 times**, firmly. On very cold days (below 10°F), 4–5 presses.
4. Pull the starter cord briskly — a full, strong pull, not a half-speed yank.
5. Once it catches, run 5–10 seconds, then **gradually open the choke**. If it sputters and dies, it wasn't warm enough — close the choke and let it run longer.

**If you flooded it** (too much priming or cranking with the choke closed — you'll smell raw gasoline): stop priming, set choke to OPEN, throttle to FAST, and pull several times to clear the excess fuel. It'll usually sputter to life by the third or fourth pull.

Warm engine? **No choke**, maybe one primer press at most. Choking a warm engine is the fastest way to flood it.

## Carburetor Clogs and Quick Cleaning

The carburetor's internal passages are absurdly small — a speck of varnish blocks a jet. If the blower fires on starter fluid or the primer charge and then dies, or only runs with the choke partially closed, the carb is restricted.

**Try this first:** drain the carburetor bowl. There's usually a small drain screw at the bottom. Catch a few ounces of fuel in a rag or container — this flushes water (from condensation and ethanol) and sediment. It fixes a surprising number of no-starts.

**If flushing doesn't help:** remove the bowl (usually one bolt at the bottom) and clean the main jet — the small brass piece the bolt threads through. Poke its hole clear with a fine wire, spray with carburetor cleaner (**~$8 a can**), and reassemble.

**When to just replace it:** If the carb is heavily varnished or corroded, replacement beats a rebuild. Complete aftermarket carbs for common snow blower engines run **$15 to $40** — often cheaper than a rebuild kit plus your time. Usually a direct bolt-on: two mounting nuts and the fuel line.

## Electric and Battery Snow Blowers

No fuel, no plug — but electrics have their own gremlins:

- **Corded models:** Confirm the cord is undamaged and plugged in, and the outlet has power (test with a lamp). Most have a two-stage switch — a button you hold while squeezing the bail. A tripped GFCI on an outdoor outlet is a classic cause.
- **Battery models:** Dead silence usually means the battery. Check it's fully seated and fully charged — lithium packs self-discharge in storage, so one left at half charge since March may be nearly dead. A worn-out pack runs **$80 to $200** to replace.
- **Thermal overload:** Some electrics have a reset button or self-resetting thermal switch. Let it cool 20–30 minutes and retry.
- **Auger jam:** Motor hums or clicks but nothing moves? Something's jammed in the auger or impeller. Disconnect power (unplug or remove the battery), then clear it with a stick — **never your hand**.

If a corded model is completely dead with a good outlet and cord, the switch or motor brushes may have failed — usually a shop job.

## End-of-Season Storage: Start Next Winter Happy

Nearly every November no-start is really an April storage failure. Ten minutes of prep prevents all of it:

- **Stabilize or drain the fuel.** Either run it dry (fuel valve off, run until it dies) or fill the tank and add stabilizer, then run 5 minutes so treated fuel reaches the carb. Full-tank-plus-stabilizer is the better option — less air means less moisture.
- **Change the oil.** Old oil is acidic. Fresh oil sitting in the crankcase all summer protects the internals. A quart costs a few dollars.
- **Replace the spark plug.** Start next season with a fresh one already installed.
- **Grease the auger gearbox and check belts** while you still remember how everything felt last winter.
- **Store it clean and covered.** Brush off salt (it corrodes) and cover it to keep dust out of the carb.

## How Much Does a Snow Blower Repair Cost?

If the checklist doesn't get it running:

- **Tune-up (fuel drain, plug, carb clean, oil):** $75–$125
- **Carburetor replacement:** $50–$100 parts and labor
- **Ignition coil replacement:** $60–$100
- **Belt replacement:** $40–$80

The DIY versions cost a fraction of this, but factor in your time. A machine that's sat for years with compounded problems may be better off at the shop — or, if it's a 15-year-old single-stage needing a carb, coil, and belts, replaced outright.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How many times should I prime a snow blower?**

Three firm presses for a cold start; 4–5 below about 10°F. More risks flooding. A warm engine needs none.

**Can I use starting fluid?**

Yes, sparingly — a one-second shot into the air intake. It's a diagnostic: if it fires on starting fluid but won't stay running, you have a fuel-delivery problem. Don't use it as a crutch; fix the underlying issue.

**Should I run it dry or use stabilizer for storage?**

Either works if done right. Running dry leaves the carb empty; full-tank-plus-stabilizer leaves less room for condensation and keeps seals from drying. Most small-engine pros prefer the stabilizer route. Either beats parking it on untreated half-tank fuel.

**Why does it only run with the choke on?**

The carburetor's main jet is partially clogged — the choke enriches the mixture to compensate. Clean or replace the carb; it's telling you exactly what's wrong.

**My electric blower trips the breaker. Why?**

Usually an overloaded circuit or a partially seized auger making the motor draw too much current. Try a different circuit first, then check for obstructions. If it trips with nothing jammed and no other loads, the motor may be failing.

**Is it worth repairing a 10-year-old snow blower?**

Usually yes, if the engine is sound — plugs, carbs, belts, and coils are cheap, and a solid engine has another decade in it. It stops being worth it when compression is low or the housing is rusted through.

## When to Call a Pro

Most no-starts are DIY-able, but stop here:

- **Suspected engine problems** — low compression, blue smoke, knocking. Internal engine work needs a shop.
- **Rust in the fuel tank.** A rusted tank needs replacement and a full fuel-system flush — a shop does it faster and safer.
- **Electric/battery models with dead motors or controllers.** Once cords, outlets, and batteries are ruled out, internal electrical faults are shop territory.
- **You need it today.** A $75–$125 tune-up is money well spent when the snow is falling and you need the machine now, not next weekend.

Run the 60-second checks, smell the fuel, pull the plug. That triage resolves the overwhelming majority of snow blowers that won't start — and costs nothing but a few minutes in the garage.
