To prepare your home for a winter power outage, build a 72-hour kit now: flashlights and batteries, warm blankets, one gallon of water per person per day, three days of non-perishable food, a battery or hand-crank radio, charged power banks, and a thermometer for the fridge. Seal drafts, test-start your portable generator if you own one, and learn how to release your garage door manually. Ice storms don't give second chances — a few hours of preparation in October is the difference between a cozy inconvenience and a burst-pipe emergency at 2 a.m.

Every winter, ice storms knock out power to millions of American homes, sometimes for days. In summer an outage is annoying; in winter it's dangerous: no heat means pipes freeze, food spoils, and desperate heating improvisations cause fires and CO poisonings every season.

## Before the Storm: Build a 72-Hour Winter Kit

Plan on the assumption that help may take 72 hours to reach you. Pack everything in a clear tote the whole family knows about.

**Heat and shelter.** Your goal is to keep *people* warm, not the whole house. Gather wool or fleece blankets and sleeping bags — one per person, plus spares — and pick one small interior room as your "camp room," closing it off and hanging blankets over doorways to trap warmth. Layer up before you're cold: long underwear, wool socks, hats, gloves.

If anyone in your home depends on electrically powered medical equipment — an oxygen concentrator, CPAP machine, or powered wheelchair — a portable generator or battery power station isn't optional. Plan for it now, not during the storm.

**Food and water.** Three days of food that needs no refrigeration, no cooking, and minimal prep: peanut butter, crackers, canned tuna and beans, dried fruit, nuts, granola bars, plus a manual can opener. Stock one gallon of water per person per day for drinking and basic hygiene — for a family of four over three days, that's 12 gallons. Add pet food and a week's buffer of any prescription medications.

**Light.** Flashlights beat candles for safety, and LED lanterns beat flashlights for filling a room with usable light. Keep a headlamp in the kit — hands-free light turns every task from frustrating to manageable. Stock more batteries than you think you need. Candles are fine as backup, but never leave one burning unattended or while you sleep.

**Communications.** A battery-powered or hand-crank weather radio keeps you informed when cell service falters or your phone dies. Charge power banks fully at the first storm warning, and use low-power mode to stretch the battery. Tell a relative or neighbor your plan: which room you'll shelter in and when you'll check in.

**Fridge and freezer thermometers.** Put an appliance thermometer in the fridge and one in the freezer *now*, while the power's on. When the outage hits, they tell you at a glance whether food is still safe — no guessing, no sniff-tests. They're a few dollars each.

**Household prep checklist.** If you're on a well, fill the bathtub before the storm — no power means no water pump, and you'll want water for flushing toilets. Fill your car's gas tank — the station's pumps run on electricity too. Bump the thermostat up a few degrees ahead of the storm to "pre-heat" the house's thermal mass; every stored degree buys you time. And if you own a portable generator, test-start it today and confirm you have fresh fuel stored safely outdoors — never inside the house or garage.

## Heat Without Power: What's Safe and What Kills

Your furnace needs electricity for its blower and controls, so a winter outage means no central heat even with gas or oil.

**Safe options:** a wood-burning or pellet stove, a properly vented gas fireplace, portable heaters explicitly rated for indoor use (follow the label to the letter), and lots of blankets and warm clothing.

**Deadly options — never do these:**

- **Never use the oven or stove burners to heat your home.** Gas ovens produce carbon monoxide, and running one for hours with the door open is one of the most common winter CO-poisoning scenarios.
- **Never use a charcoal grill or camp stove indoors** — not even in the garage. Charcoal produces lethal amounts of carbon monoxide.
- **Never idle a car in an attached garage** to warm up or charge a phone. Exhaust seeps into the living space even with the garage door open. Pull the car fully out first — or don't run it at all.
- **Never plug a generator into a wall outlet** (backfeeding). It can electrocute utility line workers and fry your home's wiring.

Carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless — **every home needs a working CO detector with battery backup.** If the alarm sounds, get everyone outside immediately and call 911.

## Protecting Your Pipes When the Heat Goes Out

Pipes are your home's biggest outage vulnerability: an eighth-inch crack from a burst pipe can leak 250 gallons of water a day, and an unheated house can reach pipe-bursting temperatures in hours.

1. **Let faucets drip.** A pencil-thin stream from faucets served by vulnerable runs — exterior walls, crawl spaces, attics — relieves the pressure buildup that actually bursts pipes. Drip both hot and cold slightly; moving water buys time.
2. **Open cabinet doors** under sinks on exterior walls, so whatever residual warmth remains reaches the pipes.
3. **Drain the system if the outage will be long.** If temperatures are well below freezing with no restoration estimate, shut off the main valve and drain the lines — a drained pipe can't burst.
4. **Never thaw a frozen pipe with an open flame.** A hair dryer or electric heating pad is the safe route — a torch on a frozen pipe is how walls catch fire.

For the full prevention playbook — insulation, heat tape, and which runs are most vulnerable — read [how to keep pipes from freezing in winter](/posts/how-to-keep-pipes-from-freezing-winter/). Winterizing before storm season costs a fraction of a water-damage insurance claim.

## Food Safety: The Fridge and Freezer Clock

The food-safety clock starts when the power dies — but it's forgiving if you keep the doors shut.

- **Fridge:** food stays safe for about **4 hours** with the door unopened — every peek shortens it.
- **Full freezer:** about **48 hours** unopened. A half-full freezer holds roughly 24 hours. Freezing a few jugs of water ahead of storm season helps an emptier freezer hold temperature longer.
- **The golden rule:** "When in doubt, throw it out." If perishable food has been above 40°F for more than 2 hours, it goes — no taste-testing your way to safety. This is exactly what those appliance thermometers are for.
- **Relocate early.** If the outage stretches past the fridge's safe window, move dairy, meat, and leftovers into the freezer, or into coolers packed in the snow outside (secure them from animals and keep them shaded).

Skip the sniff test — spoiled food doesn't always smell bad, and food poisoning during an outage is misery stacked on misery.
## Generator Rules That Prevent CO Deaths

A portable generator is the best tool for a multi-day outage — and one of the most dangerous if misused. The rules are non-negotiable:

1. **Place it at least 20 feet from the house**, with the exhaust pointed away from the building. Never in the garage, basement, on the porch, or near windows, vents, or doors — CO drifts and concentrates in enclosed and semi-enclosed spaces.
2. **Let it cool before refueling.** Spilled gasoline on a hot engine is a fire you don't want in the middle of a storm.
3. **Use heavy-duty, outdoor-rated extension cords** and respect the wattage rating — motors surge at startup.
4. **Never backfeed** — never plug the generator into a wall outlet to power the house. The only safe way to connect a generator to your panel is a transfer switch installed by a licensed electrician.
5. **Run your battery-backup CO detector** the whole time the generator operates.

In ice-storm country, a mid-size portable generator pays for itself in one avoided hotel stay.

## During the Outage: Small Moves That Save Big Headaches

- **Release the garage door manually.** Nearly every opener has a red emergency-release cord — pull it straight down to disconnect the trolley, then lift the door by hand. Practice this once on a normal day.
- **Unplug sensitive electronics** — TVs, computers, game consoles — to protect them from the surge when the grid comes back. Leave one lamp switched on so you know the moment power returns.
- **Fight the chimney effect.** Block drafts under doors with rolled towels, close fireplace dampers when the fireplace isn't in use (heat escapes straight up the flue), and keep that "camp room" door closed.
- **Check on neighbors**, especially elderly or disabled residents nearby.
- **Don't call 911 for outage updates** — report the outage to your utility once, then follow their outage map.

## When the Power Comes Back

- **Check the fridge first.** Read the appliance thermometer before anything else: fridge at or below 40°F, freezer at or below 0°F. Anything perishable that sat above 40°F for more than 2 hours gets tossed.
- **Look for surge damage.** Turn lights on room by room; if anything smells burnt or a breaker won't reset, leave it off and call an electrician.
- **Run the faucets** for a minute each to clear air and watch for leaks signaling freeze damage.
- **Restock everything you used** — batteries, water, canned goods, fuel, medications. The next storm doesn't wait for you to get around to it.

## When to Call a Pro

Call an electrician if breakers won't reset, you smell burning from an outlet or panel, or you want a transfer switch installed for a generator — that's licensed-electrician work, not a DIY project. Call a plumber immediately for a suspected frozen or burst pipe — water damage compounds by the hour. And if anyone shows signs of CO poisoning — headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion — get outside and call 911 first, ask questions later.

A winter outage you prepared for is an adventure; one you didn't is an emergency. Build the kit this weekend, and the next ice storm becomes a story — not a disaster.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long can my house stay warm without power?**

A well-insulated home holds livable temperatures for several hours; a drafty one can slide toward freezing overnight in severe cold. Closing off unused rooms and hanging blankets over doorways concentrates warmth where you sleep.

**Can I use my gas stove or oven to heat the house during an outage?**

No — never. Gas ovens and burners produce carbon monoxide, and running them for heat is a leading cause of winter CO poisoning. Stick to safe heating options and a working CO detector.

**How far should my generator be from the house?**

At least 20 feet, with the exhaust pointed away from the house — and never in a garage, basement, porch, or near windows, vents, or doors. Carbon monoxide drifts into enclosed spaces, which is why garage placement kills people every storm season.

**Will my pipes freeze if the power is out for just one night?**

Possibly, if temperatures are well below freezing and the pipes run through exterior walls, crawl spaces, or attics. Let faucets drip, open cabinet doors under exterior-wall sinks, and if the outage will be long, shut off the main valve and drain the system.

**How do I know if my fridge food is still safe after an outage?**

Keep the door shut — food stays safe for about 4 hours unopened. Check an appliance thermometer: anything perishable above 40°F for more than 2 hours goes in the trash. A full freezer stays safe about 48 hours unopened.

**What should I do first when the power comes back?**

Check the fridge thermometer first, then turn lights on room by room watching for surge damage, run each faucet to check for freeze-related leaks, and restock every supply you used.
