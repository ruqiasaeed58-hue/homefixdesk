Your garbage disposal can handle small soft food scraps — and that's about it. **Never put grease, turkey or chicken bones, potato peels, coffee grounds, eggshells, pasta, rice, celery, onion skins, fruit pits, pumpkin guts, paint or chemicals, or large quantities of anything down the disposal.** Plumbers report that Thanksgiving is the single busiest day of the year for disposal calls, and it's always the same culprits: turkey bones, potato peels, celery, and grease from the big meal. If your unit is already jammed, use the reset button on the bottom, free the flywheel with a hex key, and call a plumber if it hums but won't spin.

## Why Thanksgiving kills so many disposals

A garbage disposal isn't a trash can. It's a small grinder with impellers that fling food against a grind ring, and the slurry still has to travel through your drainpipes. Most disposal failures aren't broken motors — they're clogs the disposal helped create by shredding food into exactly the kind of paste that sticks to pipes.

Thanksgiving concentrates every disposal enemy into one afternoon: a sink full of potato peels, celery trimmings, turkey bones, pumpkin guts, and hot grease someone rinses down the drain. The disposal chews through it all, but the mixture settles and congeals in the pipes below, and by evening the sink won't drain. The rules below apply year-round, but if you're hosting Thanksgiving dinner, pin this to the fridge.

## The dirty dozen: 12 things that kill disposals

### 1. Grease, oil, and fat

The number-one pipe killer. Hot grease looks harmless as liquid, but it **solidifies as it cools in your drainpipes**, coating the inside with a narrowing layer of fat that traps everything else. The disposal can't do anything about grease — it just passes it through into the pipes where it hardens. Wipe pans with a paper towel and throw it in the trash, or pour cooled oil into an old container and toss that. This is the single best habit you can build.

### 2. Turkey and chicken bones

Disposals are designed for soft scraps, not bone. A turkey leg bone will **jam the impeller plate or shatter into sharp fragments** that damage the grind ring. Small fish bones are fine in moderation, but any poultry, pork, or beef bone belongs in the trash. On Thanksgiving, the carcass pile is the biggest jam-maker — the bones are large, dense, and oddly shaped.

### 3. Potato peels

The classic holiday mistake. When the disposal grinds potato peels, the **starch forms a thick, gluey paste** that clings to the disposal walls and the drainpipe — a handful of peels from five pounds of potatoes can create a full blockage. It seems harmless because it's just vegetable skin, but plumbers consider it one of the worst offenders.

### 4. Pasta and rice

Cooked pasta and rice keep absorbing water and **swell inside the drain**, turning into a dense, starchy plug. Small amounts build up over time, and a bowl of leftover stuffing or rice is a guaranteed clog. Always scrape these into the trash.

### 5. Celery and other fibrous vegetables

Celery, asparagus, artichoke leaves, corn husks, and rhubarb have long, tough fibers that **wrap around the disposal's impellers like twine** and stall the motor. Instead of grinding, the fibers tangle, the motor strains, and the thermal protector trips. Stuffing recipes produce heaps of celery trimmings — keep them out of the sink entirely.

### 6. Coffee grounds

It feels natural to rinse the filter into the sink, but coffee grounds behave like sediment. They don't float or grind — they **settle into the sludge at the bottom of the drainpipe** and compact into a dense, concrete-like mass. Toss grounds in the trash or, better, the compost.

### 7. Eggshells

Here's the myth-bust: eggshells don't sharpen the blades — **disposals don't have blades**, they have blunt impellers, and there's nothing to sharpen. The shells shatter into gritty fragments whose **membranes wrap around the impellers** and add to the sediment layer. A single shell won't hurt, but don't run a dozen through it after breakfast.

### 8. Fruit pits and seeds

Peach pits, avocado pits, cherry pits, and olive pits are essentially rocks. They'll **jam the turntable or damage the grind ring**, and avocado pits are large enough to wedge the unit solid. Peach pits can even sprout in the drain trap if they sit there — an absurd but real problem.

### 9. Onion skins

The thin, papery outer layers of onions slip **past the impellers un-ground** and straight into the drain, where they catch on pipe joints and start a clog. They tangle like celery fibers too. Chop trimmings go in the trash.

### 10. Pumpkin guts

The stringy pulp, seeds, and fibrous strands from carving pumpkins or prepping pie combine the worst of several offenders: **fibers that wrap the impellers, seeds that jam the turntable, and a starchy mass that clogs pipes.** Every Halloween-through-Thanksgiving season, pumpkin debris shows up in plumbers' horror stories.

### 11. Paint, chemicals, and drain cleaner in quantity

Never pour paint, solvents, or household chemicals down the disposal — they can **damage seals, contaminate the drain system, and create toxic fumes.** Skip heavy-duty chemical drain cleaners for disposal clogs too: they corrode the disposal's internal seals and gaskets. If a mild clog needs help, a baking-soda-and-vinegar flush is the safe move.

### 12. Large amounts of anything

Even foods the disposal can handle will defeat it in volume. **Overfeeding is the silent killer**: a giant bowl of scraps dumped in all at once overwhelms the impellers and packs the drainpipe. Always feed small handfuls at a time, with cold water running before, during, and after.

## What's actually fine to put down the disposal

Disposals aren't useless — they're just narrow-purpose. These are safe in **small quantities**:

- **Small soft food scraps** — the bits left on a plate after scraping: soft vegetables, small bits of cooked meat, crumbs.
- **Small fruit scraps** — a few grape halves, small pieces of melon rind. Nothing hard, nothing fibrous.
- **Cold water, always.** Run cold water before you flip the switch, while grinding, and for **15–30 seconds after** the grinding sound stops. Cold water keeps fat solid so it flushes through instead of melting and recoating the pipes, and the extra flush time carries the slurry fully out of the trap.
- **A squirt of dish soap** now and then helps keep the grind chamber clean.
- **Citrus peels for freshening** — a few lemon or orange peel pieces once in a while freshen the smell. Do this occasionally, not daily; large amounts of pith are starchy and can build up.

## Monthly cleaning: the ice-cube trick

Once a month, fill the disposal with a tray of **ice cubes and a cup of rock salt or baking soda**, then run it with cold water. The ice knocks buildup off the grind ring and impellers, the salt scours, and the cold water flushes it all away. Follow with a few lemon peels for freshness. This simple routine costs nothing and noticeably extends the life of the unit.

Also keep the **splash guard (the rubber flaps at the drain mouth)** wiped clean — gunk under the flaps is the main source of disposal odor.

## My disposal is already jammed — what now?

**Safety first: never put your hand or fingers down the drain**, even if the switch is off. Switches can be bumped, and a jammed impeller can snap free suddenly. Use tongs or pliers from above only.

### 1. Hit the reset button

Every disposal has a small **red reset button on the bottom of the unit** under the sink. Press it firmly. The motor has a built-in thermal overload that trips when it strains — the reset restores power. If it trips again immediately, the unit is still jammed.

### 2. Free the flywheel with a hex key

With the **switch OFF**, insert a **1/4-inch Allen (hex) wrench** into the hex-shaped socket at the very bottom center of the disposal. Work it back and forth to manually rotate the impeller plate and dislodge whatever's jamming it. You should feel it break free. Fish out the offending object with tongs, press the reset button again, and test with cold water running.

### 3. When to call a plumber

- The unit **hums but won't spin** after you've freed the flywheel and pressed reset — the motor may be burned out. Our [disposal repair guide](/posts/why-garbage-disposal-humming-not-working/) walks through the full diagnosis.
- **Water leaks** from the unit body itself (not the pipe connections) — internal seals have failed.
- The disposal is **more than 10–12 years old** and keeps jamming — replacement usually beats another repair call.

## How much a disposal clog costs

A straightforward plumber visit to clear a disposal jam typically runs **$100–$250**, depending on your area and whether the clog is in the unit or the drainpipe below. A full disposal replacement (unit + installation) generally runs **$200–$600** — the units themselves are $100–$300 and labor $100–$300. Compare that to a paper towel for wiping grease: the prevention is nearly free.

## Prevention checklist for Thanksgiving (and every day)

1. **Scrape plates into the trash** before rinsing — the disposal is a backup, not the primary waste route.
2. **Put a drain strainer in the sink** during heavy prep days to catch peels and trimmings.
3. **Collect grease in a container**, never the drain. A coffee can under the sink works.
4. **Feed scraps in small handfuls** with cold water running before, during, and after.
5. **Teach the household** — guests on Thanksgiving will cheerfully rinse turkey carcass trimmings into the sink if nobody tells them otherwise. A five-second briefing saves a plumber call.
6. **Ice-cube clean monthly** and keep the splash guard wiped down.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I put coffee grounds down the garbage disposal?**
No — coffee grounds settle into the drainpipe and compact into a dense sludge that the disposal can't clear. They don't grind or float; they accumulate. Rinse the filter into the trash or compost instead. The occasional few grounds won't destroy anything, but making it a daily habit is a reliable way to build a slow drain.

**Are eggshells good for sharpening disposal blades?**
This is the most persistent disposal myth, and it's doubly wrong: disposals don't have blades to sharpen — they use blunt impellers and a grind ring — and eggshell membranes actually wrap around the impellers while the grit adds to drain sediment. A single shell is harmless, but don't treat eggshells as maintenance.

**What should I do if I accidentally put grease down the disposal?**
Don't panic. Run cold water (not hot — hot water just pushes the liquid grease deeper before it re-solidifies) and a squirt of dish soap for a full minute to flush as much through as possible. If the drain slows afterward, a baking soda and vinegar flush can help, and a plumber's snake clears a hardened grease clog for roughly $100–$250.

**Can you put potato peels in the garbage disposal?**
No — potato peels are one of the worst disposal offenders. The starch turns into a thick paste when ground, which coats the disposal and drainpipe and hardens into a blockage. Even a few potatoes' worth of peels can clog a drain. Scrape them into the trash or compost.

**How long should I run the water after using the disposal?**
Keep cold water running for at least 15–30 seconds after the grinding sound stops. The slurry still has to travel through the trap and down the drainpipe — cutting the water early leaves ground food sitting in the pipes, where it settles and hardens.

**Is it safe to use chemical drain cleaner in a garbage disposal?**
Avoid it. Caustic drain cleaners can corrode the disposal's rubber seals and gaskets, and they can splash back up the drain when they react. For a mild clog, try baking soda plus vinegar followed by hot water, or a plunger on the sink. For a real jam, use the hex-key method — or call a plumber rather than a chemical bottle.
