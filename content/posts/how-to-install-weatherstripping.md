Weatherstripping is the cheapest meaningful fall upgrade: $20–$40 of material and one afternoon can cut door and window drafts dramatically. Three steps — measure the gap, pick the matching type, stick it on straight. Here's how to get each part right.

## The 5 types of weatherstripping (and where each belongs)

Walk into any hardware store and you'll face a wall of options. There are really only five families, each with a natural home:

**1. V-strip (tension seal).** A V-shaped strip of vinyl or spring bronze that bridges a gap and springs back when compressed. The classic choice for the sides of double-hung windows (it slides into the sash channels) and for door stops. Vinyl lasts 5–10 years; spring bronze costs more but can last 20+ years and suits older homes. Best for: narrow, even gaps on doors and double-hung windows.

**2. Foam tape.** Adhesive-backed foam in various thicknesses. The easiest to install — peel and stick — and the most forgiving of irregular gaps. The tradeoff is lifespan: 1–3 years before it compresses permanently. Best for: quick fixes, irregular gaps, rentals, window sashes.

**3. Felt.** Plain or metal-reinforced strips, sold in rolls. The cheapest option and easy to staple or glue, but it wears fast in high-traffic spots and absorbs moisture. Best for: low-use windows, attic hatches, and this-weekend fixes on a two-dollar budget.

**4. Door sweeps.** Vinyl, brush, or rubber strips mounted to the door bottom. The bottom of an exterior door is typically the single biggest air gap in the whole assembly — a 1/8-inch gap under a 36-inch door leaks as much air as a 2-inch hole in the wall. Best for: the bottom of every exterior door. Non-negotiable.

**5. Tubular rubber or vinyl gasket.** A hollow tube that compresses to seal, sold as adhesive-backed, kerf-in (presses into a saw cut), or nail-on. The most durable, best-sealing option for doors — 5–10 years — and excellent for casement windows. Best for: exterior doors you want to seal once and forget.

## Measure the gap first

Buying weatherstripping without measuring is the #1 reason the job fails — too thick and the door won't close, too thin and the draft stays. Here's the 5-minute method:

1. **Close the door or window** and study the gap in daylight. Note where it's widest — gaps are almost never even.
2. **Measure the gap width** at the top, middle, and bottom (doors) or several points around the sash (windows). Easy method: press a small ball of modeling clay into the gap, close the door gently, open it, and measure the squashed thickness. That's your gap.
3. **Buy slightly thicker than the largest measurement** — it should compress about 25–50% when the door closes. Foam tape rated for a 1/4-inch gap on a 1/8-inch gap will fight you every time.
4. **Measure lengths:** for a door, the two sides plus the top (the bottom gets a sweep). For a double-hung window, both sash channels plus the meeting rail.

Write the numbers on your phone before going to the store. "About yay wide" is how you end up making two trips.

## Installing on doors: step by step

Do this on a dry day above 50°F if you're using adhesive-backed products — cold kills adhesion. Mechanical types (V-strip, sweeps) can go on in any weather.

1. **Remove the old stuff.** Pull off old foam tape and scrape residue with a plastic putty knife. For stubborn adhesive, warm it with a hair dryer and rub with mineral spirits on a rag. The surface must be clean and dry or nothing will stick.
2. **Clean the frame.** Wipe the door stop (the trim the door closes against) with a damp cloth and let it dry fully. A quick scuff with fine sandpaper helps adhesive grab on glossy paint.
3. **Cut your pieces.** Measure the two vertical sides and the top of the door stop; cut V-strip or foam tape with scissors or a utility knife. Cut a hair long and trim after test-fitting — you can't stretch pieces longer.
4. **Install sides first, then the top.** For foam tape: peel a few inches of backing, press the starting end firmly at the top of the side jamb, and work downward without stretching the foam. For V-strip: position it so the open side of the V faces outward, and nail or press per the product instructions.
5. **Mind the corners.** Butt side pieces tight against the top piece — small corner gaps are classic leak points. Overlap slightly rather than leaving a sliver.
6. **Test the close.** The door should shut with slightly more resistance and latch without slamming. If you must force it, the stripping is too thick — step down a size. Do the dollar-bill test: close a bill in the door and pull. You want noticeable drag all the way around.

## Installing on windows: step by step

**Double-hung windows** (slide up and down): press vinyl V-strip into the sash channels with the V opening facing outward — open the lower sash, cut to channel height, and slide it in. Close the sash and check the slide: slightly more friction is right, binding is not. Add foam tape along the meeting rail (where the sashes overlap) — a notorious leak point — and a thin foam strip where the lower sash meets the sill.

**Casement and awning windows** (crank-out): these seal like a refrigerator door, so run tubular gasket or foam tape around the entire frame in one continuous run, butting corners tightly.

**Sliding windows:** treat like double-hung windows turned sideways — V-strip in the tracks the sash slides in.

Two rules for all windows: **never block the weep holes** (the little drainage slots on the outside bottom of the frame), and **never paint over weatherstripping** — paint glues it into a useless lump. Painting the trim? Paint first, let it cure fully, then install the stripping.

## Door sweeps and thresholds

The door bottom deserves its own section — it's the biggest gap and the most abused part of the assembly.

**Types of sweeps:** vinyl/rubber blade (cheap, effective, 3–5 years), brush sweeps (bristles conform to uneven floors — great for old houses), and automatic door bottoms (a spring-loaded seal that drops only when the door closes — the $30–$60 premium option, ideal over carpet or uneven floors where a fixed sweep would drag).

**Installation:**

1. Measure the door width and buy slightly wider — most sweeps trim with a hacksaw.
2. Close the door and check clearance. The sweep should just kiss the threshold — light contact, not hard dragging.
3. Hold the sweep against the inside face of the door bottom, mark screw holes, and pre-drill pilots to prevent splitting.
4. Screw it on and test: open and close several times. If it drags heavily, raise it a notch.

**Thresholds:** if the threshold itself is worn or rotted, no sweep will save you. Replacements run $15–$40 and screw down in about 20 minutes. Choose one with an adjustable vinyl insert — you can raise the seal with a screwdriver as it wears.

## Lifespan and replacement

Nothing lasts forever, and weatherstripping fails gradually — most people don't notice until the drafts return. Expected lifespans: foam tape 1–3 years, felt 1–2, vinyl V-strip 5–10, spring bronze V-strip 20+, tubular gasket 5–10, door sweeps 3–5.

Inspect every fall before heating season. Look for foam that's compressed flat and won't spring back, cracked or peeling V-strip, torn or curled sweep blades, and any stripping that's painted over. Replacement is just the installation steps above — and the second time goes twice as fast since surfaces are already prepped.

One habit extends everything: keep contact surfaces clean. Dirt ground into foam or V-strip acts like sandpaper every time the door closes. A quick wipe each fall costs nothing.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Buying without measuring.** Too thick = door won't latch; too thin = draft remains. Measure the actual gap, don't guess.
- **Installing adhesive products in the cold.** Below ~50°F the adhesive never fully bonds and the stripping peels off by January. Warm the surface with a hair dryer first, or wait for a mild day.
- **Stretching foam tape during installation.** It contracts back over a few days, opening gaps at the ends. Lay it on relaxed.
- **Forgetting the door bottom.** People meticulously strip three sides and ignore the 3/8-inch gap underneath — which leaks more than the other three sides combined. Always do the sweep.
- **Nailing through adhesive foam tape.** The holes become leak paths and the tape tears. Adhesive means adhesive; want fasteners? Buy the nail-on type.
- **Painting over weatherstripping.** The paint bonds it to the frame and it tears the first time the window opens. Paint first, strip second.
- **Sealing so tight the door won't latch.** If you must slam it, step down a thickness. A door that doesn't latch is a security problem, not an energy upgrade.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which type of weatherstripping lasts the longest?**
Spring bronze V-strip, by a wide margin — 20+ years, and it was the standard in quality homes for a century. Among modern materials, tubular silicone or EPDM gaskets (5–10+ years) easily beat foam tape (1–3 years). The do-it-once combo: bronze V-strip on windows, tubular gasket on doors.

**Can I install weatherstripping in cold weather?**
Mechanical types — V-strip, sweeps, nail-on gaskets — yes, any temperature. Adhesive-backed products need the surface above ~50°F to bond. In a pinch, warm the frame with a hair dryer right before applying and keep the door closed 24 hours. But a mild fall afternoon is the ideal window.

**My door won't close after installing weatherstripping. What did I do wrong?**
Almost certainly the stripping is too thick for the gap. Remove it, measure the actual gap with the clay-ball method, and buy one size thinner. The other possibility is placement — stripping mounted too far onto the door stop can bind.

**Should I weatherstrip interior doors too?**
Only doors between conditioned and unconditioned spaces — garage, basement, attic. Weatherstripping bedroom or bathroom doors just traps air and can unbalance HVAC airflow. Leave interior doors alone.

**How do I get old adhesive foam tape off?**
Warm it with a hair dryer on medium to soften the adhesive, then peel slowly, rolling residue off with your thumb. For what's left, rub with mineral spirits or adhesive remover on a rag, wash with soapy water, and let dry fully before applying new stripping. Use a plastic scraper — never a metal blade on a painted frame you care about.

**Is weatherstripping enough, or do I need new doors and windows?**
For drafts, weatherstripping is almost always enough. New doors and windows are justified by rot, fogged glass, or broken hardware — not air leaks, which are a sealing problem with a sealing solution. Spend the $30 first; it's the whole fix more often than not.

## When to call a pro

Call a pro when the frame itself is the problem: rotted sills, windows visibly out of square, doors needing planing or rehanging — no foam tape fixes a door warped an inch out of true. Kerf-in bronze stripping routed into historic sashes is specialist work worth paying for. And if you've stripped everything and still feel drafts, the leak is in the wall around the opening — a job for an insulation contractor with a blower door.
