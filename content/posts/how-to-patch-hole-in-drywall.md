A hole in drywall looks like a disaster but repairs beautifully — even a fist-sized hole can be made invisible in a weekend with a $15 patch kit. The trick is matching the method to the hole size: tiny holes get one treatment, medium holes another, and anything bigger than your hand needs a different approach entirely.

## Match the method to the hole size

- **Nail/screw holes and dings (under 1/2 inch)** → lightweight spackle, one coat, 10 minutes. See Method 1.
- **Small to medium holes (1/2 inch to 6 inches)** → self-adhesive mesh patch or a stick-on metal patch + joint compound, 2–3 thin coats over a day or two. See Method 2. This covers doorknob holes — the most common drywall damage there is.
- **Large holes (over 6 inches)** → cut out a square and screw in a real drywall piece (a "California patch" or full replacement). See Method 3.

## What you will need

**For small holes:** lightweight spackle, a flexible putty knife (1.5-inch), fine sandpaper (120- and 220-grit), touch-up paint.

**For medium holes:** self-adhesive mesh patch kit (or metal stick-on patch for holes up to 4 inches), lightweight joint compound ("mud"), 6-inch drywall knife, sanding sponge, primer, paint.

**For large holes:** a piece of drywall (hardware stores sell 2x2-foot "patch panels"), drywall saw or jab saw, drill, 1-1/4-inch drywall screws, wood furring strips, joint tape, joint compound, 6- and 10-inch knives.

## Method 1: Nail holes and small dings (10 minutes)

1. **Press down any raised edges.** Run the putty knife flat over the hole — if the rim sticks up, it will show through the repair. Lightly dent it inward with the knife handle if needed.
2. **Fill with spackle.** Scoop a pea-sized dab of lightweight spackle on the putty knife and press it into the hole, then scrape the knife flat across the surface to leave the hole filled and the surrounding wall clean. One pass is enough — spackle shrinks very little.
3. **Let it dry** (30–60 minutes for lightweight spackle), then sand smooth with 220-grit paper using light, circular strokes.
4. **Touch up with paint.** Dab paint on with a small brush or mini roller, feathering the edges. Flat paint hides repairs best; glossy paint shows every imperfection.

## Method 2: Medium holes — the patch kit walkthrough (the one you will use most)

This is the classic doorknob-hole repair. Total hands-on time is about 30 minutes spread over two days (drying time does the rest).

### Step 1: Prep the hole
Trim away any loose or crumbling drywall around the hole with a utility knife so the patch sits on solid edges. If the hole has torn paper facing flapping around it, cut that back too — loose paper bubbles under joint compound. Wipe the area clean of dust.

### Step 2: Stick on the patch
Peel the backing off a self-adhesive mesh patch (for holes up to about 4 inches) or a self-adhesive metal patch (for holes up to 6 inches) and center it over the hole, pressing firmly so all edges stick flat to the wall. The patch must lie perfectly flat — any lifted edge will telegraph through the compound.

### Step 3: First coat of joint compound
Load your 6-inch drywall knife with a golf-ball-sized glob of lightweight joint compound. Starting from the center, spread it outward over the patch in firm, overlapping strokes, pressing the compound through the mesh. Then scrape the knife nearly flat against the wall and pull off the excess — you want the patch covered with the thinnest possible layer, feathering the edges out 2–3 inches beyond the patch. Thin coats dry faster, shrink less, and sand easier. Thick coats crack.

### Step 4: Let it dry completely
This is where patience pays. The first coat needs 12–24 hours (joint compound dries white when ready — any dark or gray spots are still wet). Rushing to coat two over a damp coat one causes bubbles and cracking.

### Step 5: Second and third coats
Lightly sand the first coat with a sanding sponge to knock down ridges — do not sand through to the mesh. Apply a second thin coat, this time feathering 4–6 inches wider than the first. Dry, sand lightly, then apply a final skim coat feathered wider still. Each coat should be thinner and wider than the last; that gradual buildup is what makes the repair disappear.

### Step 6: Final sand and prime
Sand the final coat smooth with 220-grit paper or a fine sanding sponge, using a work light held at an angle to the wall — raking light reveals every bump your straight-on view misses. Wipe off the dust with a damp cloth. Then **prime the patch** — this step is non-negotiable. Joint compound absorbs paint differently than the surrounding wall, and unprimed patches "flash" (show as dull spots) through the topcoat. One coat of primer, then two coats of your wall paint, feathering outward.

## Method 3: Large holes (over 6 inches)

For big damage, a patch kit is not enough — you need real drywall.

1. **Cut a clean square.** Use a straightedge and utility knife (or a jab saw) to cut the damaged area into a neat square or rectangle, cutting back to the centers of the studs on at least two sides if possible.
2. **Add backing.** Screw short wood furring strips inside the opening, overlapping the edges, so the new drywall piece has something to fasten to.
3. **Cut and fit the new piece.** Cut a drywall patch 1/8 inch smaller than the opening on each side. Test-fit it — it should drop in with a small, even gap all around.
4. **Screw it in.** Drive 1-1/4-inch drywall screws through the patch into the backing strips every 6 inches, dimpling the screw heads just below the paper surface without tearing it.
5. **Tape and mud.** Apply self-adhesive mesh tape (or paper tape bedded in compound) over all seams, then build up 3 thin coats of joint compound exactly as in Method 2, feathering wide. Sand, prime, paint.

**The California patch shortcut:** for holes 6–12 inches with intact backing, you can cut the new drywall with a 2-inch paper "flange" left around it, mud the flange directly to the wall, and skip the wood backing. It is faster and plenty strong for anything that will not take an impact.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **One thick coat instead of three thin ones.** The single biggest beginner error. Thick compound shrinks, cracks, and takes days to dry. Thin coats are faster overall.
- **Skipping primer.** The repaired spot will flash through the paint and you will see it forever. Prime every patch.
- **Sanding before the compound is fully dry.** Damp compound gums up, tears, and leaves grooves. Wait until it is uniformly white.
- **Not feathering wide enough.** A patch sanded flush but only as wide as the hole still shows as a hump. Each coat goes wider — 8 to 12 inches of feathering for a 4-inch hole.
- **Painting with the wrong sheen.** If the wall is eggshell and you touch up with flat (or vice versa), the repair is visible from across the room. Match the existing sheen.

## Matching texture

If your wall has texture (orange peel, knockdown), a smooth patch will stand out even if it is perfectly flat. For small repairs, orange-peel texture in a spray can ($10–$15) does a convincing job: practice on cardboard first, apply light coats, and let it dry before priming. Knockdown texture needs the spray plus a light pass with a wide knife after it firms up. Heavily textured walls on large repairs are where calling a pro starts making sense.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long does a drywall patch take from start to finish?**
Hands-on time for a medium hole is under an hour. Calendar time is 2–3 days because each coat of joint compound needs 12–24 hours to dry. You can shorten this with 20-minute setting-type compound (it hardens chemically rather than drying), but it is harder to sand — save it for when you are experienced.

**Can I just stuff the hole with newspaper and spackle over it?**
For tiny holes it sort of works, but for anything bigger the spackle has nothing solid behind it and will crack and sink. A $5 mesh patch is the correct backing and takes the same effort.

**Do I need to prime before painting the patch?**
Yes. Joint compound is far more porous than the surrounding painted wall. Without primer, the paint soaks in unevenly and the patch shows as a dull "flashed" spot. One coat of any drywall primer solves it.

**The patched area looks perfect until the light hits it at an angle. What went wrong?**
Almost certainly insufficient feathering or a ridge left from sanding. Shine a work light along the wall while you sand — raking light is brutally honest — and widen your final skim coat.

## When to call a pro

- Water damage: if the drywall is soft, discolored, or smells musty, there may be a leak behind it and possibly mold. Fix the moisture source first; a pro should assess anything beyond a small dried stain.
- Holes in ceilings, especially large ones — overhead mudding is genuinely difficult, and ceiling drywall is often thicker (5/8-inch, sometimes fire-rated).
- Plaster walls (common in pre-1960 homes) repair differently than drywall; the techniques above assume gypsum drywall.
- If the wall has heavy texture over a large repair area and you want it invisible, a drywall finisher earns their fee.
