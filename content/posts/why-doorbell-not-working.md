A doorbell that stopped working is almost never a wiring-in-the-wall problem. Wired doorbells have just three parts — the button, the transformer, and the chime — and they fail in a predictable order. Test them in sequence and you will find the culprit in under 30 minutes.

## How a wired doorbell works

Understanding the loop makes troubleshooting obvious. It is a simple circuit:

1. The **transformer** constantly supplies 16–24V to the circuit.
2. The **button** is a normally-open switch — pressing it closes the circuit.
3. Closing the circuit energizes the **chime's** electromagnet, which pulls the plunger to strike the tone bars — "ding."
4. Releasing the button opens the circuit; a spring returns the plunger — "dong."

That is the whole system. Any break in this loop — dead button, failed transformer, stuck plunger, broken wire — silences it. Test in the order the signal travels: button → chime → transformer → wiring.

## Quick diagnosis

- **Button feels stuck, corroded, or mushy** → the button failed (most common). See Step 1.
- **Button clicks but no chime** → chime mechanism or transformer. See Steps 2–3.
- **Chime hums constantly** → stuck button or shorted wiring. See Step 1.
- **Intermittent ringing** → loose wire at button or chime. See Step 4.
- **Wireless doorbell dead** → batteries or pairing. See Step 5.

## Safety note

Doorbell circuits run on low voltage (16–24V) from a transformer — much safer than household wiring. You can test the button and chime without turning off the breaker, but turn off the breaker when replacing the transformer, which connects to 120V house wiring.

## What you will need

- Screwdriver set
- Multimeter ($15 — useful for the whole house)
- Replacement doorbell button ($5–$10)
- Replacement transformer ($15–$20) if needed
- Sandpaper or wire brush

## Step 1: Test and replace the button

The outdoor button takes weather abuse and fails most often.

1. **Inspect it.** Corrosion, a stuck plunger, or a cracked housing means replacement — do not bother testing further.
2. **Test for continuity:** remove the button, disconnect its two wires, and touch the multimeter probes to the button's terminals while pressing it. No continuity = dead button.
3. **Bypass test:** touch the two wires together briefly. If the chime rings, the button was the problem.
4. **Replace the button:** connect the two wires to the new button's terminals (order does not matter), mount it, and test.

## Step 2: Check the chime

The chime box (usually in a hallway) has a mechanical plunger that strikes metal bars.

1. **Remove the chime cover** and look for: dust buildup, a stuck plunger, or broken wires.
2. **Clean it:** dust and spider webs genuinely stop chimes from working. A soft brush and compressed air fix many "dead" chimes.
3. **Check the wires** on the terminals labeled FRONT, TRANS, and REAR — tighten any loose screws.
4. **Test voltage** at the TRANS terminals with the multimeter — you should read 16–24V. No voltage = transformer problem (Step 3).

## Step 3: Check the transformer

The transformer (usually near the electrical panel, in a closet, or in the attic) steps 120V down to doorbell voltage.

1. **Find it** — follow the thin doorbell wires from the chime, or look for a small metal box mounted on or near the breaker panel.
2. **Test output voltage** with the multimeter: 16–24V across the low-voltage terminals. Zero or very low = failed transformer.
3. **Replace it:** turn off the breaker first. The transformer connects to 120V on one side (black/white wires) and the doorbell wires on the other. Wire the new one identically, matching the voltage rating (16V is standard; some chimes need 24V — check the chime label).

## Step 4: Trace wiring problems

If all three parts test good, the wiring between them has an issue:

1. **Check every connection point** — button, chime, transformer. Loose wire nuts or corroded terminals are the usual suspects.
2. **Look for damage** where wires run through attics or crawlspaces — rodent chewing and nails through wires happen.
3. **Test continuity** of each wire run with the multimeter to find a break.

## Step 5: Wireless doorbells

- **Replace batteries** in both the button and the chime unit — the #1 fix.
- **Re-pair** the button and chime per the manual (usually hold a button on each).
- **Check range and interference:** thick walls, metal doors, and WiFi routers can block the signal. Move the chime closer or change the channel/frequency if the model allows.
- **Consider upgrading:** a video doorbell ($50–$150) replaces the whole system and adds a camera.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Replacing the chime first.** The button fails far more often — always start there.
- **Buying the wrong transformer voltage.** Match the chime's requirement (check the label: 16V vs 24V).
- **Working on the transformer with the breaker on.** The low-voltage side is safe; the 120V input side is not.
- **Ignoring a constantly humming chime.** A stuck button keeps the transformer energized 24/7, which overheats it and can burn it out — fix the button promptly.
- **Assuming the wiring is bad.** In-wall doorbell wire rarely fails. Test the three components before opening walls.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long do doorbell transformers last?**
10–20 years typically. A humming transformer that is hot to the touch is failing — replace it before it dies completely.

**Can I upgrade to a video doorbell with my existing wiring?**
Usually yes — most video doorbells work with standard 16–24V doorbell wiring. Check the voltage at the button first; some need 24V and a transformer upgrade.

**Why does my doorbell ring by itself?**
A stuck or water-damaged button is the usual cause. Moisture inside the button creates a partial short that triggers the chime randomly. Replace the button with a weatherproof model.

**The chime works but sounds weak — why?**
Low transformer voltage, a dying transformer, or dust gumming up the plunger. Test the voltage and clean the mechanism.

**Can I have two chimes on one doorbell?**
Yes — wire the second chime in parallel with the first (TRANS to TRANS, FRONT to FRONT). But check the transformer's VA rating: two chimes draw more current, and an undersized transformer will make both sound weak. Upgrade to a 30VA transformer if needed.

**Why did my doorbell stop working after a power outage?**
Power surges during outages kill transformers. Test the transformer output first — it is the most surge-vulnerable part of the system. A $15 replacement usually brings everything back.

## Upgrading to a video doorbell: what to check

1. **Voltage:** most video doorbells need 16–24V AC. Measure at the existing button wires.
2. **Transformer VA rating:** video doorbells draw more current than mechanical chimes. If your transformer is an old 10VA unit, upgrade to 16V/30VA ($18) to avoid weak performance and overheating.
3. **Existing chime compatibility:** some video doorbells need a small power adapter wired at the chime to work with mechanical chimes — check the model's instructions.
4. **WiFi signal at the door:** the camera needs a solid 2.4GHz signal through your front wall. Test with your phone at the door before buying.

## When to call a pro

- You are not comfortable working at the breaker panel to replace a transformer — an electrician does it in 30 minutes.
- The wiring is damaged inside walls and needs new wire runs fished through.
- Multiple low-voltage systems (doorbell + thermostat + security) are failing — may indicate a panel or grounding issue.
- Your home has old or aluminum wiring and you find damaged connections — get professional eyes on it.
