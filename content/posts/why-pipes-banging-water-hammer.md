That loud bang — or series of bangs — from inside your walls when you shut off the water is called **water hammer**: hundreds of pounds of moving water stopping in a fraction of a second. A washing machine filling its tub is the #1 trigger, because its solenoid valves slam shut in milliseconds. The free fixes are securing loose pipe straps and draining the system to restore waterlogged air chambers; stubborn cases get solved with **water hammer arrestors** screwed onto the washing machine or dishwasher valves.

The bang matters because it's not just noise. Every hammer blow is a pressure spike — sometimes two to four times your normal pressure — hammering joints, fittings, and appliance valves. Ignore it for years and the fatigue shows up as leaks. The good news: this is one of the most fixable noises in a house.

## What water hammer is: the 10-second physics

Water is heavy and nearly incompressible. When it's flowing and a valve slams shut, the moving water column has nowhere to go — it stops instantly, and its momentum converts into a **pressure wave** racing back through the pipe at the speed of sound in water (about 4,800 feet per second). The wave slams into the nearest elbow or valve, the pipe shudders in its straps, and you hear the bang.

Three factors make hammer worse:

1. **How fast the valve closes.** A faucet handle turned by hand: no problem. A washer solenoid snapping shut in milliseconds: bang.
2. **How fast the water was flowing.** Higher velocity means more momentum — long, straight runs to a washer hammer the hardest.
3. **How high the static pressure is.** At 80+ psi, the same valve closure produces a much nastier spike than at 55 psi. A pressure check is part of every hammer fix — [Why Is My Water Pressure Low Throughout the House?](/posts/why-water-pressure-low-throughout-house/) explains the gauge test.

Your plumbing already has a defense: pockets of trapped air (air chambers) that compress and absorb the shock. Over time that air dissolves into the water, the chambers go dead, and the bang begins — which is why hammer often appears gradually in a house that used to be quiet.

## Identifying the trigger fixture

Before fixing anything, find **which fixture** causes the bang. Stand near the noise source (basement, utility room) and have someone run fixtures one at a time while you listen.

**The usual suspects, in order:**

- **Washing machine.** The undisputed #1 culprit. Its fill valves are solenoids that close in milliseconds at the end of every fill cycle. If the banging syncs with the washer's fill cycles, you've found it.
- **Dishwasher.** Same solenoid-valve story on a smaller scale — a single sharp thud when the fill valve closes.
- **Toilets.** A worn fill valve that closes abruptly can cause a single thump after the tank fills. A new fill valve is a $15 fix.
- **Single-lever faucets and sprinkler valves.** A faucet slammed shut hard, or an electric irrigation valve, can each produce one bang. Close faucets more gently; sprinkler systems often need their own arrestor at the valve box.

**What it isn't:** a continuous rattling or buzzing while water flows is usually a loose washer or vibrating pipe, not water hammer. A high-pitched squeal points to a worn valve washer. True water hammer is a single decisive bang (or a short series) at the moment the water **stops**.

## The free fixes: secure straps and restore the air chambers

### Fix 1: Secure loose pipes

- Add a strap wherever you find more than **6 feet** of unsupported horizontal pipe (4 feet for vertical runs).
- Use cushioned straps, or slip a scrap of foam insulation between pipe and strap — this damps vibration instead of just relocating it.
- Cost: a bag of straps and screws is **under $10**, and the job takes about an hour in the basement.

Tightening straps won't eliminate the pressure wave itself, but in many homes it's the difference between a startling bang and a dull thud.

### Fix 2: Drain and refill to restore waterlogged air chambers

Many homes have short **air chambers** — vertical pipe stubs, usually 12–18 inches tall, capped at the top and installed before fixture valves. They trap air that cushions hammer blows. Over years the air dissolves and the chamber becomes waterlogged — a dead piece of pipe. Restoring them is free:

1. **Shut off the main water valve**, then **open the highest and lowest faucets** (top-floor bathroom, basement laundry tub). You're draining the pipes, not the water heater — 10–20 minutes.
2. **Wait until flow stops**, then **close the faucets and turn the water back on slowly** to avoid stirring up sediment.
3. **Run each fixture briefly** until the water runs smooth and trapped air escapes.

Cost: **$0**. If the banging disappears, the air chambers were waterlogged and you've restored them. It's temporary — the air dissolves again over a year or two — so many homeowners move to arrestors for a permanent fix.

## Water hammer arrestors: the permanent fix

**The two types:**

- **Screw-on mini arrestors ($15–$30 each).** Screw directly onto the washing machine's hot and cold shutoff valves (hose threads); the washer hoses screw onto them. Five minutes with pliers — no soldering, no pipe cutting. Buy these first.
- **Soldered/sweat-fit arrestors ($20–$40 each).** For dishwasher supplies and hard-piped runs, installed as close to the quick-closing valve as possible. Cutting pipe and soldering means most homeowners hire a plumber — **$150–$300** per arrestor installed.

**Where to put them:**

- **Washing machine:** one on the hot valve, one on the cold valve — the #1 hammer source in the house.
- **Dishwasher:** one on the supply line, under the sink or at the shutoff valve.
- **Irrigation or ice maker:** one per zone valve, or at the fridge supply valve.

**Placement rule:** as close to the quick-closing valve as possible. An arrestor ten feet down the line is far less effective than one right at the valve.

## The pressure check tie-in: high pressure makes hammer worse

Static water pressure and water hammer are directly linked: the same solenoid valve closing at **80 psi** produces a far more violent spike than at **55 psi**. If your house pressure is running high, no arrestor works as well as it should — and the high pressure is quietly damaging everything else too.

**Test it with a $10 hose-bib gauge.** Screw it onto an outdoor spigot or the water heater drain bib, turn it on full with no other water running, and read the static pressure. Code caps residential pressure at **80 psi**; the sweet spot is **55–65 psi**.

**Above 75 psi:** your **pressure-reducing valve (PRV)** is failing or misadjusted. Turning it down directly reduces the violence of every hammer event. [Why Is My Water Pressure Low Throughout the House?](/posts/why-water-pressure-low-throughout-house/) covers the PRV in detail: what it does, how to adjust it safely in small increments, and when to replace it.

**Normal pressure (50–70 psi) but still hammering?** The problem is purely the fast-closing valve plus lost air chambers — arrestors are the right answer, no pressure fix needed.

## Why not to ignore it: joint and fitting fatigue

A single bang is startling; thousands of bangs are destructive. Repeated water hammer does this over the years:

- **Joints fatigue.** Every spike flexes soldered and threaded joints microscopically until they develop weep leaks — often inside a wall, discovered by the water stain below.
- **Appliance valves take the beating.** Washer and dishwasher solenoids are designed for normal pressure, not repeated 200+ psi spikes. A failed fill valve can mean a flooded laundry room, not just noise.
- **Pipe straps work loose.** The movement that makes the noise also backs screws out over time, making the noise — and the movement — worse in a feedback loop.

None of this happens from one bang. It happens from five years of bangs, three times a week, on every wash cycle. That's why the washer — running hundreds of cycles a year — is where arrestors pay for themselves fastest.

## Safety warnings

**Know your main shutoff before draining the system.** If you don't know where it is or it hasn't been turned in a decade, find it and test it on a calm afternoon — not mid-project with the pipes drained.

**Turn the water back on slowly.** Slamming the main valve open after a drain sends a surge of air and sediment through every fixture. Open it gradually, then run each faucet until the sputtering stops.

**Don't overtighten screw-on arrestors.** Hand-tight plus a quarter-turn with pliers is enough; crushing the hose washer turns a $20 fix into a plumber call. And never stand in pooled water while touching the washer's electrical connections.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do my pipes only bang when the washing machine runs?**
Washer fill valves are solenoids that close in milliseconds — the fastest-closing valves in a typical house. The faster the valve closes, the bigger the pressure wave. Screw-on arrestors at the washer's hot and cold valves fix this in minutes.

**Is water hammer dangerous?**
Not immediately, but destructive over time. Each blow is a pressure spike that fatigues joints, fittings, and appliance valves. Years of hammer lead to weep leaks inside walls and premature valve failure. It's cheap to fix and expensive to ignore.

**Will draining my pipes really stop the banging?**
If your home's air chambers have become waterlogged, yes — draining restores the trapped air that cushions the pressure wave. It's free and takes under an hour, though the air dissolves again over a year or two, so arrestors are the permanent version of the same idea.

**Can high water pressure cause banging pipes?**
Absolutely. High static pressure (75+ psi) makes every hammer event more violent. Adjusting the PRV down to 55–65 psi reduces the hammer and protects your whole system. Always check pressure as part of a hammer diagnosis.

**How much does it cost to fix water hammer?**
Free (drain-and-refill, tightening straps) to $10 in straps, to $30–$60 for a pair of screw-on washer arrestors, to $150–$300 per arrestor for plumber-installed units. Almost every case resolves for under $100 in parts.

**Do I need an arrestor on both hot and cold?**
For the washing machine, yes — both fill valves close fast and both lines hammer. For a dishwasher, one arrestor on its supply line is enough. Match arrestors to fast-closing valves, not to every fixture.

## Prevention tips

- **Check house pressure yearly with a $10 gauge.** Keeping static pressure at 55–65 psi is the best hammer prevention — and protects every fixture in the house.
- **Close single-lever faucets gently.** Slamming a kitchen faucet shut is a manual water hammer event — you control this one.
- **Replace aging toilet fill valves.** A $15 valve that closes smoothly beats a worn one that slams. If a toilet thumps after every fill, it's telling you it's done.
- **Re-drain the system every couple of years.** If air chambers were your fix, calendar a reminder — the air dissolves and the bang returns.
- **Install arrestors proactively at the washer.** A $30 pair of screw-on arrestors on the washing machine valves is cheap insurance for the hardest-working solenoids in the house.
- **Keep pipe straps snug.** Check accessible straps in the basement every few years — vibration backs screws out, and loose pipes amplify every future bang.

## When to call a pro

- The banging comes with **visible pipe movement or leaks** — a joint may already be failing
- Your gauge reads above 80 psi and you're not comfortable adjusting or replacing the PRV yourself
- The hammer persists after arrestors and a pressure check — there may be a deeper issue like an undersized line or failing main valve
- Arrestors need soldering into walls for a dishwasher or hard-piped run and you're not confident sweating copper
- An irrigation system hammers — sprinkler valve manifolds are a different plumbing world
- The noise comes from inside a finished wall with no access — a plumber's listening experience pays for itself
