You wake up on a freezing morning, turn on the kitchen faucet, and get nothing — or just a weak trickle. Before you panic, here's the good news: **you can thaw frozen pipes safely yourself in most cases**, and doing it the right way takes less than an hour.

The safe method is simple: open the affected faucet, then apply gentle, controlled heat starting at the faucet end and working backward toward the frozen section. A hair dryer on low, hot wet towels, or a UL-listed heat tape will do the job. **Never use an open flame, propane torch, or kerosene heater** — this is how house fires start every winter.

Once the water flows again, your job isn't done. Check the entire exposed run of pipe for leaks, because thawing often reveals a burst pipe you couldn't see while it was frozen. If you find a burst section, shut off the main water valve immediately and call a plumber.

## How to confirm a pipe is actually frozen

Not every "no water" morning means a frozen pipe. Run this quick diagnosis before you start thawing anything:

**The faucet test.** Turn on the cold water tap on the problem faucet. If nothing comes out, or only a weak trickle, while other faucets in the house work normally, the pipe serving that fixture is very likely frozen. If NO faucets in the house produce water, the freeze is probably in your main supply line, a well system, or a municipal supply issue — thawing one pipe won't help.

**Check the conditions.** Frozen pipes happen when indoor temperatures drop near or below 20°F (-7°C), especially along exterior walls, in unheated crawlspaces, attics, basements, and garages. A faucet that stops working the morning after the season's first hard freeze is the classic signature.

**Touch the exposed pipe.** A frozen section feels noticeably colder than surrounding pipe, and you may see frost, ice, or condensation on its outside. That's your frozen spot.

**Look for a burst.** Before thawing, scan the exposed pipe for cracks, bulges, or water stains on the floor or wall beneath it. If you already see a split pipe, skip the thawing — go straight to shutting off the main valve and calling a plumber.

## Why frozen pipes burst (and why the ice isn't the direct cause)

This is worth understanding, because it changes how you thaw. Most people assume expanding ice splits the pipe. That's not quite what happens.

When water freezes in a pipe, the ice blockage forms a plug. Trapped water sitting **between that ice plug and the closed faucet** gets squeezed as the ice continues to form, and its pressure spikes — sometimes to thousands of pounds per square inch. That pressure spike is what splits the pipe, often at a weak point like a joint or elbow **downstream of the ice, not at the ice itself**.

This matters because of how you thaw. **You always start applying heat at the faucet end and work backward toward the frozen section.** With the faucet open, the melting ice has somewhere to go — water and steam can escape through the open tap instead of building pressure between two ice plugs. Thawing from the middle of a frozen section can trap pressure and cause the very burst you're trying to prevent.

## Causes: why your pipe froze

- **Poor insulation** — pipes in exterior walls, crawlspaces, or attics without proper insulation are the most vulnerable.
- **Thermostat set too low** — turning the heat down below 55°F (13°C) during a cold snap, or switching it off in a vacation home, invites freezing.
- **Drafts** — cracks in walls, missing insulation, or open foundation vents near pipe runs let frigid air in.
- **Frozen outdoor faucets** — a hose left connected to an exterior spigot can send ice pressure back into the interior pipe. See [How Do I Winterize My Outdoor Faucet?](/posts/how-to-winterize-outdoor-faucet/) for the full fall routine.
- **No water movement** — pipes that sit unused overnight freeze first, which is why the standard cold-weather advice is to let a vulnerable faucet drip.

## What you'll need

- A hair dryer with low/medium heat settings (not a heat gun on high — too easy to damage plastic fittings or scorch wood)
- Several large towels and a bucket of hot water, **or** a UL-listed electric heat tape sized for your pipe
- A space heater (optional, to warm the room or cabinet around the pipe)
- A work light or flashlight to inspect the pipe run
- A bucket and towels to catch drips if there's a leak
- Your main water shutoff valve location (find it BEFORE you start — usually in the basement, garage, or utility closet)
- A thermometer or your hand to gauge pipe warmth — you're aiming for warm, never hot enough to burn your hand

## Step-by-step: thawing the pipe safely

1. **Open the faucet** on the affected fixture — both hot and cold handles if it's a single-handle faucet. Leave it open through the whole process. This gives melting water and steam an escape path, which prevents pressure buildup.
2. **Locate the frozen section.** Follow the exposed pipe run from the faucet toward the water source. Look for frost, ice crystals, or the section that feels coldest to the touch. If the pipe is hidden inside a wall, open the nearest cabinet doors and warm that room.
3. **Apply gentle heat starting at the faucet end.** Hold a hair dryer on a LOW or medium setting 6–8 inches from the pipe, moving it slowly back and forth. Work from the faucet toward the frozen blockage — never start in the middle of the frozen section.
4. **Be patient.** Thawing a frozen section can take 30 minutes to an hour depending on the length and thickness of the ice. Move the dryer along the pipe gradually. You should eventually hear dripping, then a weak flow from the open faucet.
5. **Alternative: hot wet towels.** Soak large towels in hot (not boiling) water, wring them lightly, and wrap them around the frozen section starting from the faucet end. Re-soak every 5–10 minutes as they cool. This is slower but very safe, and works well in tight spots a hair dryer can't reach.
6. **Alternative: UL-listed heat tape.** Wrap it according to the manufacturer's instructions and plug it in. Use only heat tape specifically listed for pipe freeze protection — never a general-purpose heating pad. **Do not overlap the tape over itself**, and never leave it unattended while it runs.
7. **Warm the surrounding area.** If the pipe runs through a cold cabinet or crawlspace, set a space heater several feet away to raise the ambient temperature, and open cabinet doors so warm room air reaches the pipe.
8. **Once full flow returns, keep the faucet running** for a minute or two to flush the line and confirm steady pressure.
9. **Inspect the entire exposed run for leaks.** Look closely at joints, elbows, and valves for drips, sprays, or new water stains. A burst pipe can be hidden behind a wall — check the wall and ceiling surfaces along the run too. If you find a leak, shut off the main water valve right away and call a plumber.

## Safety warnings

- **Never use an open flame.** Propane torches, kerosene heaters, charcoal grills, candles, or blowtorches near pipes are a top cause of winter house fires. The heat is too intense, it damages fittings, and it can ignite dust, insulation, or wood framing.
- **Keep electrical heating tools away from standing water.** Dry the work area before using a hair dryer or plugging in heat tape. If the floor around the pipe is wet, dry it first — water and electricity are a lethal combination.
- **Don't set a heat gun to high.** If all you have is a heat gun, use its lowest setting and keep it moving. High heat can melt PVC and PEX fittings or scorch nearby wood.
- **Never leave heat tape or a space heater unattended.** Check on them every 10–15 minutes.
- **Know where your main water shutoff is before you begin.** If thawing reveals a burst pipe, you'll need to cut the water in seconds, not minutes.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I pour boiling water on a frozen pipe?**
Not directly on the pipe — sudden extreme heat can crack the pipe or damage fittings, especially on PVC. The hot-towel method (towels soaked in hot, not boiling, water) gives you the same heat transfer at a much safer pace.

**How long does it take to thaw a frozen pipe?**
Typically 30 minutes to an hour for an accessible section with a hair dryer. Long frozen runs in exterior walls or far inside a crawlspace can take several hours. If there's no progress after 2 hours, call a plumber — the freeze may be somewhere you can't reach.

**Should I leave the faucet dripping to prevent freezing?**
Yes. A slow drip — roughly the width of a pencil lead — on the faucet served by the vulnerable pipe keeps water moving, which is the single most effective overnight freeze prevention during extreme cold. It costs pennies in water compared to a burst-pipe repair.

**Will a frozen pipe always burst?**
No. Many pipes freeze and thaw without damage, especially copper and PEX, which tolerate some expansion. Pipes burst when pressure builds between an ice plug and a closed faucet, so keeping the faucet open while thawing is the single most important protective step.

**What if the frozen pipe is inside a wall I can't access?**
Open the faucet, turn up the house heat, and place a space heater near the wall (at a safe distance). Infrared lamps aimed at the wall can help too. If the pipe doesn't thaw within a few hours, call a plumber — cutting open a wall to reach the pipe is a job for a pro.

**Is it safe to use a hair dryer on PEX or PVC pipes?**
Yes, on a low or medium setting, kept moving and held 6–8 inches away. These plastics soften under concentrated high heat, so never use a heat gun on high or hold the dryer in one spot. The pipe should feel warm to the touch, never too hot to hold.

## Prevention tips

- Insulate exposed pipes in crawlspaces, attics, basements, and garages with foam sleeves — a 10-minute job per run.
- Seal drafts: caulk cracks in exterior walls, add insulation around pipe penetrations, and close foundation vents in winter.
- Keep the thermostat at 55°F (13°C) or higher at all times during cold weather, even when you're away.
- Disconnect garden hoses and winterize outdoor faucets every fall — see [How Do I Winterize My Outdoor Faucet?](/posts/how-to-winterize-outdoor-faucet/).
- On the coldest nights, let vulnerable faucets drip slowly and open cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls.
- For chronically freezing runs, install UL-listed heat tape with a built-in thermostat as a permanent solution.

## When to call a pro

- You see or suspect a burst, split, or cracked pipe — shut off the main valve and call a plumber immediately.
- No water anywhere in the house (frozen main supply line or well line — beyond DIY scope).
- The frozen section is inside a finished wall or ceiling you can't reach.
- Two hours of careful thawing produces no result.
- The same pipe freezes repeatedly despite insulation — a plumber can reroute or add permanent heat protection.
- Any sign of significant water damage: sagging ceilings, soaked drywall, or standing water from a leak.
