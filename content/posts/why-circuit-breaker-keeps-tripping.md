A circuit breaker that keeps tripping is almost always doing its job: cutting power to protect you from an overloaded wire, a short circuit, or a ground fault. The key question is *which* of these it is. The most useful diagnostic clue is timing — **trips the instant you reset it** points to a short circuit (stop resetting and call an electrician), while **trips only when a certain appliance runs** points to an overloaded circuit you can often fix by redistributing the load.

Before you do anything else, reset it correctly: flip the breaker fully to OFF, then to ON. A tripped breaker usually sits in a middle position — pushing it straight to ON won't reset it. If it holds, you just experienced a one-time event. If it trips again, read on and diagnose before you reset it a third time.

## The 4 causes — and how to tell them apart by WHEN it trips

There are only four reasons a breaker trips, and each one has a distinct signature. The timing tells you which one you're dealing with.

**1. Circuit overload.** The most common cause by far. Too much current is flowing through the circuit — the total draw of everything plugged into it exceeds the breaker's rating (usually 15 or 20 amps). Signature: it trips when a high-draw appliance runs — a space heater, hair dryer, microwave, air conditioner — especially if several run at once. Resetting works fine until you plug the appliance back in. A related symptom worth reading about: [Why Does My Light Switch Buzz?](/posts/why-light-switch-buzzing/)

**2. Short circuit.** A hot wire touches a neutral wire directly — almost no resistance, enormous current, instant trip. Signature: **trips immediately** every time you reset it, even with everything unplugged. You may have heard a pop, smelled burning, or seen a flash at an outlet or fixture. This is the dangerous one — do not keep resetting it.

**3. Ground fault.** A hot wire touches ground — a metal box, a wet surface, the ground wire. Signature: trips quickly, often in kitchens, bathrooms, garages, basements, or outdoor circuits where moisture is present. On those circuits the [GFCI outlet usually trips first](/posts/why-gfci-outlet-keeps-tripping/), so if a *breaker* (rather than the GFCI) keeps tripping on a bathroom or kitchen circuit, the fault may be upstream of the outlets or in the wiring itself: [Why Does My GFCI Outlet Keep Tripping?](/posts/why-gfci-outlet-keeps-tripping/)

**4. Worn-out breaker.** Breakers are mechanical devices and they wear out, especially after years of trips. Signature: trips at lower loads than it should, or the handle feels loose, wobbly, or won't stay in the ON position. Sometimes the breaker itself is warm to the touch. Breakers in panels 25+ years old are prime suspects.

**The fifth pattern: random trips.** If it trips at random times with no obvious load pattern, suspect a **loose connection** somewhere — a wire that's barely making contact at an outlet, switch, or in the panel. Loose connections also cause AFCI (arc-fault) breakers to trip, since that's exactly what AFCIs are designed to detect. More on AFCI nuisance tripping below.

## The one-at-a-time appliance test

This five-minute test identifies the culprit when the timing suggests an overload. You need no tools — just patience.

1. **Reset the breaker and leave everything on that circuit unplugged.** Include the things you forget: garage freezers, dehumidifiers, power strips behind furniture. A breaker feeds outlets you may not associate with the room where the breaker labeled it.
2. **Plug in one device at a time, largest first.** Start with the biggest power hogs — space heater, window AC, hair dryer, microwave, toaster oven, treadmill.
3. **Turn each one fully on and wait a full minute** before adding the next. Some appliances trip only at startup (motors draw a surge) or after heating up.
4. **When the breaker trips, the last device you added is the suspect.** Confirm: unplug everything again, plug in only the suspect, turn it on. If it trips the breaker alone, you've found it. If it doesn't, the *combination* of devices is the problem.

**Reading the results:** One device tripping the breaker alone means the device is failing or the circuit feeds more outlets than you think. The device-plus-others combination means you're simply over the circuit's capacity — a redistributing problem, not a broken anything.

**A critical hard rule before we go further:** never "fix" a tripping breaker by replacing it with a bigger one. A 15-amp circuit has 14-gauge wire that will overheat on 20 amps — the breaker is matched to the wire, not to your needs. Upsizing the breaker without upsizing the wire is one of the most common causes of house fires.

## Space heaters and overloads: the dedicated-circuit fix

Space heaters are the single most common breaker-trippers in American homes. A typical portable heater draws 1,500 watts — about 12.5 amps on a 120-volt circuit. On a 15-amp circuit, that leaves only 2.5 amps for everything else on the same circuit: the TV, lamps, and the phone chargers tip it over.

Quick math that prevents most overload trips: **watts ÷ volts = amps**. Add up everything on the circuit. A 15-amp circuit should run no more than ~1,440 watts continuously (electricians use the 80% rule for sustained loads); a 20-amp circuit, ~1,920 watts.

Real fixes, in order of practicality:

- **Move the heater to a different circuit.** Kitchens and bathrooms usually have their own 20-amp circuits. Test with the one-at-a-time method.
- **Run the heater on low.** Most heaters have a 750-watt low setting — half the draw, often enough to take the edge off.
- **Have an electrician add a dedicated circuit** — the proper fix for a heater, window AC, or dehumidifier that lives in one spot every season. A dedicated 20-amp install typically runs **$250–$600** depending on panel access and distance.

## Hard stops: when to stop diagnosing and call an electrician

Reset the breaker at most **two or three times** while diagnosing — beyond that, repeated trips stress the wiring and the breaker itself. These signs mean stop immediately and call:

- **Burning smell** near the panel, an outlet, or a switch — even a faint hot-plastic odor
- **The panel cover or a breaker feels warm or hot** to the touch
- **Visible sparking**, scorch marks, or discoloration around an outlet, switch, or the panel
- **A buzzing or sizzling sound** from the panel that you can hear from a few feet away
- **The breaker trips instantly** every single time, with everything unplugged — likely a short in the wiring
- **Lights flicker or dim** on other circuits, which can indicate a loose main or neutral connection

**Everything past flipping a breaker belongs to the electrician.** Opening the panel cover, tightening lugs, replacing a breaker, or touching the neutral/ground bars is not DIY territory — the main lugs stay live even with the main breaker off, and an arc flash can cause serious burns in a fraction of a second. A service call to diagnose a tripping breaker typically costs **$150–$300**; a breaker replacement adds **$75–$200** in parts and labor.

## AFCI nuisance tripping in newer homes

If your home was built (or rewired) in roughly the last 15–20 years, many of your breakers are AFCI — arc-fault circuit interrupters, recognizable by the small test button on the breaker itself. They trip on the electrical signature of arcing, not just overload, which makes them excellent fire prevention and occasionally maddening.

Common AFCI nuisance triggers: older appliances with brushed motors (vacuums, treadmills), cheap LED bulbs, phone chargers, and dimmer switches on shared circuits. If an AFCI trips only with one specific device, that device's electronics are generating arc-like noise — it's almost always the device, not a wiring fault.

What you can do: replace suspicious cheap LED bulbs one at a time, and check that dimmer switches are rated for the bulbs they're controlling. What you should *not* do: swap the AFCI breaker for a standard one. Arc-fault protection is required by code in living areas for a reason, and removing it to silence nuisance trips trades a fire-safety device for convenience.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it safe to keep resetting a tripping breaker?**
Once or twice during diagnosis is fine. If it trips a third time, stop — repeated resetting wears the breaker and stresses wiring. Persistent trips need diagnosis, not patience.

**Why does my breaker trip when nothing is plugged in?**
Then the problem is in the wiring or the breaker itself: a short between wires, a nail through a cable, moisture in a box, or a worn breaker. Unplug everything, reset once — if it still trips with zero load, call an electrician.

**Can a bad appliance trip a breaker without being "broken"?**
Yes. A 1,500-watt heater on a 15-amp circuit shared with other rooms will trip it while working exactly as designed. The appliance isn't broken; the circuit is undersized for the job.

**Does a tripping breaker mean I need a bigger electrical panel?**
Usually not — one overloaded circuit doesn't mean the whole service is undersized. Consider an upgrade only if multiple circuits trip constantly, you're out of breaker spaces, or your service is still 60–100 amps — and that's an electrician's call.

**My breaker is warm but hasn't tripped. Is that OK?**
A slightly warm breaker under heavy load is normal; a hot one is not. If you can't comfortably hold your finger on it, or one breaker is noticeably warmer than its neighbors, that's a sign of a loose connection or failing breaker — call an electrician.

**How long do circuit breakers last?**
Roughly 30–40 years in normal service, less in panels exposed to heat or moisture, and less again for breakers that have tripped many times. Each trip wears the internal mechanism a little. If your panel is from the 1980s or earlier and breakers trip at light loads, age is a real suspect.

## Prevention tips

- **Learn your circuits.** Spend 20 minutes mapping which breaker feeds which outlets — label the panel clearly so diagnosis starts from knowledge, not guessing.
- **Respect the 80% rule.** Keep continuous loads under 80% of a circuit's rating: ~1,440 watts on 15-amp, ~1,920 on 20-amp circuits.
- **Give heaters and window ACs their own circuit** whenever possible — they're the two appliances most likely to overload a shared circuit.
- **Don't daisy-chain power strips**, especially with anything that heats. One strip per outlet, and never plug a heater into a strip.
- **Replace cheap or flickering LED bulbs** on AFCI circuits before assuming the breaker is at fault.
- **Have the panel connections checked by an electrician every 10 years or so** — loose lugs are a leading cause of the "random trip" pattern.

## When to call a pro

- The breaker trips instantly on reset with everything unplugged — likely a short in the wiring
- Burning smell, warm/hot panel cover, sparking, or scorch marks anywhere in the system
- Buzzing or sizzling audible from the panel itself
- Lights flickering or dimming on multiple circuits
- You need a breaker replaced, a dedicated circuit added, or anything inside the panel touched
- The panel is a known-problem brand (Federal Pacific, Zinsco) — these deserve a professional safety evaluation regardless of symptoms
