A squeaky door hinge is metal rubbing on metal — the hinge pin grinding against the knuckles as the door swings. The fix is almost embarrassingly simple: the right lubricant, applied in the right place. Five minutes, no disassembly required for most squeaks. For the stubborn ones that keep coming back, the pin-removal method below ends them permanently.

## Quick diagnosis: what kind of squeak is it?

- **Squeaks only when the door moves** → dry hinge pin. Lubricate it — see the 5-minute fix.
- **Squeak came back weeks after oiling** → you used the wrong lubricant (or the pin is corroded). See the pin-removal method and the lubricant guide.
- **Grinding or popping sound, door feels stiff** → the hinge may be bent, the pin corroded, or the door misaligned and binding. Lubricate first; if the stiffness remains, check hinge alignment.
- **Squeak is actually coming from the latch, not the hinge** → swing the door slowly and listen. If the noise happens as the latch bolt drags across the strike plate, lubricate the bolt and plate edge instead.

## The 5-minute fix (no disassembly)

1. **Protect the floor.** Lay a rag or paper towels under the hinge — lubricant drips.
2. **Apply lubricant to the top of the hinge pin.** With the door closed, squeeze a few drops of your chosen lubricant (see the guide below) right where the pin meets the top knuckle. Gravity does the work.
3. **Work the door.** Swing the door back and forth a dozen times to work the lubricant down between the pin and the knuckles.
4. **Wipe the excess.** Clean off any drips on the hinge, door, and frame — excess oil attracts dust and stains paint.
5. **Repeat for each squeaky hinge**, then wipe the pins' bottoms where dirty oil seeps out.

This solves the large majority of squeaks on the spot.

## The pin-removal method (for squeaks that keep returning)

If the squeak comes back within weeks, the pin is likely corroded or gummed with old lubricant residue. Taking the pin out lets you clean and lubricate it properly — a permanent fix.

1. **Close the door** and place a rag underneath to catch the pin.
2. **Drive the pin up.** Put a nail or nail punch under the pin head (the little cap at the bottom of the hinge) and tap upward with a hammer until the pin rises enough to grab with pliers. Some pins have a small cap on top — pop it off first.
3. **Pull the pin out** with pliers, twisting gently as you pull.
4. **Clean the pin.** Wipe off old gunk with a rag. If it is rusty or rough, polish it with fine steel wool (0000 grade) or 220-grit sandpaper until smooth. A rough pin will squeak again no matter how much oil you use.
5. **Lubricate generously.** Coat the entire pin with white lithium grease or silicone grease — not just a drop, a real coating. These stay put for years, unlike thin oils.
6. **Reinsert the pin.** Slide it back down through the knuckles and tap it home with the hammer (use the rag to protect the finish).
7. **Work the door and wipe clean.** Swing the door several times, then wipe away every trace of excess grease.

Do all the hinges on the door while you are at it — if one pin is corroded, the others are usually close behind.

## Which lubricant to use (and which to avoid)

| Lubricant | Verdict |
|---|---|
| White lithium grease | **Best for lasting fix.** Thick, stays in place for years, handles the pin-removal method perfectly. |
| Silicone spray/grease | **Excellent.** Clean, does not attract dust, safe on all finishes. |
| 3-in-1 oil / sewing machine oil | **Good for the quick fix.** Penetrates well; needs reapplication every year or so. |
| Petroleum jelly | **Decent in a pinch.** Works, but collects dust over time. |
| WD-40 | **Avoid as a lubricant.** It is a solvent and water displacer, not a lasting lubricant — the squeak returns in days, and it strips existing grease. (Fine for cleaning a pin before greasing it.) |
| Cooking oils (olive, coconut, vegetable) | **Avoid.** They go rancid, gum up, and attract dust. The classic "it worked for a week" mistake. |
| Graphite powder | **Okay for locks, messy for hinges.** Works but stains everything black. |

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Using WD-40 as the fix.** It quiets the squeak for a few days, then evaporates — and it washes away the grease that was actually protecting the pin. Use it to clean, not to lubricate.
- **Using cooking oil.** It works for a week, then turns gummy and the squeak comes back worse, now with rancid residue.
- **Removing all hinge pins at once.** Take out one pin at a time. With all pins out, a heavy door can shift or fall — and realigning it is a much bigger job than fixing a squeak.
- **Ignoring a bent hinge.** If the door binds or the hinge knuckles are visibly misaligned, no amount of oil fixes bent metal. Replace the hinge.
- **Lubricating the wrong thing.** Make sure the noise is the hinge, not the latch bolt dragging on the strike plate or the door rubbing the frame.

## Keeping hinges quiet

- A drop of silicone or 3-in-1 oil on each hinge pin once a year prevents squeaks from ever starting.
- In humid climates, hinges corrode faster — the pin-removal clean-and-grease every few years keeps them silent.
- When painting a door, mask the hinges or remove them. Paint in the knuckles is a classic cause of a "new" squeak after a paint job.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does my hinge still squeak after oiling it?**
Either the oil never reached the metal-on-metal contact (it sat on top of old gunk), the pin is corroded and rough, or you used something that evaporated (WD-40) or gummed up (cooking oil). Pull the pin, polish it smooth, coat it with lithium grease, and the squeak is gone for good.

**Can I use WD-40 on door hinges?**
As a cleaner, yes — it dissolves old gunk nicely. As the actual lubricant, no. It evaporates within days and the squeak returns. Follow any WD-40 cleaning with real grease or oil.

**Do I need to take the door off to fix a squeaky hinge?**
No. The pin-removal method works with the door hanging in place — just do one hinge at a time so the door never loses support.

**One hinge squeaks but the others are fine. Why?**
Hinges wear unevenly — the top hinge carries the most load and usually fails first. But lubricate all of them while you are at it; the rest are on the same timeline.

## When to call a pro

- The hinge is bent, the knuckles are cracked, or the screws will not hold (stripped holes in the frame) — a handyman can replace the hinge and repair the mounting in under an hour.
- The door binds or will not close properly even after lubrication — the issue is alignment, not lubrication (see our guide on doors that won't latch).
- Squeaking in a fire-rated door (garage-to-house doors): these have specific hinge and closer requirements — do not modify them casually.
