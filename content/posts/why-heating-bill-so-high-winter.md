If your heating bill spiked this winter and you haven't touched the thermostat, check fuel prices before you blame your furnace. The EIA's 2026–27 Winter Fuels Outlook (published October 6, 2026) projects heating oil up 21% this season — averaging $2,115 per household — and electricity up 4%, averaging $1,196. An oil-heated home can pay a fifth more than last winter with identical usage.

But prices are only half the story: a clogged filter, ducts leaking a quarter of your heat into the attic, or a heat pump stuck on backup heat can inflate your bill just as much. The seven fixes below address the house-side causes in order of impact.

## What the EIA's 2026–27 outlook means for your bill

The EIA's October 6, 2026 outlook projects the average U.S. household will pay this winter:

- **Heating oil:** up **21%**, averaging **$2,115** — the hardest hit, tracking crude prices. Northeastern states, home to most oil-heated households, feel it most.
- **Electricity:** up **4%**, averaging **$1,196** — relevant for baseboards, space heaters, and heat pumps on resistance backup.
- **Propane:** down **3%** — modest relief for rural tank customers.
- **Natural gas:** down **9%**, averaging **$640** — the cheapest major heating fuel this winter by far.

Build one habit: compare bills in **units, not dollars** — therms, gallons, kilowatt-hours. Flat usage with a higher bill means the market is your problem; usage that jumped with unchanged habits means the house is, and the fixes below pay off.

## Fix 1: Escape the auxiliary-heat trap (heat pump owners)

The priciest silent failure in a heated home. Heat pumps carry backup electric resistance heat — **AUX** or **EM** on the thermostat — costing two to three times as much per unit of heat as the pump itself. Meant for brief defrost or deep-cold assist, a malfunction can run it for hours daily with no clear warning.

**Warning signs:** "AUX HEAT" displayed constantly in mild weather above freezing; a spiked electric bill while the outdoor unit rarely runs; the thermostat left in "EM"/Emergency Heat mode; or an outdoor unit caked in ice that never defrosts.

**Check two things:** confirm the thermostat is on "Heat," not "Emergency Heat" — EM mode runs 100% resistance heat, the priciest way to heat a home. Then check the outdoor unit is clear of snow and ice with airflow around it. If the mode is normal, the unit is clear, and aux heat still dominates in moderate weather, call an HVAC tech — failed outdoor sensors, stuck relays, low refrigerant, or bad defrost boards are the usual culprits, all cheaper than another month of resistance-heat bills.

## Fix 2: Replace the dirty furnace filter

A clogged filter is the cheapest high-bill cause and the most ignored. Restricted airflow forces longer cycles, harder blower work, and a hotter heat exchanger — more fuel per degree of comfort. Severe restriction trips the high-limit switch, causing short cycling that wastes even more.

Can't remember the last change? It's overdue. Pull the filter and hold it to light — if light won't pass through the pleats, it was strangling your system. Our [furnace filter replacement guide](/posts/how-to-replace-furnace-filter/) covers finding the slot, reading the size, and the airflow arrow in five minutes. During heating season, check monthly and replace pleated MERV 8–11 filters every 60–90 days. A multipack costs less than one day of wasted heating.

## Fix 3: Seal ducts leaking heat into the attic

Per ENERGY STAR, the typical home loses **20–30%** of the air in its duct system to leaks and bad joints — up to a third of your paid-for heat warming the attic or crawlspace instead of your rooms.

**Telltales:** rooms that never warm up; dustier-than-normal rooms (leaky returns inhale attic dust); ducts in unconditioned spaces; air felt escaping at joints while the system runs.

**The fix:** brush mastic sealant over every accessible joint and seam, or use UL-181 foil tape — not cloth "duct tape," which fails within a few years. Then insulate ducts in unconditioned spaces. Unglamorous, but among the highest-return DIY energy projects. For ducts inside walls, a pro duct-blaster test quantifies leakage first.

## Fix 4: Fix your thermostat habits

Two habits drive most thermostat waste: setting temperatures higher than comfort needs, and "chasing" — cranking to 78 to heat faster (furnaces run at one speed, so this never works), then dropping it when the house overshoots.

**Setbacks that work:** **68°F while home and awake**, **60–65°F while asleep or away 8+ hours**. A sustained 7–10 degree setback trims heating costs noticeably — see our [thermostat winter setting guide](/posts/what-temperature-thermostat-winter/) for the math. A programmable or smart thermostat earns its keep through consistency: humans forget setbacks, schedules don't.

Heat pump owners, go easy: deep setbacks can trigger costly aux heat during recovery (Fix 1). Use a modest 2–4 degree setback or a smart-recovery thermostat that ramps gradually. And check the thermostat's location — one on an exterior wall or near a draft reads fiction, and your bills reflect it all winter.

## Fix 5: Air-seal the biggest leaks

Winter's stack effect turns your house into a chimney: warm air escapes through gaps up high, sucking cold air in down low. Sealing the big leaks is cheap and fast. In order of payoff:

1. **Attic hatch/pull-down stairs** — weatherstrip the frame, glue rigid foam to the panel.
2. **Recessed can lights** — old cans are chimneys into the attic. Use sealed LED retrofit kits or fire-rated covers.
3. **Pipe, wire, and chimney chases** — seal attic-entry gaps with fire-rated caulk or foam (metal flashing near flues).
4. **Exterior outlets and switches** — foam gaskets behind cover plates cost pennies.
5. **Windows and doors** — fresh weatherstripping, caulked exterior joints, door sweeps where daylight shows.

A tube of caulk, a few cans of foam, and a Saturday cover the worst of it.

## Fix 6: Check attic insulation depth

Sealing stops air movement; insulation slows heat conduction — and many homes lack both. The ten-second test: if attic joist tops poke through the insulation, you don't have enough. Most U.S. zones want R-38 to R-60 up there, roughly 10–14 inches of blown fiberglass or cellulose.

Topping up with blown cellulose is a cheap DIY win — stores often lend the blower free with a minimum bag purchase, and two people can do an average attic in a day. Don't block the soffit vents (attics need airflow against moisture), and wear a respirator, goggles, and full coverage — old insulation dust is miserable to breathe.

## Fix 7: Audit the rate, not just the house

Bills split into **supply** (the fuel) and **delivery** (getting it to you), and either can jump alone. An expired fixed-rate contract rolling onto a variable rate is a classic deregulated-market bill shock.

**The 10-minute audit:** compare usage units — not dollars — to the same month last year; check whether a fixed-rate contract expired; ask your utility for the cheapest plan (time-of-use, budget billing, low-income rates); in deregulated states, compare suppliers but read the fine print — teaser rates that spike after three months are common.

## Help paying the bill

If the bill is unaffordable even after the efficiency fixes, apply early — funds are first-come and run out mid-winter. **LIHEAP** (Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program) helps income-qualified households pay heating bills through state offices. The **Weatherization Assistance Program (WAP)** funds free professional air sealing, insulation, and furnace upgrades for qualifying homes — the permanent version of Fixes 5 and 6. And most utilities offer payment plans, winter shutoff protection, and hardship grants if you call and ask.

## Safety warnings

**Never seal combustion air vents** around gas appliances — they need makeup air; starving them risks carbon monoxide.

**Keep insulation and foam clear of hot surfaces** — recessed lights, flues, and chimneys need proper clearances and fire-rated materials.

**Don't set below 55°F in winter, even away** — one burst pipe erases years of setback savings.

**Smell gas or feel carbon monoxide symptoms** (headache, nausea, dizziness with heat running)? Leave the house and call your gas utility or 911 from outside.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why is my heating bill so high this winter compared to last year?**
Two forces: markets and your house. The EIA's 2026–27 outlook projects heating oil up 21% (averaging $2,115 per household) and electricity up 4% (averaging $1,196). House-side, the usual suspects are a dirty filter, ducts leaking 20–30% of heated air, a heat pump stuck on aux heat, and wasteful thermostat habits. Compare bills in usage units — not dollars — to separate the market's share from your house's.

**Is it cheaper to leave the heat on all day or turn it down when I leave?**
Turn it down. Reheating costing more than maintaining temperature is a myth — heat loss tracks the indoor-outdoor temperature gap, so a cooler house loses heat more slowly all day. Set back 7–10 degrees for 8+ hours to use measurably less fuel. Exception: heat pumps, where deep setbacks can trigger costly aux heat on recovery — use 2–4 degrees or smart recovery instead.

**Why does my heat pump's AUX heat keep coming on?**
Brief aux heat during defrost or extreme cold is normal; constant aux heat in mild weather isn't. Verify the thermostat isn't in Emergency Heat mode and the outdoor unit is clear of ice. If both check out and aux heat still dominates, call an HVAC tech — failed sensors, stuck relays, low refrigerant, or defrost-board failures are typical.

**Can a dirty furnace filter really raise my heating bill?**
Yes. Restricted airflow means longer cycles and harder blower work per degree of comfort, and can trip the high-limit switch into wasteful short cycling. A $10 filter swapped every 60–90 days is the cheapest bill-reduction measure in the house.

**How do I know if my ducts are leaking?**
Rooms that never warm up, dustier-than-normal rooms, ducts in unconditioned spaces, and air felt escaping at joints while the system runs. A pro duct-blaster test quantifies it; sealing accessible joints with mastic or foil tape is a high-return DIY job.

**Will a smart thermostat actually save money?**
Only through consistent setbacks — the savings come from the schedule, not the label. It pays off if it reliably drops temperatures 7–10 degrees during sleep and away hours, and heat-pump models with smart recovery avoid aux-heat spikes.

## Prevention tips

- **Fall tune-up** ($100–$150) catches the burner, capacitor, and airflow issues behind winter bill creep.
- **Stock a year's filters in October** — cheaper than one wasted month of heating.
- **Seal air leaks before adding insulation** — insulation over gaps underperforms.
- **Log usage monthly in units** — next winter's spike shows up in November, not January.
- **Lock in supply rates before heating season** where markets are deregulated.
- **Apply for LIHEAP in fall**, not February when funds run thin.

## When to call a pro

- Aux/emergency heat runs constantly in mild weather with a clear outdoor unit
- The furnace short-cycles even with a clean filter
- Suspected major duct leakage in inaccessible areas — get a duct-blaster test
- Attic insulation under ~10 inches and you want a pro blown install
- Bill jumped on flat usage and the utility can't explain the rate — an energy audit may find it
- Gas smell, carbon monoxide symptoms, or scorching near the furnace — urgent, not a bill question
