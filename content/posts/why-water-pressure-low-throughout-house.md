Low water pressure throughout the entire house — weak flow at every faucet, a shower that's more trickle than spray, a washing machine that takes forever to fill — almost always traces back to a **whole-house cause**: a partially closed main shutoff valve, a failing pressure-reducing valve (PRV), corroded galvanized supply pipes, or a problem on the municipal side. A $10 pressure gauge screwed onto a hose bib tells you in two minutes whether the problem is inside your house or out on the street.

Before touching anything, answer one question: is it **one fixture** or **every fixture**? That single fork saves an hour of wasted troubleshooting. If pressure is fine everywhere except one faucet, the culprit is local — a clogged aerator or blocked cartridge — and [Why Does My Kitchen Faucet Have Low Water Pressure?](/posts/why-kitchen-faucet-low-water-pressure/) walks through that fix. If it's low at every fixture, hot and cold, keep reading.

## The diagnostic fork: single fixture vs. whole house

**Single fixture = local clog.** Unscrew the aerator and run the water. If it blasts at full force without the aerator, soak the aerator in vinegar or replace it for a few dollars. Showerheads clog the same way with mineral scale.

**Whole house = upstream restriction.** When every fixture is weak on both hot and cold, the restriction sits upstream of where your plumbing branches out — at the main shutoff valve, the pressure-reducing valve, the service line from the street, or inside aging pipes.

**Hot-only, whole house = water heater.** If cold pressure is fine but hot is weak at every fixture, the restriction is in the water heater — sediment and scale, not supply pressure. That points to a water heater service, not a pressure fix.

## The hose-bib pressure gauge test: the $10 tool that settles it

Buy a **water pressure gauge with a hose-bib fitting** ($8–$15 at any hardware store) and screw it onto an outdoor hose bib or the water heater drain bib. Turn the bib on fully and read the gauge with no other water running — that's your **static pressure**.

- **Normal: 50–70 psi.** Where municipal systems and PRVs are designed to land.
- **Acceptable: 40–80 psi.** Below 40, something is restricting or failing; above 80 brings code violations, worn fixtures, and banging pipes.
- **Below 40 psi at the hose bib:** the problem is upstream — main valve, PRV, service line, or the city supply.
- **50+ psi at the hose bib but fixtures still trickle:** the restriction is inside your house's distribution plumbing — very often corroded galvanized pipes.

This test takes five minutes and converts guesswork into data. If you do nothing else in this article, do this.

## The whole-house causes, in likelihood order

### 1. A partially closed main shutoff valve

The most common and cheapest fix. Main valves get bumped during plumbing work or left half-open after a repair. Find yours — usually in the basement or crawlspace where the water line enters the house, or in a ground box near the curb.

**What to do:** open it fully. On a gate valve (round wheel), turn counterclockwise until it stops, then back it a quarter-turn so the stem never seizes. On a ball valve (lever handle), the lever should be parallel to the pipe. Cost: **$0**. If the valve is corroded and won't move, don't force it — a snapped stem means no water at all, and a main valve replacement runs **$200–$500** installed.

### 2. A failed or misadjusted pressure-reducing valve (PRV)

On municipal water, a **PRV** — a bell-shaped device right after the main shutoff — steps high city pressure down to house-safe levels. The internal spring and diaphragm wear out after roughly **10–15 years**, and a failing PRV chokes flow while showing low pressure downstream. A PRV with a ruptured diaphragm may also leak from its vent hole.

**How to recognize it:** pressure has declined gradually over months or years, the gauge reads low downstream of the PRV, and the utility reports no work on your street.

**The adjustment caution:** PRVs have an adjustment bolt and locknut under a cap. Clockwise raises pressure, counterclockwise lowers it — but **adjust in small increments** (quarter-turn at a time), re-check with your gauge, and give the system a minute to settle between turns. Never set your house above **80 psi** — plumbing code treats that as the ceiling, and over-pressure destroys fixtures and appliance valves. A new PRV runs **$60–$150** for the part, **$250–$500** installed — a fair "call the plumber" job, since mis-soldered joints on a main line are a flooding risk.

### 3. Corroded galvanized steel supply pipes

Homes built before roughly **1960** often have galvanized steel supply lines. Inside, the zinc coating breaks down over decades and **iron corrosion builds up like arterial plaque**, shrinking a 3/4-inch pipe's effective diameter to a fraction of its original size.

**How to recognize it:** pressure declining gradually over years, brown tint when you first turn on a tap, and visible dull-gray threaded pipes in the basement. The classic signature: the static-pressure gauge reads okay, but flow collapses under load — run the shower and flush a toilet at the same time.

**The reality check:** there is no real fix for corroded galvanized pipe. Chemical descaling is temporary and risks loosening debris that clogs fixtures. **Repipe is the real fix** — new copper or PEX supply lines. Budget **$2,000–$5,000** for the main runs, **$4,000–$12,000** for a full-house repipe depending on size and access.

### 4. Water softener, well pump, or municipal supply

Three less-common causes worth ruling out quickly. A **water softener** with fouled resin or a failing control valve restricts flow — bypass it temporarily and re-test; resin replacement runs **$300–$600** every 10–15 years. On a **private well**, a tired pump or waterlogged pressure tank is the usual suspect — the tank should cut in around **40 psi** and cut out around **60 psi** (tank **$300–$800**, pump **$1,000–$2,500**). And sometimes it's the **city**: main work or a partially closed street valve can drop neighborhood pressure for days. **Check with neighbors** — if they're low too, call the utility before spending a dollar on a plumber.

## PRV adjustment: the safe way

The PRV is a brass, bell-shaped fitting (typically 3/4-inch) on the main line right after the shutoff valve, with an adjustment bolt on top. Inside, a spring pushes against a diaphragm; over years the spring weakens and the diaphragm stiffens, so the valve delivers less and less — the slow decline most homeowners notice but can't explain.

**Adjustment rules:** target **55–65 psi**. Turn the bolt **a quarter-turn at a time**, clockwise to raise, counterclockwise to lower. Re-check with the gauge after each turn and wait a minute between turns. If the locknut is corroded or the bolt won't move, replace the PRV rather than forcing it.

Note the link to the sister problem: **high static pressure makes water hammer worse**. If your test comes back above 75 psi, adjusting the PRV down quiets the banging as well as protecting fixtures. [Why Are My Pipes Banging When I Turn Off the Water?](/posts/why-pipes-banging-water-hammer/) covers the hammer side of the story.

## The old galvanized pipe reality check

The most misunderstood — and most expensive — whole-house cause. Once the zinc coating is gone, rust builds inward for decades; a 50-year-old galvanized line can have less than half its original flow area.

**Telltale signs:** pressure declining gradually over years (not overnight), rust-colored water on first draw, threaded dull-gray pipes in the basement, and a static gauge that reads okay while flow collapses under load.

**Why there's no cheap fix:** interior corrosion is structural, not a clog. Flushing or chemical descaling buys months at best and can loosen rust chunks that clog fixtures. The real fix is a **repipe** — copper lasts 50+ years and costs more; **PEX** is flexible, cheaper, freeze-tolerant, and the common choice for repipes today. It's the only fix that permanently solves the rusty water too.

## When to call the city vs. a plumber

**Call the water utility when:** neighbors report the same low pressure, pressure dropped suddenly overnight with no work done in your house, or there's construction, hydrant work, or a main break on your street.

**Call a plumber when:** the gauge reads low and the city says their supply is fine, the main shutoff valve is stuck or corroded, the PRV needs replacement, you suspect galvanized pipes and want a repipe estimate (get three), or pressure is normal at the gauge but weak at fixtures and you can't find the restriction.

Tip: ask the utility to confirm the **static pressure at the meter**. If the meter reads 70 psi and your hose bib reads 35 psi, everything between those points — service line, main valve, PRV — is your side of the responsibility line, and that's your search area.

## Safety warnings

**Never exceed 80 psi at the house.** Plumbing code caps residential pressure at 80 psi — above that, appliance solenoid valves, toilet fill valves, and water heater relief valves take a beating. Always re-check with a gauge after touching a PRV.

**Don't force a stuck main valve.** Snapping a corroded gate-valve stem leaves you with no water and an emergency call. If it won't move with firm hand pressure, call a plumber — and make sure you know where your main shutoff is before you need it.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is normal water pressure for a house?**
50–70 psi is the normal range, with 55–65 psi as the practical sweet spot. Code treats 80 psi as the maximum; below 40 psi, you'll notice weak flow at every fixture.

**Why is my water pressure suddenly low everywhere?**
Sudden whole-house pressure loss points to something that changed: a partially closed valve, a failed PRV, or a municipal problem. Check with neighbors first — if they're affected too, call the city before calling a plumber.

**Can a bad water heater cause low pressure throughout the house?**
Only on the hot side. If cold pressure is fine but hot is weak at every fixture, the restriction is in the water heater — sediment or scale — not a supply pressure problem.

**How much does it cost to fix low water pressure?**
It depends on the cause: a partially closed valve costs nothing, a PRV replacement runs $250–$500 installed, and a full repipe for corroded galvanized pipes $4,000–$12,000. The $10 gauge test comes first because it tells you which bill you're facing.

**Is 100 psi water pressure dangerous?**
For your plumbing, yes. At 100 psi, appliance hoses, fill valves, and fixtures fail years early, and water hammer gets dramatically worse. If your gauge reads over 80 psi, your PRV is failing or missing.

**Do I need a plumber to adjust a PRV?**
Not necessarily — a careful homeowner can adjust one with a gauge and patience, a quarter-turn at a time. But if the PRV is old, corroded, or the bolt won't move, hire a plumber for the replacement.

## Prevention tips

- **Check your pressure once a year.** A $10 gauge and two minutes at the hose bib catch a drifting PRV before it becomes a symptom.
- **Exercise the main shutoff valve annually.** Turn it fully off and back on so it never seizes when you need it.
- **Flush the water heater annually.** Sediment is the hot-side equivalent of galvanized corrosion — a yearly drain keeps hot pressure up.
- **Clean aerators and showerheads yearly.** Soak them in vinegar to dissolve scale before minor buildup becomes a "low pressure" complaint at one fixture.

## When to call a pro

- The main shutoff valve is stuck, corroded, or leaking — forcing it risks a snapped stem and a flood
- The PRV needs replacement rather than adjustment (old, corroded, or non-responsive)
- The hose-bib gauge reads normal but fixtures trickle — the restriction is buried in the distribution plumbing
- You have galvanized steel supply pipes and want a repipe estimate — get three quotes, and ask about PEX vs. copper
- Any main-line work where a mistake means flooding the basement
