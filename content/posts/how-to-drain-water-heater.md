If your water heater rumbles, pops, or crackles when it heats up, that's **sediment** — minerals from your water that have settled into a crusty layer at the bottom of the tank. Draining the tank once a year flushes that sediment out, which quiets the noise, restores efficiency, and can add years to the heater's life. The whole job takes about **30 minutes of actual work** (plus draining time), one garden hose, and no special skills.

This guide explains why sediment is such a problem, how often to drain, the exact step-by-step for gas and electric heaters, what to do when the drain valve won't cooperate, how to refill without trapping air, and when to call a plumber.

## Why Draining Matters: The Sediment Problem

Every gallon entering your heater carries dissolved minerals — mostly calcium and magnesium. Heated, they precipitate out and sink. Over months and years they build into a thick sediment layer, sometimes several inches deep in hard-water areas.

That layer causes real problems:

- **Noise.** The burner or elements heat the sediment, and water trapped in it boils and bubbles up through the crust — the rumbling and popping. If your heater sounds like percolating coffee, sediment is why: [why your water heater is making noise](/posts/why-water-heater-making-noise/).
- **Wasted energy.** Sediment insulates. The burner heats through inches of mineral crust before heat reaches your water, so the heater runs longer and burns more fuel.
- **Tank damage.** The tank bottom overheats because heat can't transfer through the sediment. That weakens the steel and accelerates corrosion — the #1 killer of water heaters.
- **Reduced capacity.** Inches of sediment displace real water volume. A 50-gallon tank with heavy buildup holds meaningfully less hot water.
- **Shorter lifespan.** The average tank heater lasts 8–12 years. Regular draining is the biggest factor pushing toward the long end — well-maintained tanks can go 15+ years.

Sediment speed depends on water hardness. Very hard water lays down significant sediment in a single year; soft water builds it slowly. If you've never drained your heater and it's 5+ years old, expect a lot of gritty water on the first flush.

## How Often Should You Drain?

**Once a year** is the standard and the right cadence for most households. Put it on the same annual list as [replacing your furnace filter](/posts/how-to-replace-furnace-filter/) and testing your smoke detectors — fall is a natural time.

Adjust for your water:

- **Hard water (scale on faucets, spots on dishes):** every 6 months is worth it.
- **Water softener installed:** once a year is plenty.
- **Never drained and 10+ years old:** drain carefully, but disturbing a decade of sediment can reveal problems like a valve that won't reseal. Some plumbers advise leaving very old heaters alone until replacement.

## Tools and Materials

You need almost nothing:

- **A garden hose** long enough to reach from the drain valve to a floor drain, sump pit, utility sink, or outside. The water will be hot and dirty — a floor drain or driveway is ideal; avoid prized lawns.
- **A bucket** — for catching the first blast and testing clarity at the end.
- **Work gloves** — the valve and the first water out can be hot.
- **A flathead screwdriver** — most drain valves open with a screwdriver slot.
- **Towels or rags** — there will be drips.
- **A replacement drain valve (optional but smart):** a brass ball-valve drain valve costs **$10–$15** and is vastly better than the cheap plastic valves most heaters ship with. Buy one before you start — you may end up wanting it.

**Total cost: $0** if you own a hose.

## Safety First: Scalding and Power

Two hazards to respect:

1. **Scalding water.** The tank holds 40–50 gallons at 120–140°F. Wear gloves, keep kids and pets away, and point the hose discharge somewhere safe. **Let the heater cool before you start** — turn it off the night before, or run hot water (a shower, laundry) to draw the temperature down, then shut it off and wait a couple of hours. Draining a full-temperature tank is how people get hurt.
2. **Power and gas.** Electric: **turn off the breaker** — elements exposed to air burn out in seconds. Gas: turn the gas control to **PILOT** (or OFF per your manual).

As with any gas appliance: **if you smell gas at any point, stop, leave the house, and call the gas company from outside.**

## Step-by-Step: Draining the Tank

### Step 1: Shut off power or gas

Electric: flip the water heater's dedicated breaker to OFF — tape a note on the panel so nobody flips it back. Gas: turn the control knob to PILOT. This keeps the pilot lit (no relighting needed) while stopping the main burner.

### Step 2: Close the cold water inlet

On top of the heater, find the cold inlet pipe (usually a blue handle or marked "C"). Close that valve — quarter-turn: handle across the pipe; gate valve: clockwise until snug. This stops new water entering while you drain. If the inlet valve won't budge, don't force it — shut the house's main water instead.

### Step 3: Attach the hose to the drain valve

The drain valve sits near the tank's bottom — a small spigot, usually plastic, sometimes brass. Thread the hose on snugly by hand. Run the other end to a floor drain, sump pit, or outside — lower than the valve if possible, since gravity does the work. Secure the discharge end; a whipping hose makes a spectacular mess.

### Step 4: Open a hot water tap in the house

Open the **hot** side at the nearest sink and leave it open — this breaks the vacuum so the tank can drain. Without it, draining slows to a crawl.

### Step 5: Open the pressure relief valve

Find the temperature-and-pressure (T&P) relief valve with its small lever. Lift the lever briefly — you should hear air hiss in. This is a second vacuum-breaker and a check that the valve works. If it's stuck, or dribbles continuously afterward, note it — a failed T&P valve is a replace-it-now item (about **$15** for the part).

### Step 6: Open the drain valve and drain

Open the drain valve slowly — counterclockwise on the common plastic gate type. Hot, possibly discolored water starts flowing through the hose. The first few gallons are usually the dirtiest: cloudy, rusty-looking, gritty. That's the sediment leaving. Good.

A full 50-gallon tank takes **20–60 minutes** to drain by gravity. You don't need to stand there — check periodically.

### Step 7: Flush until the water runs clear

Once empty, open the cold inlet for 10–15 seconds with the drain still open. This stirs up remaining sediment and blasts it out. Repeat two or three times until the hose water runs clear — catch some in the bucket to check for grit.

This flush is what separates a real cleaning from just emptying the tank. The sediment that matters clings to the bottom; the stir-and-flush dislodges it.

### Step 8: Close everything up

Close the drain valve firmly — snug, not gorilla-tight. Remove the hose, drop the T&P lever if still lifted, and close the hot tap.

## What If the Drain Valve Won't Open or Clogs?

Old drain valves are the #1 headache in this job:

- **Valve won't turn:** Plastic gate valves seize with mineral buildup. Try gentle persuasion — not force. If the handle flexes or cracks, stop. Forcing it snaps the valve off, leaving an open hole and 50 gallons of water. At that point, call a plumber — they have the tools to handle it safely.
- **Opens but only dribbles:** Sediment is clogging the opening. Work the valve open and closed a few times to break it up. A straightened coat hanger gently poked into the opening (hose off, bucket ready, gloves on) can clear it.
- **Won't close afterward:** Try working it open and closed several times to seat it. If it still leaks, you need a replacement — a brass ball valve threads into the same opening. Since the tank is already empty, this goes from emergency to minor detour. This is why you bought that **$10–$15 brass valve** before starting.

## Refilling Without an Airlock

Refill in this order — wrong order traps air and can burn out electric elements:

1. **Confirm the drain valve is closed** and the hose is off.
2. **Open the cold inlet** fully.
3. **Leave that hot tap open.** Air escapes through it — you'll hear sputtering, then a steady stream. Once water flows smooth with no sputtering, the tank is full. Close the tap.
4. **Check for leaks** around the drain valve and inlet connections under pressure.
5. **Restore power or gas — only after confirming the tank is full.** Electric: breaker back on. An element exposed to air for even a minute can burn out. Gas: knob from PILOT to ON, set your temperature.

**Temperature setting:** 120°F is the recommendation — comfortable for showers and dishes, cool enough to prevent scalding and to slow sediment formation and corrosion. If yours was at 140°F, dropping to 120 saves a noticeable amount on energy.

Wait about an hour, then run hot water to confirm. Slight discoloration for the first few gallons is normal — residual sediment clearing the lines, harmless.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I drain it without turning it off?**

Don't. On electric heaters, exposed elements burn out in seconds once water drops below them. On gas, the burner firing against a partially empty tank overheats the steel. Shut off the heat source first — it's one switch.

**How long does the whole job take?**

20–60 minutes of draining for a 40–50 gallon tank, plus ~30 minutes of hands-on work and 10 minutes of flush cycles. Call it 90 minutes start to finish, most of it waiting.

**The water coming out is rusty — is my tank failing?**

Some rust color on the first flush is normal — it's sediment and scale, not necessarily tank failure. Concerning signs: rusty *hot* water at taps day-to-day, or rust that doesn't clear after flushing — the tank's lining may have failed.

**Should I drain a tankless water heater?**

Tankless units don't store water, so they don't accumulate sediment the same way — but they need **descaling** every 1–2 years in hard-water areas. That's a different procedure: a pump circulating vinegar or descaling solution through the unit. Follow the manufacturer's descaling procedure.

**Can I just drain a few gallons instead of the whole tank?**

A partial drain removes loose sediment but leaves the compacted bottom layer — the layer causing noise and efficiency loss. The full drain plus stir-and-flush is what actually cleans the tank. If you're going to bother, do the whole thing.

**My drain valve is plastic — should I replace it with brass?**

Yes, while you're at it. A full-port brass ball valve (**$10–$15**) drains faster, clogs less, and won't seize or snap. It's the best upgrade you can make to a tank heater, and it makes next year's drain a five-minute job.

## When to Call a Pro

Draining is DIY-friendly, but call a plumber when:

- **The drain valve is seized, snapped, or won't reseal.** A plumber replaces it without flooding your basement — the most common reason a DIY drain becomes a service call.
- **The T&P relief valve fails its test** — stuck shut or dribbling continuously. The T&P valve is the tank's last defense against overpressure; a failed one is a safety issue.
- **Water leaks from the tank itself** — seams, base, or fittings (not valves). A leaking tank can't be repaired; it's replacement time. Budget **$1,200–$2,500** installed for a standard tank, depending on size and local labor.
- **The heater is 12+ years old and never serviced.** A first-ever drain on a very old heater can disturb things best left alone. A plumber can assess whether a flush is safe or whether to plan the replacement.
- **You smell gas** around a gas heater. Leave the house and call the gas company from outside.

Thirty minutes, one hose, once a year — quieter operation, lower bills, a longer-lived heater. Put it on the calendar.
