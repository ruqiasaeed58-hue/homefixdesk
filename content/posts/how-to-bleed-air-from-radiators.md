Radiators hot at the bottom but cold at the top? That's trapped air. The air pocket sits at the top and blocks hot water from circulating through the upper section. The fix is called bleeding: open a small valve at the top corner and let the air hiss out until water flows steadily. About ten minutes per radiator, one two-dollar tool — a radiator bleed key — and the radiator heats evenly immediately.

This guide covers the full process for US homes with hydronic (hot-water) heating — cast-iron radiators, panel radiators, or hot-water baseboards: how to tell trapped air from sludge buildup (a very different fix), the exact bleeding sequence, restoring boiler pressure afterward, and what to do if bleeding doesn't solve it.

## The Cold-Top/Cold-Bottom Test: Air or Sludge?

Before you touch a bleed valve, run a quick diagnostic with the heating on and radiators fully warmed up. Run your hand along each radiator (careful — they're hot) and look for a pattern:

**Cold at the top, hot at the bottom = trapped air.** Air is lighter than water, so it collects at the top in a pocket hot water can't enter. Quick, nearly free fix — this is the bleeding scenario.

**Cold at the bottom, hot at the top = sludge buildup.** Iron-oxide sludge settles at the bottom over years, blocking water flow through the lower channels. Bleeding does nothing for sludge — it needs a professional power flush. If your radiators are cold at the bottom, skip the bleed key and call a heating tech.

**Cold everywhere = a different problem.** If a radiator never warms at all, check the valve at the base — it may be shut or seized. Thermostatic radiator valves (TRVs) can stick shut after a long summer off.

A radiator cold on one end or with patchy cold spots may have partial sludge or a flow-balance issue. Try bleeding first — it's free — but if the pattern doesn't change, you need a flush or a balancing adjustment by a pro.

## Why Air Gets Trapped in the First Place

Air enters a hot-water system in ordinary ways — trapped air isn't a sign of a major problem:

- **Recent draining or service.** Any work that opens the pipes lets air in, which migrates to the highest points (top-floor radiators) as the system runs.
- **Make-up water.** Boilers lose small amounts of water over time; fresh make-up water contains dissolved air that comes out of solution when heated.
- **Slow permeation.** Over years, tiny amounts of air seep in through pipe joints and the expansion tank's air side — faster in older systems.
- **New installations.** New radiators and pipework start full of air, and small pockets work their way to the radiators in the first weeks after commissioning.

If your radiators worked fine last winter and one or two are now cold on top, it's almost certainly just accumulated air. Bleed them and move on.

## Tools and Materials You'll Need

This is one of the cheapest DIY fixes in home heating:

- **A radiator bleed key (~$2).** A small T-shaped metal key that fits the square bleed nipple on top of the radiator, sold near the heating supplies at any hardware store. Some radiators take a flat-head screwdriver instead, but a proper key won't slip and strip the fitting.
- **A small cup or mug** to catch the water that follows the air.
- **An old towel or rag.** A little water always escapes, and radiator water is dark with iron oxide and stains carpet and paint.
- **Gloves (optional).** Radiator water is hot and dirty; work gloves keep the mess off your hands.

## Safety Before You Start

- **Let the system cool slightly first.** Run the heat to full temperature, then shut the boiler off and wait 20–30 minutes. Bleeding a pressurized, full-temperature boiler risks a faceful of near-boiling water; bleeding stone-cold doesn't move air as well.
- **The water stains.** Iron-oxide boiler water marks everything it touches. Keep the rag under the valve and don't bleed over light carpet or freshly painted walls.
- **Watch the boiler pressure afterward.** Bleeding drops system pressure — repressurize as described below. Low pressure starves the pump and can trip the safety lockout.
- **Don't force anything.** The bleed valve needs only a quarter to half turn. If it's seized, stop — snapping the nipple off means a flooded floor.

## Step-by-Step: Bleeding Your Radiators

### Step 1: Heat up, then shut the boiler off

Turn the thermostat up so the boiler fires and all radiators heat fully — about 20–30 minutes. Full-temperature circulation moves air to the bleed points. Then shut the boiler off and wait 20–30 minutes for safe temperature, with the circulator pump stopped (moving water interferes with bleeding).

### Step 2: Locate the bleed valves

The bleed valve is a small square-topped nipple at the top of the radiator, usually opposite the inlet pipe. On cast-iron radiators it's at the top of the last section; on panel radiators it's a small fitting on the top side panel. (Baseboards are covered below.)

### Step 3: Start with the lowest floor

Work bottom-up: ground floor first, then upstairs. Air rises, so bleeding lowest first keeps upstairs air out of radiators you've already done.

### Step 4: Open the valve and listen

Hold the cup under the valve with the rag behind it to catch spray. Fit the bleed key onto the nipple and turn slowly counterclockwise — a quarter to half turn. You'll hear a hiss as air escapes. Don't open it further; a wide-open valve sprays and is harder to close smoothly.

### Step 5: Close it at the first steady stream of water

When the hissing stops and a steady stream of water flows — even dark water — turn the key clockwise to close firmly. Don't overtighten; snug is enough.

### Step 6: Work through the house and watch the gauge

Repeat for every radiator, floor by floor, checking the boiler's pressure gauge every floor or two. US systems run at **12–15 psi** when cool. Below 12 psi, repressurize before continuing.

### Step 7: Repressurize the boiler

Find the fill (feed) valve on the water line into the boiler — there's usually a backflow preventer on the same line. Open it slowly and watch the gauge climb to 12–15 psi (the middle, ~13–14 psi, is ideal), then close it. UK guides quote 1–1.5 bar — same range, different units; US gauges read psi. Refilling every week or two isn't normal — it means a leak or a failed expansion tank, and a tech needs to find the cause.

### Step 8: Fire the boiler back up

Turn the boiler on, set the thermostat normally, and let the system run. Feel each radiator — they should heat evenly, top to bottom. Still cold on top after two rounds? It may have sludge — see below.

## Bleeding Hot-Water Baseboards

Many US homes have baseboard convectors instead of tall radiators — long, low units along the walls. Same principle, different fitting: baseboards usually have a small air vent (bleed screw) at one end of the fin-tube element, often under the end cap. Some take a bleed key; others take a flat-head screwdriver.

Pull off the end cap, hold a cup under the vent, and open it just like a radiator bleed valve. Baseboards hold far less water, so you'll hit the water stream in seconds. Because baseboard runs are long, air collects at the highest point of each loop — check every baseboard in the zone even if only one feels cold.

## What to Do If Bleeding Doesn't Help

Bleeding fixes trapped air. If you've bled twice and the problem persists, you're dealing with something else:

- **Sludge (cold bottoms).** A power flush pushes chemical cleaners through at high velocity — a half-day pro job, typically **$300–$600**. Afterward, ask about a magnetic system filter to capture sludge before it settles.
- **A stuck TRV or seized valve.** If one radiator stays cold, unscrew the thermostatic head and press the pin underneath — it should spring back. Free a stuck pin with penetrating oil; a fully seized one means replacing the valve body.
- **System balancing.** Hot radiators near the boiler and lukewarm ones far away mean the lockshield valves need rebalancing — a tech job done with temperature measurements.
- **A failing circulator pump.** Weak flow mimics air problems: slow-to-heat radiators, noisy pipes. A pump original to a 20-year-old boiler is a suspect.
- **Persistent pressure loss.** Constant refilling points to a leak or a waterlogged expansion tank. Bleeding manages the symptom; a tech finds the cause.

## Preventing Air Buildup

- **Bleed once a year** in early fall. Ten minutes per radiator prevents the mid-winter cold-top surprise.
- **Keep pressure correct.** Check the gauge monthly during heating season — stable 12–15 psi means less make-up water and less dissolved air entering the system.
- **Have the expansion tank checked.** A failed tank causes pressure swings that draw air through seals. A tech can recharge or replace it during an annual service — pair it with your yearly [furnace filter replacement](/posts/how-to-replace-furnace-filter/).
- **Fix leaks promptly.** Even a weeping valve union lets air in as water seeps out. A $15 valve repack now beats a system-wide air problem in January.
- **Consider an automatic air vent** at the system's high points. It bleeds small amounts continuously, so manual bleeding becomes a once-every-few-years task.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How often should I bleed my radiators?**

Once a year, in early fall, is enough for most systems. Bleed again mid-winter if a radiator goes cold on top. If you're bleeding the same radiator every few weeks, something is actively introducing air — likely a leak or a failed expansion tank — and it's time for a tech.

**Can I bleed radiators with the heating on?**

Bleed with the boiler off but the water still warm. Bleeding at full temperature with the pump running can spray hot water, and moving water makes it harder to get all the air out. Run the heat, shut the boiler off, wait 20–30 minutes, then bleed.

**Why is the water coming out of my radiator black?**

That's iron oxide — rust particles suspended in the water, normal in a steel-and-iron system. It stains fabric permanently, so keep a rag under the valve. Very thick, gritty black water that keeps coming can mean heavy sludge — a flush-not-bleed situation.

**Is it safe to bleed radiators myself, or should I call a pro?**

Bleeding is firmly DIY: a $2 key, no pipe work, minimal risk if you let the system cool first. Call a pro for seized bleed valves, dropping boiler pressure, radiators that stay cold after bleeding, or suspected sludge.

**What pressure should my boiler be at after bleeding?**

For US hydronic systems, 12–15 psi on a cool system. It reads a few psi higher when hot — normal expansion, not overpressure. Below 12 psi cold, open the feed valve and refill to the middle of the range.

**Do I need to bleed all my radiators or just the cold one?**

Bleed the cold one first, but do the whole house once you're set up — air migrates, and a fine radiator today can go cold on top next week. Floor by floor, lowest to highest, takes about an hour.

## When to Call a Pro

- **Seized bleed valves.** Gentle key pressure only — a snapped valve means a flooded floor.
- **Cold bottoms that don't respond to bleeding.** That's sludge; it needs a power flush.
- **Frequent pressure loss.** Refilling more than a couple of times per season means a leak or a bad expansion tank.
- **Noisy boiler or pump.** Kettling, banging, or a grinding circulator can mean the system needs a full service.
- **Any gas smell.** Leave the house and call the gas company from outside. Never work on gas fittings yourself.

A two-dollar bleed key and an hour each fall keeps hot-water radiators heating evenly for years. Now that you know the cold-top trick, you'll never pay a service call for trapped air again.
