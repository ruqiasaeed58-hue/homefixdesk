**Before you touch a single part, buy an $8 oven thermometer and hang it from the center rack.** Set the oven to 350°F, wait 20 minutes, and read the thermometer through the glass without opening the door. If it reads 325°F or 375°F, you've proved the problem is the oven — not your recipe. About **1 in 4 home ovens runs 25°F or more off** its dial setting, the single most common reason food burns on one side and stays raw on the other.

The thermometer test takes 20 minutes and costs less than a pizza. It turns a vague "my oven bakes weird" complaint into a specific diagnosis, and it tells you whether a free five-minute recalibration will fix everything. Work through the causes below in likelihood order only after the test.

## Cause #1: A dying bake element (electric ovens)

The **bake element** — the metal coil at the bottom of an electric oven — produces the heat. When it's failing, one section glows orange while another stays dark, so one side of the rack cooks faster than the other.

**How to check:** turn the oven on and watch the element through the door glass. A healthy element glows a steady, even orange-red across its full length. A failing one shows **dark patches, blistering, flaking, or a visible break**.

**The fix:** bake elements are one of the cheapest, easiest oven repairs — the part runs **$20–$50** and swaps out with two screws and a wiring plug. **Flip the breaker off before touching anything.**

## Cause #2: A calibration offset — the oven lies about its temperature

Even with a perfect element, most ovens don't hit exactly the temperature they display. Manufacturers allow a tolerance of about ±25°F, and surveys consistently find roughly a quarter of home ovens running **25°F or more off** the dial.

**How to check:** this is exactly what the thermometer test reveals. If the oven holds steady at 325°F when set to 350°F, it's not broken — it's just miscalibrated. A consistent offset is good news: the oven is stable, just dishonest.

**The fix:** nearly every modern oven lets you recalibrate — usually by holding the "Bake" or "Settings" button until the display shows a calibration or offset mode, then dialing in the difference. The whole process takes about **5 minutes**.

## Cause #3: Wrong rack position

Heat rises, and oven geometry punishes the wrong rack slot. Food too close to the bake element gets scorched bottoms while the top stays pale; too close to the broil element burns tops before the center cooks through.

**The rule:** the **middle rack** is the default for nearly everything — cakes, casseroles, sheet-pan dinners — because radiant heat from the top and bottom elements balances there. Move to the **upper third** for browning (roasting vegetables, finishing a casserole) and the **lower third** for cooking the bottom through (pizza, pie bottoms).

## Cause #4: Overcrowding and blocked airflow

An oven heats evenly only if hot air can circulate around the food. Cramped racks and pans that nearly touch the walls create dead zones and hot spots.

**The fixes that cost nothing:**
- **Leave 1–2 inches of air space** around every pan, on all sides
- **Don't cover two racks with pans** — if you must, stagger them so they're offset, never stacked directly above each other
- **Skip dark-coated pans for delicate baking** — they absorb radiant heat faster and over-brown bottoms; light-colored aluminum bakes most evenly
- **Remove anything stored in the oven** — an unused baking stone is a giant heat sink disrupting airflow
- **Don't tent with foil mid-bake** unless the recipe calls for it — foil blocks radiant heat and creates a cold spot underneath

## Cause #5: A worn or damaged door gasket

The **door gasket** — the heat-resistant seal around the oven door — keeps heat in. When it flattens, cracks, or pulls loose, hot air leaks out the front, the front runs cooler than the back, and temperatures swing as the element cycles harder to compensate.

**How to check:** with the oven cool, run your finger along the gasket, looking for **flattened sections, cracks, fraying, missing chunks, or spots pulled out of their channel**. Or hold a lit match near the door edges with the oven on — smoke blown outward reveals a leak.

**The fix:** replacement gaskets cost **$20–$60** and clip into the channel by hand — just buy the one for your exact model.

## Cause #6: A weak or dead convection fan (convection ovens)

If your oven has a **convection mode** and still bakes unevenly, the fan that makes convection work may be dying. The fan circulates hot air to eliminate hot spots; when the motor weakens or the blade cakes with grease, circulation fails.

**How to check:** turn convection on with the oven cold and listen. You should hear the fan start. Silent, squealing, or intermittent means the motor is failing.

**The fix:** a convection fan motor runs **$40–$100**, and replacement usually involves removing the oven's rear panel. That's a comfortable DIY job on a freestanding range, but on a wall oven (which must be pulled from the cabinet) most people hand it to a tech.

## Electric vs. gas: different hardware, same symptoms

The causes above were written with **electric ovens** in mind. **Gas ovens** get uneven heat from different hardware:

- **Weak igniter.** The igniter glows to light the burner; as it ages, it glows longer before lighting, and a slow-to-light burner gives uneven heat for the first several minutes. Replacement igniters run **$25–$60**.
- **Clogged burner ports.** Spills and corrosion clog the small flame holes, so the flame burns in patches. With the gas off, scrub the burner with a stiff brush and clear each port with a pin.
- **No user calibration on some models.** Many basic gas ovens lack electronic offset adjustment — if the thermometer test shows a steady offset, compensate by setting the dial differently (e.g., set 375°F to get 350°F).

**Gas safety is non-negotiable: if you smell gas — a rotten-egg or sulfur odor — at any point, stop what you're doing, don't touch any electrical switches, leave the area, and call your gas company or 911 from outside the house.**

## The 5-minute calibration fix, step by step

If the thermometer test showed a consistent offset, do this before ordering any parts:

1. **Look up your oven's calibration mode.** Search "[your brand and model] oven calibration" — most brands use a hold-the-bake-button sequence, but the exact one varies.
2. **Enter the offset mode.** The display will show "0" or "CAL" with up/down arrows.
3. **Dial in the difference.** If the thermometer read 25°F low at a 350°F setting, add +25°F. Most ovens allow offsets of ±35°F.
4. **Save and exit.** The oven remembers the offset through power outages.
5. **Re-test with the thermometer.** Run the 20-minute test again and confirm the oven now holds within a few degrees of the set temperature.

If the oven won't hold a steady temperature at all — swinging 40–50°F up and down — that's not calibration; it's a failing **thermostat or temperature sensor**, a $25–$60 part on many models but usually a tech job.

## Loading habits that cause hot spots

Even a perfectly calibrated oven bakes unevenly if it's loaded wrong:

- **Rotating nothing, ever.** If you know your oven runs hot in the back (most do), rotate pans 180° halfway through. It costs 10 seconds and solves half of all uneven-browning complaints.
- **Opening the door to peek.** Every peek drops the temperature 25–50°F, and the element then blasts heat to catch up — that's how tops burn while centers stay raw. Use the oven light and the glass.
- **Baking in a barely-preheated oven.** The beep means the air hit temperature; the walls haven't caught up. Wait a full 20 minutes after the beep for baking.
- **Ignoring the hot spot map.** Bake a tray of white bread slices spread across a rack for 10 minutes — the browning pattern is your oven's fingerprint. Position pans around the hot corner.
- **Using convection for everything.** Convection over-browns delicate cakes and soufflés. Switch it off for fragile bakes.

## When parts are worth replacing vs. calling a tech

**Worth doing yourself (breaker off, or gas off for gas ovens):**
- Bake element — $20–$50, two screws, the classic DIY oven repair
- Door gasket — $20–$60, clips in by hand
- Thermometer test and recalibration — $8 and 5 minutes, always first
- Burner port cleaning (gas) — free, just a brush and a pin

**Call a tech (typically $100–$200 for the visit plus parts):**
- The oven won't hold a steady temperature — thermostat, sensor, or control board
- Convection fan motor on a wall oven — it must be pulled from the cabinet
- Any gas igniter replacement if you're not fully comfortable around gas valves
- Error codes, a tripped breaker, or anything smelling of burning plastic or gas
- The oven is under warranty — DIY repairs can void it

**Replace the oven instead:** if it's 15+ years old and needs a control board ($150–$300+ installed), or the repair quote exceeds half the cost of a new range. A basic electric range starts around $500–$700.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it normal for an oven to be off by 25 degrees?**
Unfortunately, yes. Manufacturers allow roughly ±25°F of tolerance, and about 1 in 4 home ovens runs at least that far off. The thermometer test tells you exactly where yours stands.

**Why does everything burn on the bottom of my oven?**
Three usual suspects in order: the rack is too low (move it to the middle), the oven is running hot (thermometer test + recalibration), or you're using dark-coated pans that absorb extra radiant heat. Switch to light-colored aluminum and re-test.

**Should I use convection or regular bake for even cooking?**
Convection is more even for multi-rack baking and roasting because the fan circulates hot air. But it runs effectively 25°F hotter than conventional bake — reduce the recipe temperature by 25°F and check doneness early. For delicate cakes, conventional bake is gentler.

**My oven is brand new and bakes unevenly — is it defective?**
Probably not. New ovens still ship within the ±25°F tolerance and need several full heat cycles to settle in. Run the thermometer test; recalibrate if needed. If it's consistently more than 50°F off or won't hold temperature, that's a warranty claim.

**Can a dirty oven cause uneven heating?**
Yes. Heavy grease buildup insulates some spots and absorbs radiant heat in others. A self-clean cycle or manual deep clean ($10–$15 for oven cleaner) restores even heat transfer.

**How often should I replace the oven door gasket?**
There's no fixed schedule — inspect it yearly. Gaskets typically last 8–12 years. If you feel heat escaping around the door or the oven takes longer to preheat, check the gasket first.

## Prevention tips

- **Run the thermometer test once a year** — a 20-minute check catches calibration drift before it ruins a holiday dinner
- **Keep the door gasket clean** — wipe it down when you clean the oven; grease-soaked gaskets fail faster
- **Don't slam the oven door** — it loosens the gasket and misaligns the latch
- **Clean spills promptly** — baked-on spills clog gas burner ports and insulate electric elements
- **Preheat fully** — wait 20 minutes past the beep before baking; the beep means the air is hot, not the walls
- **Use the right rack and the right pan** — middle rack default, light-colored aluminum for even baking
- **Rotate pans halfway** through long bakes if you know your oven's hot spot

## When to call a pro

- The oven won't hold a steady temperature — thermostat, sensor, or control board failure
- You smell **gas** — leave the area and call the gas company or 911; never a DIY fix
- The convection fan motor needs replacing on a wall oven
- Error codes, a tripped breaker, or anything smelling of burning plastic
- The repair quote exceeds half the cost of a new range — replace instead
- The oven is under warranty — let the manufacturer handle it
