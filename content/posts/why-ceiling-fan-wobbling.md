A ceiling fan that wobbles looks alarming — and if it wobbles badly enough, it can loosen its mount over time. But most wobbling has a boring, fixable cause: a loose screw, a blade that got bumped, or normal imbalance. Work through this checklist from easiest to hardest, and the wobble usually disappears.

## Safety first

- **Turn the fan off and let the blades stop completely** before touching anything.
- If you need to work on the electrical box or downrod mount, **turn off the breaker** for that circuit, not just the wall switch.
- Never operate a fan that wobbles violently or where the mounting bracket visibly moves — tighten the mount first or call an electrician.
- Use a stable step ladder, not a chair.

## Quick diagnosis: what kind of wobble?

- **Wobble that started suddenly** → something came loose. Check blade screws and the downrod mount (Steps 1–2).
- **Wobble from day one of installation** → blades were never balanced or a blade bracket is bent. Go to Steps 3–4.
- **Wobble plus clicking or scraping** → a blade is hitting something, or a wire is touching a blade. Stop the fan and look (Step 3).
- **Wobble only on high speed** → classic imbalance — a balancing kit fixes it (Step 4).

## Step 1: Tighten every screw

Vibration loosens screws over time — this fixes more wobbles than anything else.

1. **Blade screws:** each blade attaches to a bracket with 2–3 screws. Tighten them all firmly (do not over-tighten to the point of cracking the blade).
2. **Blade bracket to motor:** the brackets attach to the motor housing — tighten those too.
3. **Downrod set screws:** where the downrod meets the motor, there is usually a pin and set screw. Make sure the pin is fully seated and the set screw is tight.
4. **Canopy screws:** the decorative cover at the ceiling hides the mounting bracket. Slide it down (it usually unscrews or has small screws) and tighten the mounting bracket screws into the electrical box.

Run the fan. If the wobble is gone, you are done.

## Step 2: Check the mounting box

This is the safety-critical one. A ceiling fan must hang from a **fan-rated electrical box** — a box marked "suitable for fan support." Standard light-fixture boxes are not rated for a spinning fan's weight and vibration.

1. With the breaker OFF, slide the canopy down and look at the box.
2. The box should be fan-rated and firmly attached to a joist or brace — no flexing, no cracked plaster around it.
3. The mounting bracket should be screwed tight to the box with no play.

If the box moves when you push it, or it is a thin plastic light box, **stop and call an electrician.** A fan hanging from the wrong box is a genuine fall hazard, not a DIY balancing job. Installing a fan-rated brace box costs a pro about $150–$250.

## Step 3: Inspect the blades

1. **Look for damage.** A blade that got hit (moving furniture, tall person) may be cracked or warped. Hold a yardstick up to each blade tip as it passes and note the height — all tips should track within about 1/8 inch of each other.
2. **Check blade brackets.** Brackets bend easily. If one blade sits noticeably higher or lower than the others, gently bend its bracket back so the blade aligns. Small adjustments make a big difference.
3. **Clean the blades.** Dust buildup is uneven and adds real imbalance, especially on the leading edges. Wipe all blades with a damp cloth.
4. If a blade is cracked or warped beyond straightening, replace the whole blade set ($20–$40) — mismatched blades wobble.

## Step 4: Balance the blades

If everything is tight and straight but it still wobbles, the blades are simply imbalanced. A balancing kit ($5–$10 at any hardware store) fixes this.

1. **Find the heavy blade.** Clip the kit's plastic balancing clip onto the trailing edge of one blade, about halfway out from the motor. Run the fan and note the wobble. Move the clip to each blade in turn — the blade where the clip *reduces* the wobble most is the culprit.
2. **Fine-tune the position.** Slide the clip along that blade (toward the tip = more effect, toward the motor = less) until the wobble is minimal.
3. **Add the weight.** Peel a self-adhesive weight from the kit and stick it on top of the blade, centered where the clip sat. Remove the clip.
4. **Re-test.** Run the fan on all speeds. A tiny wobble on high is normal; the fan should run essentially smooth.

Two notes: never stack multiple weights on one blade (if one does not work, the blade is bent — go back to Step 3), and balance with the light kit installed if the fan has one, since it affects the balance.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Ignoring a violent wobble.** A fan that visibly shakes its mount is a safety issue, not a cosmetic one. Fix it or stop using it.
- **Overtightening blade screws.** Snug is right; cracking the blade means buying a new set.
- **Balancing before tightening.** No amount of balancing fixes loose screws. Always tighten first.
- **Using a non-fan-rated box.** The #1 real danger with ceiling fans. If you are unsure, have an electrician check.
- **Bending blades instead of brackets.** Adjust the metal bracket, never the blade itself.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a wobbling ceiling fan dangerous?**
A mild wobble is usually just annoying. A severe wobble that moves the mounting bracket can loosen the mount over time — tighten everything, and if the bracket still moves, stop using the fan until a pro checks the box.

**Why does my new ceiling fan wobble?**
New fans wobble because blades were never balanced at the factory and bracket alignment varies. Tighten everything, align the blades, then balance with a kit.

**Can I run a wobbling fan on low speed only?**
Temporarily, yes — lower speed means less vibration. But treat it as a stopgap while you fix the cause, not a solution.

**How much wobble is normal?**
A tiny, barely-visible wobble on high speed is normal for most fans. If you can see it clearly from across the room or hear it, it needs attention.

## When to call a pro

- The mounting box is not fan-rated, moves, or the ceiling around it is cracked — an electrician should install a proper fan brace box.
- The wobble persists after tightening, straightening, and balancing — the motor shaft itself may be bent.
- You see scorched wiring, smell burning, or the fan hums loudly without spinning properly — electrical issue, stop using it.
- The fan is very old and the motor bearings are worn (grinding noise) — replacement ($100–$300 installed) beats repair.
