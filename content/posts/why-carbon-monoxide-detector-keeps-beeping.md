The beep pattern is your detector's diagnostic language, and learning to read it is a genuine safety skill. **One short chirp every 30–60 seconds means a low battery. A chirp every 15 minutes, an "ERR" display, or a voice saying "replace" means the detector has reached the end of its life (5–10 years). Continuous bursts of four loud beeps, repeating, mean possible carbon monoxide — get everyone outside immediately and call 911 from fresh air.** The first two are maintenance. The third is an emergency, and you must treat it like one even if you feel fine.

## The beep-pattern decoder

Stop what you're doing for a minute and listen to the exact pattern. Everything about your response follows from it.

### Pattern 1: One chirp every 30–60 seconds — low battery

This is the most common cause, especially as temperatures drop in fall. Like smoke detectors, CO alarms chirp when the battery voltage falls below the unit's threshold — and cool night air drops battery voltage, which is why the chirping so often starts at 2 a.m. **Fix:** replace the battery with the exact type the unit requires (usually 9V or AA — check the compartment), press and hold the test button for 15–30 seconds to reset, and listen for a day to confirm the chirp is gone. Never just remove the battery and leave the detector disabled.

### Pattern 2: Beeps every ~15 minutes, an "ERR" code, or a voice warning — end of life

CO sensors degrade over time, and the whole unit expires after **5–10 years depending on the model** (check your manual). When the sensor dies, the detector announces it: many units chirp 3–4 times every 15 minutes, flash an error code, or in voice models literally say "replace carbon monoxide alarm." **Fix:** there is no fix — the sensor itself is spent. Replace the entire detector. Check the **"replace by" date printed on the back of the unit** when you install the new one, and write it on the front in marker. A chirp from a 9-year-old unit will never be a battery problem for long.

### Pattern 3: Continuous 4-beep bursts, repeating — possible CO emergency

This is the alarm, not a maintenance signal: **four loud beeps, a pause, four beeps, repeating**. If you hear this, do not investigate, do not open windows first, and do not assume it's a false alarm.

1. **Get everyone outside into fresh air immediately** — including pets. Move quickly but don't run; if anyone is dizzy or confused, help them out.
2. **Call 911 from outside.** Fire departments carry CO meters and will check the home for you.
3. **Do NOT re-enter** until the fire department says it's safe — even to grab a phone, keys, or the dog.
4. Do not open windows before leaving. Ventilating sounds helpful, but it **wipes out the evidence the fire department needs** to find the source, and your priority is getting out, not troubleshooting.

Carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless — you cannot tell it's there by looking or smelling. Headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, and flu-like symptoms without fever are the classic warning signs, but by the time symptoms hit, the exposure is already serious. The alarm is designed to sound long before you feel anything, which is exactly why a 4-beep alarm must be believed.

## Low battery vs. dying sensor: how to tell quickly

When you get a maintenance chirp, flip the unit over before buying anything. If the **replace-by date is near or past**, skip the battery and replace the detector — a new battery in a dying sensor buys you days, not years. If the date is years away, it's a battery issue. Also check for a **dust-clogged sensor**: vacuum the unit's vents gently with a soft brush attachment, since dust on the sensor can cause erratic chirping that mimics a dying unit.

Note the distinction from smoke alarms: smoke detectors use three-beep alarm bursts (T-3), while CO alarms use four (T-4). If you hear four beeps and the unit says CO, believe it. If you're unsure which alarm is sounding, check our guide on [why your smoke detector keeps chirping](/posts/why-smoke-detector-chirping/) — but when in doubt about a possible CO alarm, evacuate first and sort it out from outside.

## Where CO detectors must go (and the 2 spots everyone gets wrong)

Placement determines whether the alarm can save you. Follow these rules:

- **One on every level of the home**, including the basement.
- **Outside every sleeping area** — in the hallway within about 15 feet of bedroom doors, so the alarm can wake you.
- **Near (but not inside) the garage** — place one in the room adjacent to an attached garage, not in the garage itself.
- Mount at **knee to chest height or per the manual** — unlike smoke, CO mixes evenly with air, so height matters less; the key is proximity to sleeping areas and fuel-burning appliances.

The two spots people get wrong:

1. **The kitchen.** Cooking fumes, steam, and heat from the stove cause nuisance alarms and can damage the sensor over time. Keep CO detectors **at least 15 feet from the stove** — never directly in the kitchen.
2. **The bathroom.** Steam and humidity cause false alarms and sensor degradation. Never install one in a bathroom, and keep it away from the bathroom door's steam path.

Also avoid the garage itself (exhaust fumes trigger nuisance alarms), right next to a fireplace or furnace (heat and combustion byproducts), and anywhere that gets below freezing or above 100°F, which degrades the sensor.

## The winter CO source checklist

Heating season is CO season. Fuel-burning appliances that are perfectly safe when maintained and vented become dangerous when something goes wrong. Check these:

- **Furnace:** a cracked heat exchanger can leak CO into the ductwork. Annual furnace service is the single most important CO prevention step — have it inspected before the first cold snap.
- **Gas water heater:** check that the vent pipe is intact, properly sloped upward, and not blocked (bird nests and ice are common blockers).
- **Portable generator:** **never run a generator inside the house or garage** — not even with the door open. It must be at least 20 feet from the house, with exhaust pointed away from windows and doors. Generator CO poisoning spikes every winter storm season.
- **Car idling in an attached garage:** even with the garage door open, exhaust can seep into the house. Pull the car out before warming it up, and never idle in a closed or attached garage.
- **Gas stove used as a heater:** never use the oven or stovetop to heat the house — ovens are not vented and produce CO during extended use.
- **Blocked chimney or flue:** have the fireplace chimney inspected annually; a blocked flue sends combustion gases back into the room.

If the fire department ever finds CO in your home, they'll identify the source — but this checklist is what to inspect proactively every fall.

## The Daylight Saving hook: change your clocks, change your batteries

Daylight Saving Time ends on **November 1, 2026** — and fire-safety campaigns have used the clock change as a battery-swap reminder for decades: *"change your clocks, change your batteries."* It's the simplest maintenance system that actually works. Twice a year, when you adjust the clocks, walk through the house and swap the batteries in every smoke and CO detector, press every test button, and check the replace-by dates. Ten minutes, twice a year, and you'll almost never be woken by a 2 a.m. chirp — or worse, find out too late that a dead detector missed a real emergency.

## Costs: what a CO detector costs vs. what it protects

A battery-powered CO detector costs **$25–$50**; a plug-in model with battery backup runs **$35–$70**; a combination smoke + CO unit is **$40–$80**. For a typical two-story home you need three to four units, so full coverage is roughly **$100–$250** — a one-time cost every 7–10 years. Hardwired interconnected models cost more ($50–$100 each plus electrician labor) but sound every alarm in the house at once, which matters when a basement furnace leaks CO while everyone sleeps upstairs. There is no cheaper life-safety device in your home.

## Prevention: the annual 10-minute routine

1. **Test every detector monthly** — press and hold the test button until it sounds the full alarm pattern. Monthly, not yearly.
2. **Vacuum the vents** when you test — dust is the main cause of erratic chirps.
3. **Swap batteries at each clock change** (March and November).
4. **Check replace-by dates** at the November swap and replace any unit within a year of expiry.
5. **Schedule annual furnace and chimney service** before heating season.
6. **Never bring outdoor fuel-burning equipment indoors** — generators, grills, and camp stoves stay outside, always.

## When to call a pro

- The 4-beep alarm has sounded: **call 911 first**, then have an HVAC technician find and fix the source before you re-occupy the home.
- **Any fuel-burning appliance** shows warning signs: soot around the furnace, a yellow or flickering burner flame (gas flames should be steady blue), rust or water stains on the vent pipe, or a pilot light that keeps going out.
- You need **hardwired interconnected detectors** installed — that's electrician work, roughly $100–$200 per unit installed.
- You smell gas (rotten-egg odor): that's natural gas, not CO — leave immediately and call the gas company's emergency line from outside.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What does one chirp every 30 seconds mean on a carbon monoxide detector?**
It means the battery is low. Replace it with the exact battery type the unit requires, then press and hold the test button for 15–30 seconds to reset. If the chirp continues after a fresh battery, check the replace-by date on the back — a unit near its 5–10 year expiry chirps the same way when the sensor itself is dying.

**How do I know if my CO detector is expired?**
Look for the "replace by" date printed on the back or side of the unit — CO detectors expire 5–10 years after manufacture, and the sensor degrades whether or not the battery is fresh. Units near expiry typically chirp 3–4 times every 15 minutes, show an "ERR" code, or (on voice models) announce that the alarm needs replacing. An expired detector cannot be repaired; replace the whole unit.

**Should I open windows if my CO detector goes off?**
No — not before evacuating. Get everyone outside into fresh air first, then call 911 from outside, and do not re-enter until the fire department clears the home. Opening windows before you leave delays evacuation and destroys the CO readings firefighters need to locate the source. Ventilation is the fire department's job, after everyone is safe.

**Where should I not put a carbon monoxide detector?**
Don't put one in the kitchen (within 15 feet of the stove), in the bathroom, or in the garage — cooking fumes, steam, and exhaust cause nuisance alarms and degrade the sensor. Also avoid placement right next to a fireplace or furnace, in dead-air corners, or anywhere below freezing or above 100°F. Put them on every level, outside each sleeping area, and in rooms adjacent to attached garages.

**Can a carbon monoxide detector go off for no reason?**
True false alarms are rare, but nuisance chirps happen: low batteries, an expired sensor, dust in the sensor, high humidity near bathrooms, and some household chemicals can all trigger chirps. However, a full repeating 4-beep alarm should always be treated as real CO — never assume a loud alarm is a malfunction. Evacuate, call 911, and let the fire department's meters decide.

**How long do carbon monoxide detectors last?**
Five to ten years, depending on the model — the electrochemical sensor inside degrades over time and cannot be replaced separately. The replace-by date is printed on the unit; write it on the front in marker when you install it. Units with sealed 10-year batteries last the life of the sensor and never need a battery swap.
