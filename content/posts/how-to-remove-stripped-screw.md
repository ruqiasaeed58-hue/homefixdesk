A stripped screw head — the cross or slot worn smooth so your driver just spins — stops a project cold. Do not keep grinding at it with the same bit; that only makes it worse. Work through these methods from gentlest to most aggressive. One of them will get it out.

## Why screws strip (and how to stop before it gets worse)

A driver bit grips a screw head through precise contact — the bit's shape matches the recess exactly. Stripping destroys that match:

- **Cam-out:** the bit slips out of the recess under torque, grinding the edges round. Phillips heads are designed to cam out at high torque (to prevent overdriving); the trade-off is they strip easily.
- **Wrong bit:** a #1 bit in a #2 recess contacts only the tips — it spins and reams the head smooth.
- **Worn bit:** old bits have rounded edges that no longer match the recess. They feel like they fit but slip under load.
- **Soft screws:** cheap zinc screws strip far easier than hardened steel. The $2 box of screws costs more in stripped heads than quality screws do.

**The critical rule:** the moment the bit slips twice, STOP. Every additional slip removes more metal and closes off the easier removal methods. Switch to a fresh bit, add downward pressure, or move to the next method below.

## Quick diagnosis: how stripped is it?

- **Bit slips but still catches a little** → rubber band or steel wool trick. See Method 1.
- **Head completely smooth, screw is small** → screw extractor. See Method 2.
- **Head completely smooth, screw is large or stuck hard** → cut a new slot. See Method 3.
- **Head broken off entirely** → pliers on the shank, or drill it out. See Method 4.

## What you will need

- Wide rubber band (or steel wool)
- Correct-size driver bits (fresh, not worn)
- Screw extractor set ($8–$15)
- Rotary tool (Dremel) with cutting disc, or a hacksaw
- Locking pliers (Vise-Grips)
- Drill and left-hand drill bits (optional but excellent)
- Penetrating oil (for rusted screws)

## Method 1: The rubber band trick (try first)

1. **Switch to the correct bit.** A stripped head is often just a worn bit. Try a fresh bit of the exact right size first — fully seated, straight, with firm downward pressure.
2. If it still slips, **lay a wide rubber band flat over the screw head** and press the bit through it into the head. The rubber fills the worn gaps and adds grip.
3. **Push hard and turn slowly.** Downward pressure is everything — the bit must stay seated.
4. Alternative: a small wad of **steel wool** works the same way and grips even better on badly worn heads.

## Method 2: Screw extractor

Extractors are reverse-threaded bits that bite into the screw as you turn counterclockwise.

1. **Drill a pilot hole** in the center of the screw head with the bit size specified on the extractor package. Go slow — you are drilling into hardened steel.
2. **Tap the extractor in** with a hammer so it seats firmly.
3. **Turn counterclockwise slowly** with a tap wrench or adjustable wrench. The reverse threads dig in and back the screw out.
4. Apply **penetrating oil** first and wait 10 minutes if the screw is rusted or corroded.

Extractor sets cost under $15 and belong in every toolbox — this is the method that works when the rubber band fails.

## Method 3: Cut a new slot

For larger screws where you can get a cutting tool to the head:

1. With a rotary tool and cutting disc (or a hacksaw for big heads), **cut a straight slot** across the screw head, deep enough for a flat-head screwdriver.
2. **Use a manual flat-head screwdriver** (not a power driver — too easy to cam out again) with strong downward pressure.
3. Turn slowly. The fresh slot gives full engagement.

Wear safety glasses — cutting discs throw sparks and fragments.

## Method 4: Grab it or drill it out

- **Pliers:** if the head protrudes at all, clamp locking pliers onto it and turn. For a broken-off screw with exposed shank, this is the go-to.
- **Left-hand drill bits:** drilling with a left-hand bit often catches and backs the screw out on its own — the drilling action works in the removal direction. Start small and work up.
- **Drill it out completely:** as a last resort, drill the screw out with progressively larger bits until only the threads remain, then pick them out or re-tap the hole. You will need to repair the hole afterward (see our stripped-hole guide).

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Keeping at it with a slipping bit.** Every slip makes the head worse. Stop, switch methods.
- **Using the wrong size bit.** A #1 bit in a #2 screw is the most common cause of stripping in the first place.
- **No downward pressure.** Especially with power drivers — push hard, turn slow.
- **Drilling the pilot hole off-center.** The extractor will snap. Center-punch the head first.
- **Snapping the extractor.** Extractors are hardened and brittle — if it breaks off in the screw, you now have a much harder problem. Go slow, use oil, do not force it.

## Preventing stripped screws

- Match the bit exactly to the screw (Phillips #2 is not universal — check).
- Pre-drill pilot holes in hardwood.
- Drive straight, not at an angle.
- Replace worn bits — a fresh $2 bit saves a $15 extractor job.
- Use impact-rated bits in impact drivers; standard bits cam out and shatter.
- A drop of wax or soap on threads reduces the torque needed.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which screw extractor size do I need?**
Match the extractor to the screw: the package lists a pilot-hole drill size for each extractor. When in doubt, start with the smaller extractor — you can always go bigger.

**Can I remove a stripped screw from a phone or laptop?**
Yes, but gently — use precision extractors made for small electronics, and never use heat or cutting discs near a battery.

**The screw is rusted solid — what helps?**
Penetrating oil (not WD-40 — use a real penetrating oil), patience (wait 15+ minutes, reapply), and heat from a soldering iron on the screw head to break the rust bond.

**What if the extractor breaks inside the screw?**
A broken extractor is extremely hard to drill (it is harder than drill bits). At that point, options are: drill around it and plug the hole, or relocate the fastener. Prevention — slow speed, oil, no forcing — matters more than cure here.

**Can I use an impact driver to remove a stripped screw?**
Carefully, yes — an impact driver with a fresh, correct bit and strong downward pressure can shock a mildly stripped screw loose. But on a badly stripped head, the impacts just ream it smoother. Try it once with a new bit; if it slips, stop and switch to the extractor.

**What about stripped hex (Allen) screws?**
Same principles: try a slightly larger metric/SAE hex key tapped in with a hammer, or a Torx bit hammered into the rounded hex recess — the star shape bites where the hex slipped. Then extract normally.

## Method selection: quick reference

| Situation | Start with | If that fails |
|---|---|---|
| Bit still catches slightly | Fresh correct bit + pressure | Rubber band / steel wool |
| Head smooth, small screw | Screw extractor | Left-hand drill bit |
| Head smooth, large screw | Cut new slot (rotary tool) | Extractor |
| Head protrudes | Locking pliers | Cut slot, then pliers |
| Head broken off | Pliers on shank | Drill out completely |
| Rusted/seized | Penetrating oil + wait, then extractor | Heat + extractor |

## When to call a pro

- The stripped screw is in a structural connection and you cannot remove it without damage — a contractor can assess alternatives.
- It is a stripped bolt on a vehicle or appliance under warranty — let the dealer/manufacturer handle it to avoid voiding coverage.
- You have broken an extractor off inside — a machine shop with EDM or carbide tooling can remove it, but it is rarely worth it for household items; relocating the fastener is usually the practical answer.
