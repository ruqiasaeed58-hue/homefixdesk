A sump pump that runs constantly usually isn't working hard — it's working wrong. The most common cause is a **stuck or tangled float switch**: the float that should drop and shut the pump off gets wedged against the pit wall, the power cord, or the discharge pipe, so the pump never gets the signal to stop.

The exception: during a long, heavy rain event, near-constant running is normal. But if the pump runs 24/7 with no rain and dry weather, something is wrong — and a pump that runs nonstop burns out its motor, usually failing silently during the next big storm, when you need it most.

## Is it normal? Rain vs. no rain

Context decides whether constant running is a problem. Run through this quickly:

- **During or right after heavy rain:** running frequently or even continuously for hours is normal. The pit is doing its job, collecting groundwater faster than usual. It should slow down and stop within a day or so after the rain ends.
- **A day after rain:** frequent cycling is normal in high-water-table areas. Truly nonstop running is borderline — check the float.
- **Dry weather, no rain for days:** constant running is *not* normal. You have a mechanical problem, a plumbing leak feeding the pit, or an unusually high water table — in that order of likelihood.

**Safety first, before you touch anything:** electricity and water are a lethal combination. Your pump must be on a **GFCI-protected outlet**. **Never stand in water** with the pump plugged in, and **always unplug it before putting your hands in the pit.** If water is above outlet level, don't wade in — call for help.

## The float-switch test (cause #1)

The float switch is the pump's on/off brain: the float rises with the water, turns the pump on at a set level, and drops to shut it off when the water falls. When it sticks in the "up" position, the pump runs forever.

**How to test it:**

1. Unplug the pump first. Safety is not optional here.
2. Look into the pit. The float is either a ball on the end of a tethered cord, or a vertical float sliding on a rod attached to the pump.
3. **Move the float up and down by hand.** It should travel freely through its full range. Feel for catching, scraping, or resistance at any point.
4. Look for what's blocking it: the classic is the float tangled in its own power cord, followed by the float wedged against the pit wall or discharge pipe, or debris jamming the mechanism.
5. Clear the obstruction, make sure the float moves freely, plug the pump back in, and watch one full cycle: it should kick on as water rises and shut off cleanly as the pit empties.

**Reading the result:** If the pump now cycles normally, you fixed it. To prevent a repeat, **tie the power cord to the discharge pipe** with a zip tie so it can't drift into the float's path.

If the float moves freely but the pump still runs, the switch itself may have failed internally — the float moves, but the signal doesn't. A replacement float switch runs **$20–$50** with a plug-in piggyback connector, no wiring needed. If the whole pump is 7–10+ years old, replace the pump instead.

## The check-valve backflow test

The check valve sits on the discharge pipe just above the pump. Its job: when the pump shuts off, the valve snaps closed so the water sitting in the discharge pipe doesn't drain back into the pit. When the valve fails, every cycle's worth of pumped water flows right back down — the pump refills its own pit and runs again. Forever.

**How to test it:**

1. Watch the pit through one complete cycle. Let the pump run until it shuts off.
2. **Watch the water level immediately after shutoff.** If it visibly rises within a minute or two — water gurgling back down the discharge pipe — the check valve is leaking or missing.
3. Listen too: a healthy check valve makes a distinct *clunk* when it closes. Silence, followed by the sound of water draining back, is the tell.

**Reading the result:** A failed check valve means the pump does every gallon of work twice, explaining both the constant running and a higher electric bill. Replacement valves cost **$10–$25** and install with simple slip or threaded fittings. Check that the valve's arrow points *away* from the pump — a backwards check valve behaves exactly like a failed one.

## Discharge line checks (especially in winter)

If the pump runs and runs but the water level barely drops, the pump is fighting a blocked discharge line. The water has nowhere to go, so the pit never empties and the float never drops.

Walk the discharge line from the pit to where it exits the house:

- **Outside termination:** the most common blockage point. Look for ice (in winter, the pipe end freezes shut — extremely common), mud, mulch, leaves, or a rodent nest packed into the opening. In winter, extend the pipe or add a freeze-relief tee so the pump has an alternate exit.
- **Buried lines** can clog with silt or collapse — a pump that runs constantly every spring but worked fine in fall points at a buried-line problem.
- **Kinks and crushes:** flexible discharge hose pinched behind stored boxes, or rigid pipe crushed by settled soil.

A quick diagnostic: during a cycle, go outside and check whether water is actually flowing from the discharge outlet. Strong flow + slow-dropping pit = check-valve backflow. Weak or no flow + running pump = blocked discharge. Neither = the pump's impeller may be clogged or the pump is simply worn out.

Clearing an iced or clogged termination is free; having a plumber clear a buried discharge line typically runs **$150–$400**.

## High water table vs. plumbing leak: how to tell

Sometimes the pump runs constantly because water really is pouring into the pit nonstop — and the water isn't coming from the ground. A leaking water heater, a cracked supply line, a washing machine drain, or a failed water softener can feed the pit around the clock, in any weather.

**The water-source test:**

1. Pick a dry day with no rain in the forecast.
2. Note how fast water is entering the pit.
3. **Shut off the house's main water valve** and wait 30–60 minutes.
4. Check the pit again. If the inflow has slowed or stopped, the water was coming from your plumbing — you have a leak the pump has been covering for. If unchanged, it's groundwater.

**Other tells:** clear, odorless water arriving steadily in dry weather leans plumbing leak; water that rises and falls with rain leans high water table. A sudden onset after years of normal operation leans leak.

Check the water heater base, washing machine hoses, and visible supply lines first. A plumber's leak-detection visit runs **$150–$350** — cheap compared to what a hidden leak costs in water and foundation damage.

If it truly is a high water table — groundwater genuinely never stops arriving — the pump isn't broken, it's undersized for the site. Options: a higher-capacity pump, a second pit, or exterior drainage work — and a battery-backup pump stops being optional (see below). ## The burnout risk — and why a battery backup is cheap insurance

**A pump that runs constantly is a pump that's dying.** Continuous duty overheats motors designed for intermittent cycling — and the pump fails at 2 a.m. during the next big storm, discovered when the basement carpet squishes.

A quality primary pump costs **$150–$300** ($200–$400 for professional install). A battery-backup system costs **$250–$500** and sits idle until the primary fails or the power goes out — precisely when storms hit. A flooded basement cleanup costs **$2,000–$10,000+**. The backup is the cheapest insurance policy in your house.

Most submersible pumps last **7–10 years** — if yours is in that range *and* running constantly, replace the pump rather than troubleshooting the float. Pedestal pumps last longer (15–25 years), but their floats fail the same way.

## Frequently asked questions

**How often should a sump pump normally run?**
It depends entirely on your water table and weather. In a dry spell, a healthy pump may not run for weeks. After rain, several cycles per hour is normal. The red flag isn't frequency — it's running continuously with no rain to explain it.

**Can a sump pump run too much and burn out?**
Yes. Sump pumps are built for intermittent duty — short runs with cooldowns between. Continuous running overheats the motor and can shorten a 10-year pump's life to months. Constant running is both a symptom and a cause of failure.

**Why does my sump pump run every few minutes even when it's dry?**
Short-cycling usually means the float switch's on/off range is too narrow, or the check valve is leaking and each cycle partially refills the pit. Widen the float tether range and run the backflow test above.

**Is the water in my sump pit supposed to be clear?**
Usually, yes — groundwater is clear. Oily sheens, strong odors, or constant inflow in dry weather suggest a plumbing leak, a water softener discharging, or (rarely) groundwater contamination worth investigating.

**Should I unplug my sump pump in winter to save electricity?**
No — never. Freeze-thaw cycles can spike groundwater with zero warning, and the pump costs pennies a day on standby. Unplugging it gambles the whole basement.

**Do I need a battery backup if I rarely lose power?**
Power loss is only half the reason. The backup also covers primary-pump failure — a burned-out motor, a stuck float, a tripped GFCI — the more common failure mode. If your basement is finished, the backup pays for itself the first time the primary quits during a storm.

## Prevention tips

- **Test the pump twice a year.** Pour a bucket of water into the pit each spring and fall and watch a full on/off cycle — this catches stuck floats and dead pumps before storm season.
- **Keep the float's travel zone clear.** Zip-tie the power cord to the discharge pipe so it can't tangle the float.
- **Clean the pit annually.** Scoop out silt, gravel, and debris that jam floats and clog impellers.
- **Verify the GFCI monthly.** Press the test button on the pump's outlet — a tripped GFCI silently disables your pump.
- **Extend the discharge line** well away from the foundation (10+ feet) so pumped water doesn't recycle straight back into the pit.
- **In winter, check the outside discharge outlet for ice** after every freeze, and consider a freeze-relief tee.
- **Replace the pump at 7–10 years** even if it still runs — a pump's last day is always the day you need it most.

## When to call a pro

- The pump is 7–10+ years old and showing any symptoms — replacement is smarter than repair
- You've cleared the float, tested the check valve, and checked the discharge, and it still runs constantly
- The water-source test points to a plumbing leak you can't locate
- You want a battery-backup system installed or the pump hardwired to a dedicated circuit
- The pit fills faster than any pump can empty it — you may need a second pit or exterior drainage work
- Any electrical work beyond plugging/unplugging: new GFCI outlet, dedicated circuit, or wiring changes
