Paint peels when it loses adhesion to the surface beneath — and simply painting over the peeling spot guarantees it will peel again. The fix is unglamorous but simple: remove everything loose, fix the underlying cause, prime properly, and repaint. Skip any step and you will be redoing it within a year.

## Quick diagnosis: why is it peeling?

- **Peeling in a bathroom, kitchen, or near a leak** → moisture is the cause. Fix the moisture first or paint will keep failing. See Step 1.
- **Peeling in large sheets down to bare wall** → poor surface prep or painting over a glossy/unstable layer. See Steps 2–3.
- **Peeling only where new paint meets old** → incompatible paint types (latex over oil without primer). See Step 3.
- **Bubbling before peeling** → moisture or heat trapped under the paint. Same fix, but find the moisture source.

## Lead paint safety (pre-1978 homes)

Scraping old paint in a pre-1978 home can release lead dust. Wear a P100 respirator, contain the area with plastic sheeting, and clean up with a HEPA vacuum and wet mopping. A $15 lead test kit tells you in minutes whether precautions are needed.

## What you will need

- Paint scraper (stiff) and putty knife
- 120-grit and 220-grit sandpaper (or sanding sponge)
- High-adhesion primer (oil-based or shellac-based for problem areas)
- Matching paint
- Painter's tape, drop cloths
- Spackle or joint compound for gouges
- TSP substitute or degreasing cleaner

## Step 1: Fix the moisture source

Paint over a moisture problem and it will peel again — guaranteed.

1. **Bathrooms:** run the exhaust fan during and 20 minutes after every shower. Peeling above a shower is almost always steam, not bad paint.
2. **Near windows/doors:** check for leaks — water infiltration behind the paint pushes it off from beneath.
3. **Exterior walls:** peeling on an outside wall can mean moisture migrating through from outside (failed caulking, bad flashing).
4. Let the wall dry completely before proceeding — days, not hours, for a wall that was genuinely wet.

## Step 2: Scrape and sand

1. **Scrape off everything loose.** Use the paint scraper to remove all peeling, flaking, and bubbling paint. Keep going until you reach paint that is firmly bonded — if you can get an edge under it with the scraper, it comes off.
2. **Feather the edges.** Sand the transition between bare spots and old paint with 120-grit so there is no ridge. A visible edge will telegraph through the new paint.
3. **Fill gouges** with spackle, let dry, sand smooth with 220-grit.
4. **Clean the wall.** Wipe with a TSP-substitute cleaner to remove grease, dust, and contaminants. Paint does not stick to dirty walls.

## Step 3: Prime correctly (the step that prevents recurrence)

Primer is not optional on a repair — it is the adhesion layer.

1. **Spot-prime all bare areas** with a high-adhesion primer. For problem spots (stains, tannin bleed, glossy old paint), use oil-based or shellac-based primer — they seal and grip far better than latex primer on difficult surfaces.
2. If the old paint is oil-based and you are switching to latex (or vice versa), prime the entire area — the two do not bond directly.
3. Let the primer dry fully per the can's instructions. Rushing this step is a classic cause of repeat failure.

## Step 4: Repaint

1. **Apply two thin coats**, not one thick one. Thick coats skin over and trap solvents/moisture underneath — a peeling risk.
2. Let the first coat dry completely (check the can — "dry to touch" is not "ready to recoat").
3. In bathrooms and kitchens, use paint labeled for high-moisture areas (satin or semi-gloss sheens resist moisture best).

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Painting over loose paint.** The #1 cause of repeat peeling. If it is not bonded, it comes off.
- **Skipping primer on bare spots.** New paint over bare drywall or old glossy paint without primer peels within months.
- **One thick coat instead of two thin ones.** Thick paint traps moisture and fails.
- **Ignoring the moisture source.** No paint survives an active leak or a steam-filled bathroom with no ventilation.
- **Painting in high humidity.** Paint needs to cure — avoid painting bathrooms on steamy days or when humidity is above 70%.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does paint peel in the bathroom but nowhere else?**
Steam. Hot showers create moisture that penetrates paint film, especially flat-finish paint. Exhaust ventilation + moisture-rated paint solves it permanently.

**Can I just peel off the loose bits and touch up?**
Only if the surrounding paint is firmly bonded. Test by pressing tape firmly onto the old paint and ripping it off — if paint comes with the tape, the adhesion has failed over a larger area than it looks.

**Latex over oil paint — will it stick?**
Not without primer. The rule: if you do not know what the old paint is, prime with a bonding primer. The thumbnail test (scratch the old paint — if it comes off in a rubbery sheet it is latex; if it chips hard it is oil) helps identify it.

**How long should a proper repaint last?**
Interior walls: 7–10 years. Bathrooms: 5–7 years even with good prep, due to moisture cycling.

## When to call a pro

- Peeling covers large areas or whole rooms — a painter with proper spray equipment does it faster and better ($300–$800 per room).
- The cause is water infiltration you cannot find — a contractor should trace the leak before any repaint.
- Lead paint is present and the area is large — lead-safe certified painters.
- Plaster walls are crumbling beneath the paint — the plaster needs repair or skim-coating first, which is skilled work.
