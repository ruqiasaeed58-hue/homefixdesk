If you wake up on a cold morning to find the insides of your windows beaded with water or iced over at the edges, the cause is simple physics: warm, moisture-laden air in your house is hitting cold glass, and the vapor is turning back into liquid. It is one of the most common winter complaints in American homes, and it is almost always a humidity problem — not a window problem. The good news is that a combination of lowering indoor moisture and warming the glass surface will stop it in most homes without replacing a single window.

Left alone, chronic window condensation is more than an annoyance. Water running down glass soaks into wooden sills and trim, feeds mold in the corners, and can even rot the sash over a few seasons. So it is worth fixing properly, and winter is exactly the right time to do it.

## Why condensation forms: warm moist air meets cold glass

Condensation is the same phenomenon as dew on morning grass. Air holds water vapor, and warm air holds a lot more of it than cold air. When warm indoor air drifts against a windowpane chilled by outdoor temperatures, the air right next to the glass cools rapidly. Cooled air cannot hold as much vapor, so the excess condenses into droplets on the glass. The bigger the temperature gap and the more humid your indoor air, the worse the fogging and dripping.

A normal home generates a surprising amount of moisture every day. Cooking a pot of pasta, running the shower, breathing, even drying a load of laundry indoors — a family of four can put 2 to 3 gallons of water into the air daily. In summer that moisture vents away easily. In winter, homes are sealed up tight against the cold, so all that vapor has nowhere to go. It accumulates until it finds the coldest surface in the house — your windows — and condenses there.

Older single-pane windows are the worst offenders because the glass gets nearly as cold as the outdoor air. Double- and triple-pane windows keep the interior glass much warmer, which is why they rarely sweat unless humidity is very high. So if your double-pane windows are dripping, that is a strong signal your indoor humidity is running too high.

## Quick fixes you can do today

Before buying anything, start with the free moves. They often cut condensation dramatically within a day.

**Wipe and observe.** Dry the windows with a towel or a squeegee each morning for a few days. This does not fix anything, but it protects the sills while you work on the real causes — and watching where the moisture appears tells you which rooms are the worst offenders. Condensation concentrated on bathroom and kitchen windows points to ventilation problems; condensation on every window points to whole-house humidity.

**Ventilate the moisture rooms.** Run the bathroom exhaust fan during every shower and for at least 20 minutes after. Run the range hood every time you cook, especially when boiling water. These two habits alone remove a huge share of household moisture at the source.

**Crack a window briefly.** It feels backwards in winter, but opening a window for 10 to 15 minutes a day lets damp air escape and drier outdoor air in. Cold outdoor air holds very little moisture, so even a short airing drops indoor humidity noticeably. Bedrooms are the best candidates — two people breathing in a closed bedroom all night is a classic condensation recipe.

## Humidity control: the real fix

Quick fixes manage symptoms. Controlling indoor humidity is the cure. Aim for 30 to 50 percent relative humidity in winter — the EPA recommends staying under 60 percent year-round, and most homes feel comfortable around 35 to 45 percent in the heating season.

**Measure first.** A $10 to $15 digital hygrometer from the hardware store tells you what you are actually dealing with. Place one in the worst room and check it morning and evening for a few days. If readings sit above 50 percent in winter, you have confirmed the diagnosis. Without a number, you are guessing.

**Run a dehumidifier in problem rooms.** Basements, laundry rooms, and tightly sealed bedrooms are the usual suspects. A small portable dehumidifier (30-pint units run $150 to $250) can pull a gallon or more of water from the air daily. Empty the tank regularly or hook up the drain hose so it runs continuously. In very tight, well-insulated homes, a whole-house dehumidifier tied into the HVAC system ($1,200 to $2,500 installed) handles the entire house automatically.

**Turn down the humidifier.** Many forced-air systems have a whole-house humidifier on the furnace, and homeowners often set it too high. If yours is cranked up, dial it back until window condensation stops. The humidistat setting that works at 40°F outside will be far too high at 10°F — outdoor temperature matters, so adjust through the season.

**Stop drying clothes indoors.** A single load of laundry drying on an indoor rack releases roughly half a gallon of water into the air. Use the dryer (vented outdoors, with the vent hose intact and unobstructed) or dry clothes in a ventilated garage instead.

## Warm the glass: the other half of the equation

Condensation needs two ingredients: moisture and a cold surface. You have been cutting moisture; now raise the glass temperature so vapor has less reason to condense.

**Thermal curtains and cellular shades.** Heavy curtains or honeycomb (cellular) shades add an insulating air layer over the glass. They are the fastest way to warm the interior glass surface, and they pay for themselves in heating savings. One caution: keep the covering slightly open at the bottom so a little warm room air still reaches the glass — a fully sealed curtain can trap moist air against the pane and make condensation worse in the gap.

**Temporary window insulation film.** The shrink-film kits sold every fall ($5 to $10 per window) add a dead-air pocket over the glass. They look nearly invisible once the hair dryer tightens them, and they make a measurable difference on single-pane windows. They are seasonal — you peel them off in spring — but for old windows they are the best dollars-per-result fix available.

**Replace worn weatherstripping and caulk.** Gaps around the sash let cold air wash over the interior glass and also let moist indoor air into the frame cavity. Fresh V-strip or foam weatherstripping ($5 to $15 per window) and a bead of exterior caulk where trim meets siding both help. This is also just good draft-proofing.

## Failed seals vs. surface condensation: know the difference

Not all window moisture is the same, and the fix depends on which kind you have.

**Surface condensation** forms on the room-side face of the glass. You can wipe it off with your finger. This is the humidity problem described above, and everything in this article fixes it.

**Condensation between the panes** of a double- or triple-pane window looks like fog or droplets trapped inside the glass where you cannot reach it. That means the seal on the insulated glass unit has failed, letting moist air into the space between panes. No amount of dehumidifying will fix it — the moisture is sealed inside the unit. A failed seal also means the window has lost much of its insulating value.

**Condensation on the outside** of the glass (the exterior face) on cool mornings is actually a sign your windows are working well — the exterior pane is cold because heat is not leaking through. You can ignore it entirely.

If you have between-the-panes fog, skip the humidity fixes for that window and read the replacement section below.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Cranking the humidifier up.** More humidity is the opposite of what foggy windows need. Dial it down until the condensation stops, then leave it.
- **Sealing curtains tight against the glass.** Trapped moist air behind a fully closed curtain condenses worse. Leave a small gap at the bottom for air circulation.
- **Ignoring the bathroom fan.** The fan only works if it runs — during and 20 minutes after every shower. A loud, useless fan is a repair waiting to happen.
- **Drying laundry indoors all winter.** Each load adds roughly half a gallon of water to your air. Vent the dryer properly or dry elsewhere.
- **Blaming the windows first.** In the vast majority of cases, sweating windows are a humidity problem, not a window problem. Replacing windows without fixing humidity wastes thousands.
- **Using a humidifier and dehumidifier at the same time.** It happens more often than you would think. Pick one strategy per zone.
- **Letting water sit on sills.** Standing water rots wood trim and feeds mold. Wipe sills dry while you work on the underlying cause.

## Frequently asked questions

**What humidity level stops window condensation?**
There is no single number because it depends on outdoor temperature — the colder it is outside, the lower your indoor humidity needs to be. As a rule of thumb, keep it between 30 and 50 percent, dropping toward 30 percent during deep cold snaps. A hygrometer takes the guesswork out.

**Will a dehumidifier stop window condensation?**
Yes, if high humidity is the cause — and it usually is. A portable dehumidifier in the worst room often clears condensation within a day or two. For whole-house problems in tight homes, a whole-house unit on the HVAC system is the set-and-forget answer.

**Why do only some windows get condensation?**
The coldest windows sweat first. North-facing windows, windows in unheated rooms, and windows behind heavy curtains are colder than the rest, so they show condensation while others stay clear. Bathrooms and kitchens also generate more moisture locally. The pattern tells you where to focus.

**Can condensation damage my windows?**
Yes, over time. Repeated wetting rots wooden sashes and sills, peels paint, stains drywall below the window, and grows mold in the corners and on the caulk. Aluminum and vinyl frames resist rot but can still grow mold in the tracks. A few seasons of neglect can turn a humidity problem into a carpentry bill.

**I have condensation between the panes — can I fix it myself?**
Not really. Between-the-panes fog means the insulated glass unit's seal has failed, and the fix is replacing the glass unit (the sash can usually stay). Some companies offer defogging services that drill and vent the unit, but results are mixed and the insulating value is never fully restored. Replacement of the glass unit is the reliable fix.

## When to call a pro

Call a window professional if you have condensation trapped between the panes of double- or triple-pane windows — that is a failed seal, and the glass unit needs replacement ($150 to $400 per sash, far cheaper than a whole new window). Call an HVAC contractor if indoor humidity stays above 50 percent despite portable dehumidifiers and ventilation fixes — you may need a whole-house dehumidifier or an HRV/ERV ventilation system, especially in a tightly built home. And call a mold remediation specialist if you find black mold spreading in window tracks, on drywall, or inside the wall cavity — surface wiping is fine for small spots, but widespread growth needs professional handling. For everything else — measuring humidity, ventilating, filming windows, and adjusting the humidifier — this is firmly DIY territory.
