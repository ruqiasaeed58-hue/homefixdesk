Your house feels draftier in fall because the temperature gap between inside and outside grows — and every air leak that was invisible in July suddenly starts stealing heat in October. The good news: most drafts come from a short list of predictable spots, and you can find every one of them with a lit incense stick and an hour of your time. Seal the big ones and you'll feel the difference the same evening, usually for less than $100 in materials.

## The stack effect: your house is a chimney

Warm air rises. In fall, heated indoor air rises to the top floor and escapes through every gap it finds — the attic hatch, recessed lights, plumbing vents, the seam where walls meet the attic floor. As warm air leaves, it creates suction at the bottom of the house, pulling cold outdoor air in through foundation gaps, basement windows, rim joists, and under exterior doors. Building scientists call this the stack effect: your house is the chimney.

In summer the effect is weak because indoor and outdoor temperatures are close. But a fall cold snap can create a 30- to 40-degree gap, and the stack effect strengthens dramatically as that gap grows. That's why the house that felt fine in September feels like a wind tunnel in October — nothing changed except the thermometer.

Wind adds a second force: windward walls get pressurized (pushing cold air in) while leeward walls get depressurized (pulling warm air out). On gusty fall evenings both forces combine — which is exactly when drafts feel worst.

## Find every draft: the DIY detection walkthrough

Pick a cold, windy day — that's when leaks reveal themselves. Then set the stage:

1. **Turn off the furnace, bath fans, dryer, and range hood.** You want the house at rest, not pressurized by appliances.
2. **Close all exterior doors and windows, plus the fireplace damper.** Leave interior doors open so air moves freely.
3. **Start with your hands.** Walk each room's perimeter with the back of your hand near baseboards, window frames, door frames, and outlets — the back of your hand senses moving air best. Mark every hit with painter's tape.
4. **Bring out the incense stick.** Light one and hold it 2–3 inches from each suspect gap. Smoke streaming sideways means moving air; a steady vertical stream means no leak. Keep it well away from curtains and never leave it unattended. (A candle works, but incense smoke is easier to read.)
5. **Do the dollar-bill test** on doors and windows: close a bill in the frame and pull. If it slides out easily, the seal is weak. Test several points — gaps are rarely even.
6. **Check the attic hatch.** Hold the incense near the hatch perimeter. If smoke gets sucked upward hard, you've found one of your biggest leaks.

No wind that day? Create your own: close everything, turn on all bathroom exhaust fans and the range hood, and let them run 10 minutes. This slightly depressurizes the house, pulling outdoor air in through the leaks so your incense can find them. A $25 infrared thermometer is a nice upgrade — scan baseboards and frames for sudden temperature drops — but hand plus incense finds 90% of what matters.

## The top 8 leak spots, ranked

Ranked by how much air they typically leak, combined with how often homeowners overlook them:

**1. The attic hatch or pull-down stairs.** An unsealed hatch leaks like a window left cracked all winter — and it sits at the very top of the stack effect, exactly where warm air wants to exit. The most overlooked leak in American homes.

**2. Recessed can lights.** Older non-IC-rated cans are holes cut into your ceiling that vent straight into the attic. Each one is a small chimney. If you feel airflow around the trim, they're leaking.

**3. Windows — the sash, not the glass.** Glass doesn't leak; the gaps where sashes meet the frame do. Check the meeting rail (where the two sashes overlap) and the sash sides on older double-hungs with worn weatherstripping.

**4. Exterior doors.** The bottom is the usual suspect — visible daylight under a closed door is a highway for cold air. Worn weatherstripping on the sides and top comes second.

**5. Outlets and switches on exterior walls.** Each is a small hole into the wall cavity, which connects to the attic and outdoors. Tiny individually, but a house can have dozens.

**6. Plumbing and wiring penetrations.** Under sinks and behind toilets, builders drill holes bigger than the pipe and rarely seal them. Check with the incense stick — you'll often be surprised.

**7. Basement rim joists.** The seam where the rim joist meets the foundation and the subfloor is one of the leakiest joints in the house — and it's where the stack effect pulls cold air *in*, so sealing here pays double.

**8. The fireplace.** An open damper is a straight shot from your living room to the sky, and even "closed" metal dampers seal badly. On a windy day you can often feel cold air pouring down the chimney.

## Quick wins vs. bigger fixes

**Quick wins (a weekend, mostly under $100 total):**
- Caulk where window frames and door trim meet the wall (exterior-grade outside, paintable inside).
- Add adhesive foam weatherstripping to drafty sashes and door frames.
- Put door sweeps on every exterior door with a visible bottom gap.
- Install foam gaskets behind outlet and switch covers on exterior walls ($5 for a 12-pack).
- Weatherstrip the attic hatch and lay insulation over it — or buy a pre-made hatch cover.
- Close the fireplace damper when not in use, or add a chimney balloon for the season.

**Bigger fixes (plan these — they're the heavy hitters):**
- **Air-seal the attic floor.** Caulk and foam every ceiling penetration (wires, plumbing vents, chimney chases, light boxes). Sweaty work, but it attacks the stack effect at its exit point — the single highest-impact project.
- **Seal and insulate rim joists** with rigid foam board plus canned spray foam.
- **Add attic insulation — but only *after* air sealing**, since insulation barely slows moving air.
- **Seal ductwork** in attics and crawlspaces with mastic; leaky ducts can waste 20–30% of heated air.
- **Replace windows last.** They're the most expensive way to fix what $30 of weatherstripping usually solves.

## How much energy drafts waste

The U.S. Department of Energy estimates that air leaks waste **25–40% of the energy** used to heat and cool a typical home. ENERGY STAR puts it this way: sealing air leaks (combined with adding insulation) can cut heating and cooling costs by **up to 15%**.

In dollars: a $200/month heating bill over a five-month season is $1,000; 15% off saves $150 every winter — so a $60 afternoon of caulk and weatherstripping pays for itself before January. Beyond money, sealed houses are quieter, less dusty, and don't have that one freezing hallway. Drafts also drive moisture trouble: warm indoor air leaking into a cold attic condenses, feeding mold and winter ice dams.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Sealing tight without thinking about combustion air.** Gas furnaces, water heaters, and stoves that draw air from inside the house can backdraft — spilling exhaust indoors — if you starve them of air. Never seal up a mechanical room without checking this; when in doubt, ask an HVAC pro.
- **Caulking window weep holes.** Those little slots on the outside bottom of frames are drainage — sealing them traps water inside the frame.
- **Wrong product, wrong place.** Interior caulk used outside cracks within a season. High-expansion spray foam around window and door frames can bow them — use low-expansion "window and door" foam only.
- **Insulating the attic before air sealing it.** Insulation slows heat transfer; it does almost nothing against moving air. Seal first, insulate second.
- **Obsessing over windows while ignoring the attic.** A weekend spent weatherstripping windows is wasted if the attic hatch — leaking ten times more air — sits untouched. Fix the biggest leaks first.
- **Blocking soffit vents with new insulation.** Keep baffles in place so air flows from soffits to the ridge vent, or the roof deck grows moisture problems.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do I only notice drafts in fall, not summer?**
Drafts are driven by the indoor-outdoor temperature gap. In summer it's small so air creeps through the same gaps; in fall the gap hits 30–40 degrees, the stack effect kicks into high gear, and every leak announces itself. The leaks were always there — fall just turns up the volume.

**Will new windows fix my draft problem?**
Usually less than you'd hope. The glass isn't the leak — the sash and frame gaps are, and those can be weatherstripped and caulked for a fraction of replacement cost. Replace windows for fogged glass or rotted frames, not as a draft strategy.

**Is it safe to seal every gap I find?**
Almost. The exception: homes with fuel-burning appliances need combustion air — seal too aggressively and you can cause carbon monoxide to spill indoors. Keep working CO detectors on every floor, and talk to an HVAC pro before sealing a small mechanical room.

**How do I check for drafts when it's not windy?**
Make your own wind: close everything, run all bath fans and the range hood for 10 minutes to slightly depressurize the house, then test with incense. The leaks will pull outdoor air in and reveal themselves.

**Do those fabric draft snakes actually work?**
Temporarily, yes — a draft snake blocks the bottom gap, often the worst one. But it's a bandage: a $10 door sweep does the same job permanently without being moved every time the door opens.

**Should I pay for a professional energy audit?**
If DIY sealing didn't finish the job or bills are inexplicably high, yes. A blower-door test measures exactly how leaky the house is, and the auditor's infrared camera pinpoints where air moves. Many utilities subsidize audits to $100 or free — check with yours.

## When to call a pro

Call a professional when the job goes beyond caulk and weatherstripping. If you find vermiculite insulation, mold, or knob-and-tube wiring in the attic, stop and get expert advice before sealing around it. If you suspect backdrafting from fuel-burning appliances — soot around the draft hood, condensation near the furnace, CO detector alerts — call an HVAC technician immediately; that's a safety issue, not a DIY project. And for blown-in insulation, rim-joist spray foam, or duct sealing in tight crawlspaces, a qualified insulation contractor with a blower door will finish in a day what takes a homeowner three weekends — and verify the results with real measurements.
