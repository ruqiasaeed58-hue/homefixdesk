**Clean your lint screen after every single load, and deep-clean the full vent run every 6 to 12 months.** That's the short answer — and for fire safety, the lint screen habit matters as much as the annual vent cleaning. A clogged dryer vent is the single most common cause of clothes-dryer fires in American homes, yet it's one of the easiest maintenance tasks to stay ahead of.

A clean vent means your dryer heats faster, finishes loads in one cycle, and runs cooler and quieter. A neglected one means longer drying times, higher energy bills, extra wear on the machine — and in the worst case, lint igniting inside a duct full of trapped heat. If your dryer already takes two cycles to finish a load, your vent is past due.

## Why a clogged dryer vent is a fire risk

Your dryer's lint screen only catches a fraction of what comes off your clothes. The rest rides hot exhaust air into the vent duct, coating the walls — especially in ridges of flexible foil hose, at every elbow, and on the damper flap at the exterior cap. Dryer lint is almost pure **tinder**: tiny, dry fabric fibers that ignite at relatively low temperatures.

Add trapped heat. A restricted vent can't push hot exhaust air out, so the temperature inside the dryer cabinet and duct climbs. The U.S. Fire Administration reports roughly **2,900 clothes-dryer fires every year**, and the leading factor — by a wide margin — is the **failure to clean**. A high-temperature safety fuse is supposed to prevent ignition, but an old or degraded fuse may not trip in time.

Gas dryers add one more layer of risk: a clogged vent can trap **carbon monoxide** from combustion, letting it backdraft into the laundry room. This is why gas dryers deserve extra attention and why the gas supply should always be shut off before servicing the machine.

## The cleaning schedule by household type

The rule isn't one-size-fits-all. Dryer usage and laundry habits change how fast lint accumulates, so calibrate the schedule to your household.

**Lint screen — every load, no exceptions.** Pull it and peel the lint off. If you use dryer sheets or fabric softener, scrub the screen with warm soapy water every month or two — softener residue forms an invisible film that clogs the mesh even when it looks clean. Run water over it; if water beads instead of flowing through, the screen is glazed and needs washing.

**Full vent run — every 6 months if any of these apply:**
- A household of 4 or more people (laundry volume alone doubles lint buildup)
- Pets — pet hair is the fastest lint-builder there is
- The vent run is long (over 15 feet), has multiple bends, or exits through the roof
- The vent uses flexible foil or plastic hose (it catches lint far faster than rigid metal duct)
- Someone in the house runs the dryer nearly every day

**Full vent run — every 12 months for everyone else.** Two adults, no pets, a short straight metal-duct run to an exterior wall: once a year is the standard, anchored to early fall when you're already doing seasonal maintenance.

**The exterior cap — check it twice a year.** Birds, rodents, and wasps love nesting in vent caps, and a stuck flap mimics every symptom of a clogged duct. Add it to your spring and fall walk-around.

## Warning signs your vent is already overdue

Don't wait for the calendar if your dryer is showing these symptoms — treat any of them as a clean-it-this-week signal:

**Clothes take two cycles to dry.** The classic early sign. A restricted vent recycles moist air back through the drum. A normal mixed load should dry in 35–50 minutes.

**A burning smell during or after drying.** Lint trapped near the heating element can smolder. **If you smell burning with a hot dryer, stop using the dryer until the vent is cleared.**

**The laundry room feels hot or humid.** That heat and moisture should be exiting outside. A steamy laundry room means exhaust is leaking or backing up — often a crushed hose behind the dryer or a fully blocked duct.

**The outside of the dryer cabinet is unusually hot.** Trapped heat backs up into the machine itself, accelerating wear on the heating element and thermostats.

**The exterior flap barely opens while the dryer runs.** Go outside during a cycle — the vent flap should swing open with strong airflow. Weak or no movement means the duct is restricted.

**Drying takes longer than it used to — even gradually.** Compare against your own baseline. Any unexplained increase in drying time is a vent (or lint screen) problem until proven otherwise.

If your dryer isn't heating at all rather than heating slowly, that's usually a different fault — [Why Is My Dryer Not Heating?](/posts/why-dryer-not-heating/) walks through the heating element, thermal fuse, and thermostat checks.

## Cleaning the vent yourself: step-by-step

This is a 30–45 minute DIY job. You need a **dryer vent cleaning brush kit** ($15–$30 at any hardware store), a vacuum with a hose or crevice tool, and a flashlight.

**Safety first: unplug the dryer.** For an **electric dryer**, pull the plug — 240-volt dryers can draw enough current to kill. For a **gas dryer**, shut the **gas supply valve** behind the dryer (the handle should sit perpendicular to the pipe) and unplug the power cord too.

**1. Pull the dryer away from the wall — carefully.** Slide it forward slowly, watching the vent hose and (on gas models) the gas line. Never kink or yank the gas line; if it doesn't move easily, stop and call a pro.

**2. Disconnect the vent hose.** Most hoses are held by a clamp with a screw or a tension band. Loosen the clamp and pull the hose off the dryer's exhaust port. Have a trash bag ready — a neglected connection can dump a shocking amount of lint.

**3. Vacuum the dryer exhaust port.** Stick the vacuum hose into the dryer's exhaust stub and pull out everything reachable. Shine the flashlight in — heavy buildup here means the vent has been restricted for a while.

**4. Run the brush kit through the duct.** Assemble the rods, push the brush from the dryer end toward the exterior cap, then pull it back — repeat several times, adding rods as needed. For long runs, many kits let you attach a power drill to spin the brush, which scrubs far more effectively.

**5. Clean the transition hose and consider rigid duct.** Vacuum the flexible hose thoroughly. If it's foil accordion hose (or white vinyl — a code violation in most areas), replace it with **semi-rigid or rigid metal duct** while it's disconnected. Foil hose sags, crushes, and catches lint at every ridge; smooth metal cuts future buildup dramatically. A 4-inch rigid elbow and short run costs $10–$20.

**6. Check the exterior cap and flap.** Go outside and clear the cap of lint, nests, and debris. Confirm the damper flap swings freely and snaps shut when the dryer is off. If the cap is cracked or the flap is seized, replace it — exterior vent caps run $15–$35.

**7. Reconnect and test.** Reattach the hose with a tight clamp (metal foil tape over the joint, not screws — screws protrude into the duct and catch lint). Push the dryer back without crushing the hose, then run the machine empty on high heat for 10 minutes and check the exterior flap: it should open fully with strong, hot airflow.

## When to call a pro

- **Long runs over 20–25 feet** or ducts with many elbows — a brush can't maintain scrubbing pressure, and you risk packing lint into a worse mid-run blockage
- **Roof-terminated vents** — cleaning from the roof requires ladders and fall protection; a pro has both
- **Ducts routed through finished walls or ceilings with no cleanout access** — a technician can cut in access panels and reseal properly
- **White vinyl hose** or crushed foil inside the walls — replacement behind drywall is a pro job
- **The dryer still runs hot or slow after a full DIY cleaning** — internal machine lint may need a technician to open the cabinet safely
- **Any gas smell at any point** — stop everything and call your gas company, not your handyman
- **You can't move the dryer safely** (gas line has no slack, stacked unit, tight closet)

A professional dryer vent cleaning typically runs **$90–$170** depending on run length and access difficulty. Roof-vent cleanings and major rerouting can run higher, but compare that against the cost of a new dryer ($500–$1,000+) — or a house fire.

## The energy and insurance angles

**Energy savings are immediate and measurable.** A clogged vent can add 30–60 minutes to a load. At roughly 3 kW, an extra hour of electric drying costs around $0.35–$0.50 per load at average US rates — a family running 6–8 loads a week can waste $100–$200 a year on dryer time that vent cleaning eliminates. A $25 brush kit pays for itself in weeks.

**Your homeowner's insurance increasingly cares about this.** Adjusters have grown more aggressive about scrutinizing neglected-maintenance fires. A dryer fire in a vent that hasn't been cleaned in years can complicate a claim — insurers can argue that routine maintenance was the homeowner's responsibility. A dated photo or receipt from each annual cleaning is cheap documentation that you maintained the system.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use a leaf blower instead of a brush kit?**
A leaf blower can pack lint tighter into elbows and connections, and blowing from the dryer end sends debris flying across your siding. A rotating brush scrubs the duct walls instead of relocating the problem. Brush first; a leaf blower can finish off loose dust afterward on low.

**Does my vent need cleaning if I have a short run?**
Yes — just less often. A 5-foot straight metal run still collects lint at the exterior cap, and birds love short caps. Inspect the cap twice a year and deep-clean annually.

**Can I clean the vent from the outside only?**
Partially. Lint concentrates most heavily near the dryer-end connection where exhaust is hottest and wettest. Always open the dryer-end connection and work the full run.

**My dryer has a "vent blockage" sensor — is manual cleaning still needed?**
Yes. The sensor detects severe restrictions, not gradual buildup — it trips long after efficiency has tanked and risk has risen. Treat it as a late alarm, not a maintenance plan.

**How do I know the vent is actually clear after cleaning?**
Run the dryer on high heat with no clothes for 10 minutes, then go outside: the flap should be fully open with strong, steady, hot airflow. A normal mixed load should then dry in one cycle.

**Is professional cleaning worth it for a short exterior-wall vent?**
Usually not necessary — that's the ideal DIY scenario. Save the pro call for roof runs, long convoluted ductwork, or situations where DIY cleaning didn't fix the symptoms.

## Prevention tips

- **Peel the lint screen after every load** — make it the habit of whoever moves the clothes
- **Wash the lint screen with soap and water** every month or two if you use dryer sheets or fabric softener
- **Replace foil accordion hose with smooth metal duct** — the single biggest long-term upgrade
- **Keep the area behind the dryer clear** so the hose never gets crushed flat against the wall
- **Anchor vent cleaning to a date** — early fall, paired with furnace or smoke-detector checks
- **Check the exterior cap every spring and fall** for nests, stuck flaps, and damage
- **Avoid overloading the dryer** — packed drums shed more lint and need longer cycles
- **Clean the lint screen slot too** — lint compacts below the screen inside the housing; vacuum it monthly
