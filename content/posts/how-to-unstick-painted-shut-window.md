A window that has been painted shut is sealed by layers of dried paint bridging the gap between the moving sash and the frame. Forcing it risks cracked glass, a broken sash, or a pulled-out frame. The fix is methodical: cut the paint seal on every edge, break the bond gently, and work the sash free. Patience beats force every time.

## Why windows get painted shut (and why force fails)

Paint forms a surprisingly strong adhesive bond — each coat adds to it, and old oil-based paint is essentially glue. A window painted shut 20 years ago may have 5+ coats bridging the sash-frame gap, creating a bond stronger than the wood itself in places.

Force fails because the load path goes through the weakest link: push hard on a stuck sash and the glass (the most fragile part) takes the stress, or the thin sash stile splits along the grain. Cutting the bond eliminates the resistance instead of fighting it — that is why a sharp knife beats a strong arm.

**The hidden second seal:** most people score the inside seam and wonder why the window still will not move. Paint also bridges the exterior seam, the meeting rail between sashes, and often the parting bead. Every bridged seam must be cut. Walk around the house and do the outside — it is the step everyone skips.

## Quick diagnosis: why is it stuck?

- **Painted shut** → you can see paint bridging the sash-to-frame seam. See Steps 1–3.
- **Swollen wood (humidity)** → no visible paint bridge, but the sash is tight in the frame. See Step 4.
- **Stuck plus broken sash cord / balance** → the window moves a little then jams. See Step 4.
- **Painted shut AND old house (pre-1978)** → test for lead paint before disturbing it. See the safety note below.

## Lead paint safety (pre-1978 homes)

If your home was built before 1978, the paint may contain lead. Do not sand, scrape aggressively, or create dust without precautions: wear a P100 respirator, lay plastic sheeting, keep children and pets away, and clean up with a HEPA vacuum and wet mopping. For extensive work on many windows, a lead-safe certified contractor is worth it. A $15 lead test kit from the hardware store tells you in minutes.

## What you will need

- Utility knife with fresh blades (several — they dull fast on paint)
- Window zipper tool or painter's 5-in-1 tool (the thin rigid blade is ideal)
- Putty knife (stiff, 1.5-inch)
- Hammer and a wood block
- Pry bar (small, flat)
- Mineral spirits and rags
- Candle or paraffin wax (lubricant for the tracks)
- Work gloves and safety glasses

## Step 1: Score every paint seam

The paint seal exists on all four sides of the sash — inside and outside. Miss one and the window will not move.

1. **Inside:** run the utility knife firmly along the seam where the sash meets the frame — top, bottom, left, right. Cut deep enough to slice through all paint layers down to wood.
2. **Outside:** do the same from the exterior side.
3. **The meeting rail:** for double-hung windows, score where the two sashes meet in the middle.
4. Replace the blade when it starts dragging — a dull blade tears paint instead of cutting it and is more likely to slip.

## Step 2: Break the seal with the zipper tool

1. Work the thin blade of the window zipper (or 5-in-1 tool) into the scored seam.
2. **Tap it gently** with the hammer to drive it along the seam, breaking the paint bond. Work the full length of each side.
3. Go around twice — the first pass cracks the bond, the second pass frees it.

## Step 3: Work the sash free

1. Place the wood block against the sash frame (never against glass) and **tap firmly** with the hammer, working around the perimeter. The wood block spreads the force and prevents dents.
2. Try moving the sash. For a double-hung window, push up on the bottom sash from below with both hands.
3. If it moves a little then sticks, score again at the sticking point and tap some more.
4. **Never pry against the glass.** Pry only against the wood frame, and use the wood block as a fulcrum pad.

## Step 4: Free a swollen or jammed sash

If paint is not the (only) problem:

1. **Humidity swelling:** run a dehumidifier in the room for a day or two, or wait for drier weather. Forcing a swollen sash gouges the frame.
2. **Wax the tracks:** rub a candle or paraffin block along the sash channels and the sash edges. Wax is the traditional, long-lasting window lubricant — better than oil, which attracts dirt.
3. **Broken sash cords/balances:** if the window moves but will not stay up, the counterbalance mechanism is broken. Replacement balances ($15–$30 per window) are a separate repair — doable DIY with the sash removed.

## Step 5: Keep it from sticking again

1. **Clean the channels** of paint chips and debris.
2. **Wax all sliding surfaces** — sash edges and channels.
3. When repainting in the future: **move the sash every few hours** while paint dries, and never paint the sash shut deliberately. Paint the sash and frame separately with the window open.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Forcing the sash.** Cracked glass and split stiles come from impatience. If it will not move, score deeper, not harder.
- **Prying against glass.** Glass breaks suddenly and dangerously. Pry wood against wood only.
- **Scoring only the inside.** The exterior paint seal holds just as firmly. Do both sides.
- **Using oil or WD-40 in the tracks.** It attracts dust and turns into grinding paste. Use dry wax.
- **Ignoring lead paint.** In pre-1978 homes, disturbing old paint without precautions is a genuine health hazard — test first.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I just cut the sash free with a saw?**
No — you will destroy the window. The scoring method preserves the sash and frame for normal operation.

**The window moves but won't stay open — what now?**
The paint seal is broken (good), but the sash balance mechanism is broken. With the sash free, replacing the balances is the next repair.

**How do I prevent painters from painting windows shut again?**
Tell them explicitly, and check before the paint dries. A good painter masks the sash-frame gap or works the sash while painting.

**Is it worth restoring old wood windows vs. replacing?**
Old-growth wood windows, properly restored with weatherstripping, perform comparably to mid-range replacements and last far longer. Full replacement runs $500–$1,000+ per window installed — restoration is usually the better value unless the wood is rotted.

**How long does it take to unstick a window?**
About 30–60 minutes per window once you have the technique — most of it careful scoring. The first window takes longest; by the third you will be fast. Do not rush: the time is in the cutting, not the forcing.

**Should I remove the storm window first?**
Yes — if there is an exterior storm window, remove it for access to the outer sash seam. Label the storm panels so they go back in the right openings.

## After it is free: tune-up checklist

1. **Sand the sash edges lightly** where paint built up — just enough to remove the ridge, not bare wood.
2. **Wax every sliding surface** — sash sides, meeting rail, and channels. Paraffin or candle wax, rubbed on generously.
3. **Check the weatherstripping.** Old windows often have none — adhesive V-strip weatherstripping ($5 per window) dramatically cuts drafts once the window operates.
4. **Test full travel** — the sash should move top to bottom without binding. If it binds at one spot, mark it, remove the sash, and plane or sand that high spot.
5. **Repaint correctly:** paint the sash with the window open, let it dry, then close. Never bridge the gap with wet paint.

## When to call a pro

- The sash, sill, or frame is rotted — epoxy repairs or partial rebuilds are restoration work ($200–$500 per window).
- Lead paint is present and the job is extensive — a lead-safe certified contractor.
- The window is historic or the glass is original wavy glass — a restoration specialist preserves value that replacement destroys.
- The frame itself is racked or the house has settled — structural issues beyond a stuck sash.
