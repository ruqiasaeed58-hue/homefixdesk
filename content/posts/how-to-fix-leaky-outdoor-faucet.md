An outdoor faucet (hose bib) that drips constantly wastes hundreds of gallons over a summer — and in cold climates, a leaking hose bib can freeze, burst the pipe inside the wall, and flood the house. The fix is usually a $2 washer and 20 minutes of work.

## Quick diagnosis: where is it leaking from?

- **Drips from the spout when turned off** → worn seat washer inside. See Step 1.
- **Leaks around the handle stem when turned on** → worn packing nut. See Step 2.
- **Leaks from the wall connection or vacuum breaker** → connection issue. See Step 3.
- **Faucet is a frost-free type and leaks inside the wall** → the long stem's washer failed — needs stem replacement. See Step 4.

## What you will need

- Adjustable wrench (two — one to hold, one to turn)
- Screwdriver
- Replacement seat washers (assorted pack, $5)
- Packing string or replacement packing washer
- Plumber's grease or petroleum jelly
- Teflon tape

## Step 1: Replace the seat washer (spout drip)

1. **Turn off the water** to the faucet — there is usually a shutoff valve inside the house on that line. If there is not one, turn off the main.
2. **Remove the handle:** take out the handle screw and pull the handle off.
3. **Unscrew the packing nut** (the nut just behind the handle) with a wrench, holding the faucet body steady with the second wrench.
4. **Pull out the stem.** At the end of the stem you will see a small rubber washer held by a screw — the seat washer.
5. **Replace the washer:** remove the screw, swap in a new washer of the same size, and tighten the screw. A dab of plumber's grease helps.
6. **Reassemble** in reverse order. Turn the water back on and test.

If the drip persists with a new washer, the brass **seat** inside the faucet body is pitted or cracked — a seat wrench ($10) lets you replace or reface it, or it is time for a new faucet.

## Step 2: Fix a leaking handle (packing nut)

Water seeping around the handle stem when the faucet is on means the packing has worn.

1. With the water on (so you can test), **tighten the packing nut** a quarter-turn. Often that is all it takes.
2. If it still leaks, turn off the water, remove the handle and packing nut, and **replace the packing**: either wrap packing string around the stem 2–3 times or install a new packing washer.
3. Reassemble and test. The handle should turn smoothly with no seepage.

## Step 3: Fix vacuum breaker and connection leaks

- **Vacuum breaker** (the small cap on top of modern hose bibs, required by code to prevent backflow): if it leaks or sprays when the water runs, the internal seal is worn. Unscrew it and replace it ($5–$10) — or replace the whole breaker cap.
- **Wall connection:** if water comes from where the faucet meets the wall, the pipe connection inside may be loose or the faucet body cracked from freezing. Tightening rarely fixes a cracked body — replacement is needed.

## Step 4: Frost-free sillcock issues

Frost-free faucets have a long stem (8–14 inches) with the shutoff seat deep inside the warm wall. They fail differently:

1. **Leak from the spout:** the washer at the end of the long stem is worn. The stem unscrews as a unit — replace the washer at its tip.
2. **The #1 killer:** leaving a hose connected in winter. A connected hose traps water in the stem, which freezes and bursts the pipe inside the wall — even on a "frost-free" faucet. **Always disconnect hoses before the first freeze.**
3. If the pipe burst inside the wall, you will see water inside the house when the faucet is on — that is a plumber job involving opening the wall.

## Winterizing (cold climates)

1. **Disconnect all hoses** before freezing weather — non-negotiable.
2. **Shut off the interior valve** and open the outside faucet to drain the line.
3. Leave the outside faucet open at 45° over winter so any residual water can expand without bursting the pipe.
4. Consider an **insulated faucet cover** ($5) for extra protection.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Leaving the hose connected in winter.** The single most expensive mistake — it defeats the frost-free design and bursts pipes inside walls.
- **Using one wrench.** Always hold the faucet body with a second wrench — twisting the whole faucet can break the pipe connection inside the wall.
- **Overtightening the packing nut.** Snug stops the leak; cranking makes the handle impossible to turn and damages the packing.
- **Ignoring a slow drip.** "It is just outside" still wastes water and money — and in fall, it becomes a freeze risk.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does my outdoor faucet leak even when I just replaced the washer?**
The brass seat the washer presses against is likely pitted or cracked. Replace or reface the seat with a seat wrench, or replace the faucet.

**Should I replace my old hose bib with a frost-free model?**
In any climate with freezing winters, yes — if you are replacing the faucet anyway. The long-stem design moves the shutoff inside the warm wall. But remember: it only works if hoses are disconnected.

**Can I leave a hose connected if I have a frost-free faucet?**
No. This is the most common misunderstanding. A connected hose traps water in the stem where it can freeze and burst — frost-free or not.

**My faucet has low pressure — is it related to the leak?**
Possibly — a partially failed washer can restrict flow. But low pressure at one outdoor faucet is more often a partially closed interior shutoff valve or a kinked supply line.

## When to call a pro

- Water appears inside the house when the outdoor faucet runs — burst pipe inside the wall; open the wall and repair ($500–$2,000+).
- The faucet body is cracked or the pipe connection inside the wall is damaged.
- There is no interior shutoff valve and you need one installed — a plumber adds it in under an hour.
- The leak is at the main line or you need to solder copper pipe and are not equipped for it.
